Episode 7: Influence: The Seven Principles That Move People

2021-10-19 · Instructor: Robert Cialdini · The Knowledge Project

Robert Cialdini on the seven principles of influence on The Knowledge Project. Note: the transcript covers the freely released first part. The member-only second part is not included.

Why this episode matters

This episode, with about 1.04 million views at the time of research, is the track’s persuasion core. Robert Cialdini wrote “Influence”, the book that named the field. The central claim: persuasion runs on seven universal principles, and each one is a shortcut the brain uses to make good decisions fast. Influence means pointing to those principles where they genuinely exist. Manipulation means fabricating them.

That distinction opens the episode and governs everything after it. Every principle gets three treatments: how it works, the study that proves it, and how to defend against its misuse. One honest gap: the transcript covers the freely released first part of the conversation. The member-only second part, covering Warren Buffett’s influence and online compliance tactics, is not in the source material and is not covered here.

Ask for advice, not opinion

The cold open carries a complete lesson. When you want someone’s buy-in, do not ask for their opinion. Asking for an opinion makes them a critic. Asking for advice makes them a partner. The research finding: people asked for advice become more favorable to the idea and give better, more constructive input, because they are now on your side of it.

Principle 1: Reciprocation

Definition: the rule of reciprocation says we must give back to those who first gave to us. Every human culture trains it. It lets societies give first without fear, because the obligation to return is built in.

The McDonald’s study, run by Cialdini’s colleague Steve Martin in Colombia and Brazil: for one week, children got a balloon. Half the families got it while leaving, as a thank-you. Half got it on entry. The entry-balloon families bought 25 percent more food. First is the key. The standard business model asks the customer to go first: sign, then we serve. Reciprocation reverses it: we go first.

The cheapest gift is information. White papers, tips, the three things that improve their outcome, given freely and not as a sales device. If the gift is designed to sell, people read it as a device, not a gift, and the obligation never forms.

Concessions are the negotiation form. The blood donor study: asked to give blood every six weeks for two years, everyone refused. Asked then for one donation tomorrow, compliance rose from 33 percent to 55 percent, and observers confirmed they showed up. Two mechanisms combine: the concession triggers a return concession, and the contrast makes the small ask look smaller. The contrast alone does not work. The episode stresses that the subjects had to say no first. Without the refusal, the retreat has no power.

The Cuban missile crisis retelling is the historical case. The public story: Kennedy stood firm and Khrushchev blinked. The declassified record: a secret reciprocal concession. American missiles out of Turkey for Soviet missiles out of Cuba. What saved the world was not the hard line. It was the trade.

Defense: do not reject all gifts. Reframe the manipulative ones. When a gift arrives as the lever for a much larger ask, rename it mentally from gift to trick, show them the door, and keep the extinguisher. By the rule of reciprocation, the exploiter may be exploited.

Principle 2: Liking

We say yes to people we like. Two levers move liking honestly: similarity and genuine compliments.

The email negotiation study: negotiators bargaining by email deadlocked 30 percent of the time, both sides walking away with nothing. When instructed to exchange personal information first, hobbies, hometowns, majors, deadlocks fell to 6 percent. The key was not the amount of information. It was the count of genuine commonalities. Similarity found is rapport built. The internet hands you this tool: social profiles list the commonalities before you ever meet.

Joe Girard is the exemplar. The Guinness record holder sold an average of six cars and trucks a day from a Detroit dealership. His method: every buyer got a greeting card every month, and inside each card one line: “I like you. Joe Girard.” We like people who like us, and hearing it monthly compounded for years.

Defense: notice the liking spike. If you like a salesperson far more than a 30-minute interaction warrants, step back and inventory what they did: the drink, the compliments, the claimed similarities. Then separate the salesperson from the car. You drive the car home, not the person.

Principle 3: Social proof

When uncertain, we do not look inward. Inward shows only the lack of confidence. We look outward, at what people like us do. That is the whole principle.

The Beijing restaurant study: managers put an asterisk on certain menu items. The asterisk did not mean chef’s special. It meant “one of our most popular items.” Those items sold 13 to 20 percent more. Every demographic moved, but first-time visitors moved most. Uncertainty is the amplifier. The most uncertain are the most influenceable.

The Opower story is social proof at civilization scale. Home energy reports compared households to similar neighbors. High users cut usage dramatically. The mechanism had two parts: proof that people like me do it, and proof that it is feasible for me. The company is credited with 30 billion pounds of CO2 never released.

Defense has two layers. First, read reviews like a detective. Phony reviews lean on personal stories instead of product features, and on verbs instead of nouns, because the writer never touched the product. Second, trust the 4.2 to 4.7 band. In the cited study, that range converted best. Below it the product looked weak. Above it the ratings looked rigged. And when a company is caught faking, penalize it publicly. Post the cheating everywhere. Private boycotts do not change incentives. Public ones do.

Principle 4: Authority

The distinction that matters: being in authority versus being an authority. Being in authority is power: the boss card, threats, coercion. It works and it costs. People finesse around it. Being an authority is expertise: genuine knowledge that earns following.

The Bose ad is the masterclass. The original ad led with the word “new” and flopped. “New” signals uncertainty: no track record, no neighbor tried it. Changing the headline to the loss-framed line “hear what waiting costs you” (paraphrase of the episode headline, reworded for style) raised sales 45 percent, because the fear of loss triggers loss aversion. Then the marketers moved expert testimonials from the bottom of the ad to the top. The episode reports a further 15 point increase, for a 60 percent increase from the initial generation of the ad. Authority first infuses everything below it. Authority last is decoration.

Defense asks two questions. One: is this person truly an expert on this topic. Matthew McConaughey selling cars and the actor who played a doctor selling cough syrup both fail this test. Two: if they are an expert, is there a reason besides merit for the recommendation. Paid influencers pass question one and fail question two. Either failure unhooks the authority.

Principle 5: Scarcity

We want more of what we can have less of. Rare, scarce, and dwindling in availability all pull harder. FOMO is the folk name. Loss aversion is the engine: Kahneman’s finding that losing a value hurts about twice as much as gaining the same value pleases.

The e-commerce A/B tests, across six sites and 29 tested elements: the top converters were all influence principles. Scarcity of supply ranked first. Social proof ranked second. Scarcity of time, the limited offer, ranked third. Two of the top three were scarcity.

The trend variant of social proof deserves its own note. “40 percent market share” persuades. “30 percent last year, 35 six months ago, 40 now” persuades more. People project the trend forward. Momentum beats position.

Uniqueness is scarcity without countdown timers. If no rival can match a feature, or a combination of features, say so. What they cannot get elsewhere is what they fear losing.

Principle 6: Commitment and consistency

People want to act consistently with what they have said and done, especially in public. Get a small, voluntary, public step in your direction and the larger step follows.

Gordon Sinclair’s restaurant: 30 percent no-shows. His receptionist changed two words. “Please call if you must cancel” became “Will you please call if you must cancel,” followed by a pause. Every caller said yes. No-shows fell 67 percent and never rose again. The pause did the work. The yes was active, public, and voluntary.

The management application: never let anyone leave a meeting without the question “will you complete this by next time” and a pause. A no is useful information: the person needs time, resources, or help. A yes is a commitment device that raises follow-through.

Unmooring works in reverse. To move someone off an old commitment, never call the old choice stupid. Say it could be right given what was known then, then introduce the new information. “As a good decision maker, you use all the information” lets them change without losing face.

Self-defense: when new information arrives about your own past choice, ask whether you would decide the same way knowing it then. If not, update. Consistency is a virtue until it becomes a cage.

Principle 7: Unity

The newest principle. If the communicator can arrange to be seen as one of us, sharing an identity, yeses follow. Not like us. Of us. Tribal identity is the strong form, but any genuine shared membership works.

The campus study: a student asking for United Way donations added one line: “I am a student here too.” Donations rose 400 percent. Four words of shared identity quintupled giving.

Cialdini’s own Tim story is the applied version. He needed data from a colleague famous for refusing. Leading with the deadline already failed. He led instead with twelve years in the same psychology department. He reminded Tim that they shared twelve years in the same psychology department, then made the ask. He had the data that afternoon. Unity first, request second.

Q&A

  1. What separates influence from manipulation? Influence points to principles where they genuinely exist: real expertise, real popularity, real scarcity. Manipulation fabricates them: fake statistics, fake authorities, fake urgency. One informs. The other counterfeits.

  2. Why does “go first” beat “promise later” in reciprocation? The rule obligates return only for what was actually given. Promises of future service ask the customer to go first, which triggers caution. A real gift given first triggers the obligation, as the 25 percent McDonald’s lift shows.

  3. Why did the blood donor retreat work only after a refusal? Two mechanisms need the no. The concession triggers a return concession, and the contrast shrinks the second ask. Without the refusal there is no concession to return, only a small ask that looks like the opening bid.

  4. What did the email negotiators teach about similarity? Deadlocks fell from 30 percent to 6 percent when negotiators shared personal information first. The active ingredient was the count of genuine commonalities, not the volume of disclosure. Find real overlap and surface it early.

  5. Why do first-time visitors respond most to social proof? Uncertainty is the amplifier. First-timers have no internal reference, so they borrow the crowd’s. The Beijing asterisk moved every group but moved the uncertain most.

  6. Why did the loss-framed headline beat “new”? “New” signals uncertainty and invites waiting. “Missing” signals loss, and loss aversion makes the prospect about twice as powerful as the equivalent gain. The episode headline (hear what waiting costs you, paraphrase) changed the frame and lifted sales 45 percent.

  7. How does the Gordon Sinclair pause work? “Will you please call” plus a pause forces an active yes. Active, public, voluntary commitments bind. The passive “please call” binds nothing. Two words and a silence cut no-shows 67 percent.

  8. Why is “I am a student here too” stronger than “I am like you”? Like means similarity, and similarity attracts. Of means membership, and membership obligates. Shared identity carries obligation in a way that shared taste does not. Four hundred percent is the measured difference.

Memory aids

Mnemonic for the seven: ReLiSoAuScCoUn. Reciprocation, Liking, Social proof, Authority, Scarcity, Commitment, Unity. “Real Leaders Seldom Argue, Smart Colleagues Unite.”

Never-confuse pair: influence versus manipulation. Both use the same seven levers. Influence finds them. Manipulation forges them. The test is always verifiability.

Never-confuse pair: being in authority versus being an authority. The first is a title with threats behind it. The second is knowledge with evidence behind it. Titles cost goodwill. Knowledge compounds it.

Trap card: the contrast effect needs the refusal. Presenting the big and small options together does not trigger reciprocation. Get the no, then retreat.

Trap card: unity claims must be genuine and relevant. “We are both human” is true and useless. Twelve years in the same department is specific and binding.

How to imbibe this

This week, run four practices.

  1. Go first. Give one piece of genuinely useful information to a prospect or colleague with no ask attached. Note what comes back within two weeks.
  2. Advice not opinion. In your next meeting, replace “what is your opinion of this plan” with “what advice do you have on this plan.” Compare the tone of responses.
  3. The two-question authority check. For the next three expert endorsements you encounter, ask: true expert, unbiased motive. Log the failures.
  4. The pause. End your next delegation with “will you complete this by Thursday” and a full pause. Record the yes and the follow-through.

Self-catching: when you feel suddenly warm toward a seller, run the liking inventory. Drink, compliment, claimed similarity: name them before you decide.

Think it yourself

Do these without AI. Use a notebook.

  1. Write the accusation-free version of your last pitch, then add one honest reciprocation gift at the front. What changes.
  2. List three genuine commonalities with your next negotiation counterpart, found from public profiles. Draft the two-sentence opener that surfaces them.
  3. Audit your last five online purchases against the review detective rules: story-heavy, verb-heavy, rating band. Which ones would you now distrust.
  4. Take one old commitment you are still honoring. Run the new-information test in writing. Would you choose it again.
  5. Draft a unity opener for your hardest current ask: the real shared membership, stated in one line.

Skills you can now use

  • Open with a genuine gift of information and let reciprocation work.
  • Ask for advice to manufacture partners and avoid manufacturing critics.
  • Surface real commonalities before negotiating, by email or in person.
  • Place true authority testimonials first, never last.
  • Write loss-framed headlines and mean them.
  • Manufacture trend-based social proof from real momentum.
  • Lock commitments with the “will you” plus pause construction.
  • Unmoor others from stale commitments with the new-information frame.
  • Claim genuine shared identity before making the ask.
  • Defend against all seven misuses with the specific counter for each.
