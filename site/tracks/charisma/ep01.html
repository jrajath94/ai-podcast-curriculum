Episode 1: Cues: How Body Language Decides Before You Speak

2024-12-09 · Instructor: Vanessa Van Edwards · Diary of a CEO

Vanessa Van Edwards on cues, warmth, competence, and conversation on Diary of a CEO.

Why this episode matters

This is the most-watched episode in the track, with about 14.17 million views at the time of research. It is the foundation for everything that follows. The central claim: people decide whether they like, trust, and want to listen to you before you finish your first sentence. That decision runs on cues. A cue is any signal you send with your body, your voice, your words, or your appearance. You send thousands of them every day, mostly by accident.

Vanessa Van Edwards runs a human behavior research lab. In the episode she argues that charisma is not a gift. It is a recipe. Her lab catalogs 97 cues. You do not need all 97. You pick the ones that fit your personality and practice them until they run on their own. The episode covers the four cue channels, the warmth-competence thermostat, the five power cues, the five warmth cues, how to greet people, how to talk so people listen, and how to read the cues of others. Later episodes add negotiation, influence, storytelling, and the neuroscience underneath. This episode gives you the operating system.

Cues run on four channels

Everything you broadcast travels on four channels at once.

  1. Body cues: posture, gestures, facial expression, eye contact, touch.
  2. Vocal cues: pitch, pace, volume, inflection, pauses.
  3. Verbal cues: the words you choose and the order you put them in.
  4. Ornament cues: clothes, jewelry, hair, background, everything people can see around you.

Definition: signal amplification bias is the false belief that other people read your cues louder than they do. You think your nerves scream. They whisper. You think your confidence shines. It glows dimly. Because you underestimate how quietly you signal, you must turn the volume up on purpose.

First impressions land on your face

Van Edwards says most people walk around with a resting bothered face. That means a default expression that reads as sad, angry, or afraid. The camera version of this problem shows up in profile pictures. She names three mistakes: fear eyes (wide eyes that signal alarm), contempt (a one-sided smirk that reads as superiority), and a fake smile (mouth smiles, eyes stay dead).

Dr. Barbara Wild ran a study on smiles, cited in the episode. Genuine smiles engage the muscles around the eyes. The fix she offers is the pen trick: hold a pen between your teeth for a few seconds. The teeth grip forces the smiling muscles into the genuine position. Your face then sends the warmth cue even when you start from neutral.

The Jamie Siminoff story makes the business case. Siminoff pitched Ring on Shark Tank and walked away without a deal. He later sold the company to Amazon for a large sum. Van Edwards uses the story to show that one room of investors reading your cues wrong can cost you the outcome, while the cues themselves stay trainable.

The thermostat: warmth plus competence

Susan Fiske published the finding in 2002, cited in the episode: warmth and competence explain about 82 percent of the impression you make. Warmth answers the question “are you a friend or a foe”. Competence answers “can you act on your intentions”. Every cue you send tilts one of the two dials.

Van Edwards frames the pair as a thermostat. You do not set both dials to maximum. Too much warmth with no competence reads as a pushover. Too much competence with no warmth reads as cold. The sweet spot sits just past the middle on both. She calls the off-balance regions the danger zone: high warmth and low competence makes people like you but not follow you, while high competence and low warmth makes people respect you but not trust you.

The two dials explain about 82 percent of impressions, so the remaining 18 percent is everything else combined.

The five power cues

Power cues turn up the competence dial.

  1. The steeple: fingertips touch, palms apart, forming a church-steeple shape. It signals confidence because only a calm person holds the hands still in that shape.
  2. Earlobe to shoulder distance: drop the shoulders so the gap between earlobe and shoulder grows. Short gaps read as tense. Long gaps read as relaxed and in command.
  3. End-of-sentence eye contact: hold eye contact for about 60 to 70 percent of a conversation, and land the eye contact at the end of your sentences. The end is where the claim lands, so the eyes should be there to back it.
  4. Lower lid flex: a slight tightening of the lower eyelid. It reads as focus and authority.
  5. Downward inflection: let the pitch fall at the end of statements. Rising pitch at the end, called uptalk, turns statements into questions. The episode cites a surgeon voice study: patients judged surgeons as more likely to face malpractice claims from the tone of voice alone. Van Edwards runs a two-truths-and-a-lie exercise in which listeners catch the lie partly because the liar’s pitch rises.

The five warmth cues

Warmth cues turn up the friend dial.

  1. The triple nod: nod three times while someone speaks. In the cited research, people talked 67 percent longer to a triple nodder. It signals “keep going, I am with you”.
  2. The head tilt: a slight tilt exposes the neck, a vulnerable area, and reads as openness.
  3. The authentic smile: eyes engaged, the Barbara Wild finding. The mouth alone is not enough.
  4. The lean: lean in slightly when someone speaks. Distance reads as disinterest.
  5. The nonverbal bridge: point a foot, a knee, or a torso toward the person you want to welcome. The body forms a bridge they can walk across.

One warning: do not bow. A bow reads as submission in most Western rooms and drops the competence dial.

Greetings set the frame

Two stances, two meanings. Blading, turning the body sideways, is a protective stance and reads as guarded. Arms out, chest open, reads as welcome. For the handshake, the window is one to three seconds. Shorter reads as dismissive. Longer reads as dominance play.

Two handshake variants to know. The cupping handshake, where one hand cups over the other person’s hand, is the politician’s handshake. It reads as warmth plus control. The Trump flip, where the shaker yanks the other person’s hand toward themselves, is a danger zone move. It claims dominance and the room feels it.

Nod direction matters. Nodding up, chin lifted, reads as casual and friendly among peers. Nodding down reads as formal acknowledgment, the kind you give a superior. Choose on purpose.

Stage blocking, from her work with speakers: put science on the left side of the stage and stories on the right. The audience then files each part in the right drawer without being told.

Gestures make people believe you

The episode cites the headline number: people who gesture are 12.5 times more likely to be believed [uncertain: the episode states this as a research finding, and the original study was not named on air]. The TED study behind it is named: the most popular TED talks averaged 465 hand gestures per 18 minutes, while the least popular averaged 271. The difference is 194 gestures, or about 72 percent more movement in the talks people loved.

Vocal variety works the same way for the voice. Van Edwards teaches three voices. The TED voice is the big-stage voice, projected and paced. The numbers voice is the precise voice for data, slower and flatter so figures land. The story voice is the warm, varied voice for narratives. Monotone speakers use one voice for all three jobs.

The penguin problem names what goes wrong. A penguin stands contracted, arms pinned, body small. Many people present as penguins: small posture, small voice, vocal fry at the ends of sentences. The fix she gives is simple: speak louder. Volume forces the body open and the fry fades.

Words are cues too

Her lab counts 97 cues. The point is not to learn all of them. It is to choose your recipe. An ambivert, someone between introvert and extrovert, picks a different recipe from a high-energy extrovert. The recipe must fit the cook.

Three conversation upgrades run through the episode.

First, replace “what do you do” with “what are you working on that excites you”. She prescribes a 30-day diet: ask the excitement question for 30 days and watch conversations change. “What do you do” gets a job title. “Working on anything that excites you” gets a story.

Second, ask the goal question. “What is your goal right now” invites people to share what they care about. Third, ask the character question. The Katniss example: asking someone which fictional character they relate to reveals values faster than any resume question.

Three magic phrases build connection

  1. “I was just thinking of you.” It tells the person they occupy space in your mind.
  2. “You are always so…” followed by a genuine trait. It tells them their pattern is seen.
  3. “The last time you mentioned…” It proves you listened and remembered.

The three phrases share one mechanism. They move the conversation down the three intimacy levels she borrows from personality research: general traits, personal concerns, and self narrative. Small talk lives at level one. Friendship needs level two and three.

Hero, healer, and victim are the three narratives people tell about themselves. The hero overcame. The healer helps. The victim was harmed. You do not argue with someone’s narrative. You hear which one they tell and respond inside it.

Popular people and lucky people

The Van Sloan study, cited in the episode: the popular kids were not the funniest or the best looking. They kept the longest liked-list. They could name the most people they liked. Liking people at scale made people like them back.

The Richard Wiseman luck study, cited in the episode: lucky and unlucky people read the same newspaper. The paper hid 42 images and a message saying “tell the experimenter you saw this and win money”. Lucky people spotted the opportunities. Unlucky people missed them while hunting only for what they were told to find. Luck, in this telling, is attention [uncertain: retold on air as Wiseman’s study, the original study not named].

Your network shapes your cues

The 58,000-hour study, cited in the episode: sit within 25 feet of a high performer and your own performance rises by about 15 percent. Sit within 25 feet of a low performer and it falls by about 30 percent. Proximity is a cue delivery system.

The sweat study: researchers collected sweat from frightened people and had others smell it in an fMRI scanner. The fear centers of the smellers lit up. Emotion is contagious through channels you never notice.

The cue cycle names the loop. Actors were shown faces flashing rejection cues too fast to notice consciously. Their pupils dilated. Your body reads cues your mind misses, and then your body answers. Matthew Lieberman’s finding completes the defense: label the emotion to tame it. Naming what you feel, silently and precisely, lowers the brain’s alarm response.

The Pygmalion loop: expectations shape behavior, which shapes outcomes, which confirm the expectations. Expect the best from people and your cues change, and their results follow.

She also describes personal-brand neural networks. Everyone you meet files you in a network of associations. Allergies are the traits that repel people from your network. Attractors are the traits that draw them in. Audit both lists on purpose.

Reading other people

Lie detection first. Humans detect lies at about 54 percent accuracy, barely above a coin flip [uncertain: the episode states the figure without naming the original study]. The episode lists cues that raise the odds: question inflection on statements, a sudden drop in volume, nods that do not match the words (with the warning that nodding norms differ, naming India, Bulgaria, and Pakistan as examples), and disgust or sniffing when someone describes something they claim to like.

Monica Moore’s availability research, cited in the episode: people signal romantic availability with about eight glances, feet pointed toward the target like a croissant, and an open torso. The cues are countable, which is the point. Flirting is a cue sequence, not a mystery.

Friendship dating is her term for auditioning friends the way people audition partners. The Casa de Luz test: she visited a community space repeatedly to see whether the same people showed up, whether conversations went past small talk, and whether she felt welcomed. Friendships, like romances, need repeated exposure plus warmth cues.

The AirPods warning: constant earbuds kill weak ties, the casual acquaintances who deliver most new opportunities. And the ambivalent tie finding: a University study of police officers found that ambivalent relationships, hot and cold, damaged health more than purely toxic ones. Predictable enemies are easier on the body than unpredictable friends.

Two stories about rooms

The Truman story: at the 1944 Democratic convention, Truman’s team air-conditioned a small room and invited power brokers inside on a hot day. Comfort did the persuading. Control the room and you control the cues.

The other-shoe effect, from Richard Wiseman’s work as retold in the episode: a researcher spilled a blender’s contents, or in the interview version knocked over coffee, and the audience rated the clumsy person as warmer and more competent. A small, human mistake drops the guard of the room. Perfection raises it [uncertain: retold on air as Wiseman’s work, the original study not named].

Q&A

  1. Why does Van Edwards say charisma is a recipe rather than a gift? Charisma decomposes into countable cues on four channels. A recipe means you select the cues that fit your personality and drill them until they run without thought. A gift cannot be trained. A recipe can.

  2. What is signal amplification bias, and what follows from it? You believe others read your cues louder than they do. Your nerves whisper and your confidence glows dimly. The fix is to turn the volume up on purpose: bigger gestures, clearer inflection, deliberate warmth.

  3. Why is the warmth-competence balance a thermostat and not a maximization problem? Fiske 2002 attributes about 82 percent of impressions to the two traits. Maximum warmth with low competence reads as pushover. Maximum competence with low warmth reads as cold. The target is just past the middle on both dials, tuned to the room.

  4. How does the triple nod work, and what number backs it? Three nods while someone speaks signal continued attention. In the cited research, people spoke 67 percent longer to triple nodders. It is a warmth cue that costs nothing.

  5. What is the danger of uptalk, and which study makes the point? Rising pitch at the end of statements turns claims into questions and drains the competence dial. The surgeon voice study found patients predicted malpractice claims from tone of voice alone. Downward inflection is the repair.

  6. Why do lucky people spot more opportunities in the Wiseman study? Both groups saw the same newspaper with 42 hidden images. Lucky people kept their attention open and noticed the anomalies. Unlucky people hunted only the assigned target. Luck behaves like attention, not fate.

  7. Why are ambivalent ties worse than toxic ones? In the cited police study, hot-and-cold relationships damaged health more than consistently negative ones. The body budgets for a known enemy. It cannot budget for one that flips.

  8. What is the other-shoe effect, and when does it help? A small, human clumsy moment, like spilling coffee, lowers the room’s guard and raises warmth and even competence ratings. It helps when perfection makes you unapproachable. It is not a license for real incompetence.

Memory aids

Mnemonic for the four channels: BoVoVeO. Body, Vocal, Verbal, Ornaments. Your BVVO broadcasts before your brain finishes its first sentence.

Mnemonic for the five power cues: SELLL. Steeple, Earlobe gap, Look at sentence end, Lower lid, Low inflection at the end.

Mnemonic for the five warmth cues: THANB. Triple nod, Head tilt, Authentic smile, lean iN, Bridge. THANK the room with warmth.

Never-confuse pair: warmth versus niceness. Warmth is the friend-or-foe signal plus the competence to act. Niceness without competence lands in the danger zone: liked, not followed.

Never-confuse pair: signal amplification bias versus the spotlight effect. The spotlight effect says you overestimate how much people notice you. Signal amplification bias says you overestimate how loudly your cues land. Both push the same fix: broadcast bigger.

Trap card: the 12.5 times gesture figure is an episode claim with the original study unnamed on air. Quote the number only with that flag. The 465 versus 271 TED count is the named study behind the same point.

Trap card: nodding norms differ by culture. The episode names India, Bulgaria, and Pakistan as places where a nod can mean the opposite. Never read a single nod as a universal yes.

How to imbibe this

This week, run four practices.

  1. The resting face audit. Photograph your resting face and your current profile picture. Check for the three mistakes: fear eyes, contempt smirk, dead-eye smile. Replace the picture if any one fails. Do the pen trick for ten seconds before any call.
  2. The excitement question diet. For 30 days, replace “what do you do” with “what are you working on that excites you”. Keep a tally of which answers turn into real conversations.
  3. One power cue and one warmth cue per day. Monday: steeple plus triple nod. Tuesday: earlobe gap plus head tilt. Continue through the list. Note which pair changes the room.
  4. The weak-tie walk. Leave the AirPods out on one commute or one coffee run. Count the small interactions. Log any that could become a repeated contact.

Self-catching: once a day, ask “which dial did I just turn”. If you were all warmth in a meeting that needed a decision, you know the repair. If you were all competence with someone who needed comfort, you know that repair too.

Think it yourself

Do these without AI. Use a notebook.

  1. Watch a 5-minute talk with the sound off. List every cue you see on the four channels. Then watch with sound. Write what changed in your judgment and which channel carried the change.
  2. Score your last three profile pictures against the three mistakes. Write the verdict for each in one sentence.
  3. Record yourself answering “tell me about yourself” for 60 seconds. Count your gestures. Count your uptalks. Rewrite the answer with three deliberate gestures and downward inflection, then record again and compare.
  4. List your five most frequent contacts. Mark each as ambivalent, positive, or negative. For each ambivalent one, write one boundary that would make the pattern predictable.
  5. Run the Wiseman test on yourself. Take any newspaper or long article. Hunt for planted anomalies while a friend times you. Write down what your attention skipped and why.

Skills you can now use

  • Set your warmth-competence thermostat for a room before you enter it.
  • Open a conversation with arms-out posture, a one to three second handshake, and the exciting question.
  • Hold the five power cues through a pitch and the five warmth cues through a first meeting.
  • Spot resting bothered face, fear eyes, contempt, and fake smiles in photos, including your own.
  • Read availability and interest cues: glances, foot direction, torso openness.
  • Detect the weak signals of deception: mismatched nods, question inflection, volume drops, disgust tells.
  • Choose friends on purpose with the Casa de Luz test: repeated exposure, past small talk, felt welcome.
  • Audit your 25-foot circle and move the low-performer seats farther from your desk.
