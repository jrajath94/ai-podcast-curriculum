~00:00

Ask for Advice, Not Opinion

when you ask for an opinion you get a critic

Robert Cialdini on The Knowledge Project, ~00:00

Source fact The cold open carries a complete lesson. When you want someone's buy-in, do not ask for their opinion. Asking for an opinion makes them a critic. Asking for advice makes them a partner. The research finding: people asked for advice become more favorable to the idea and give better, more constructive input, because they are now on your side of it.

Source fact The distinction that governs everything after: influence means pointing to the principles where they genuinely exist. Manipulation means fabricating them. One informs. The other counterfeits.

Nugget 1 · Buy-in

Advice makes partners. Opinion makes critics.

What happened. Asking for advice puts the other person on your side of the idea. They become more favorable and give better input.

Why it matters. Every plan needs buy-in. The question you ask decides whether you get a partner or a reviewer.

The principle. Replace what is your opinion with what advice do you have in your next meeting.

~00:06

Principle 1: Reciprocation

Source fact Definition: the rule of reciprocation says we must give back to those who first gave to us. Every human culture trains it. It lets societies give first without fear, because the obligation to return is built in.

Source fact The McDonald's study, run by Cialdini's colleague Steve Martin in Colombia and Brazil: children got a balloon. Half the families got it while leaving, as a thank-you. Half got it on entry. The entry-balloon families bought 25 percent more food. First is the key. The standard business model asks the customer to go first: sign, then we serve. Reciprocation reverses it: we go first.

Source fact The cheapest gift is information. White papers, tips, the three things that improve their outcome, given freely and not as a sales device. If the gift is designed to sell, people read it as a device, not a gift, and the obligation never forms.

Source fact Concessions are the negotiation form. The blood donor study: asked to give blood every six weeks for two years, everyone refused. Asked then for one donation tomorrow, compliance rose from 33 percent to 55 percent, and observers confirmed they showed up. Two mechanisms combine: the concession triggers a return concession, and the contrast makes the small ask look smaller. The contrast alone does not work. The episode stresses that the subjects had to say no first. Without the refusal, the retreat has no power.

Source fact The Cuban missile crisis retelling is the historical case. The public story: Kennedy stood firm and Khrushchev blinked. The declassified record: a secret reciprocal concession. American missiles out of Turkey for Soviet missiles out of Cuba. What saved the world was not the hard line. It was the trade.

Source fact Defense: do not reject all gifts. Reframe the manipulative ones. When a gift arrives as the lever for a much larger ask, rename it mentally from gift to trick, show them the door, and keep the extinguisher. By the rule of reciprocation, the exploiter may be exploited.

Nugget 2 · Reciprocation

Go first, and mean it

What happened. The balloon on entry lifted food purchases 25 percent. The refused big ask made the small ask jump from 33 to 55 percent.

Why it matters. The obligation to return only forms for real gifts. Devices designed to sell never trigger it.

The principle. Give first, honestly. When a gift is a lever, rename it a trick and show them out.

~00:35

Principle 2: Liking

I like you.

Joe Girard, as quoted by Robert Cialdini, ~00:40

Source fact We say yes to people we like. Two levers move liking honestly: similarity and genuine compliments.

Source fact The email negotiation study: negotiators bargaining by email deadlocked 30 percent of the time, both sides walking away with nothing. When instructed to exchange personal information first, hobbies, hometowns, majors, deadlocks fell to 6 percent. The key was not the amount of information. It was the count of genuine commonalities. Similarity found is rapport built. The internet hands you this tool: social profiles list the commonalities before you ever meet.

Source fact Joe Girard is the exemplar. The Guinness record holder sold an average of six cars and trucks a day from a Detroit dealership. His method: every buyer got a greeting card every month, and inside each card one line: I like you, from Joe Girard. We like people who like us, and hearing it monthly compounded for years.

Source fact Defense: notice the liking spike. If you like a salesperson far more than a 30-minute interaction warrants, step back and inventory what they did: the drink, the compliments, the claimed similarities. Then separate the salesperson from the car. You drive the car home, not the person.

Nugget 3 · Liking

Similarity found is rapport built

What happened. Deadlocks fell from 30 to 6 percent when negotiators surfaced genuine commonalities first.

Why it matters. Liking is not luck. It is similarity plus genuine praise, delivered before the ask.

The principle. Find real overlap and surface it early. When warmth spikes unnaturally, inventory what they did.

Principle 3: Social Proof

Source fact When uncertain, we do not look inward. Inward shows only the lack of confidence. We look outward, at what people like us do. That is the whole principle.

Source fact The Beijing restaurant study: managers put an asterisk on certain menu items. The asterisk did not mean chef's special. It meant one of our most popular items. Those items sold 13 to 20 percent more. Every demographic moved, but first-time visitors moved most. Uncertainty is the amplifier. The most uncertain are the most influenceable.

Source fact The trend variant of social proof deserves its own note. 40 percent market share persuades. 30 percent last year, 35 six months ago, 40 now persuades more. People project the trend forward. Momentum beats position.

Source fact Defense: read reviews like a detective. Phony reviews lean on personal stories instead of product features, and on verbs instead of nouns, because the writer never touched the product. And when a company is caught faking, penalize it publicly. Post the cheating everywhere. Private boycotts do not change incentives. Public ones do.

Principle 4: Authority

Source fact The distinction that matters: being in authority versus being an authority. Being in authority is power: the boss card, threats, coercion. It works and it costs. People finesse around it. Being an authority is expertise: genuine knowledge that earns following.

Source fact The Bose ad is the masterclass. Moving expert testimonials from the bottom of the ad to the top increased purchases by 15 percent. Authority first infuses everything below it. Authority last is decoration.

Source fact Defense asks two questions. One: is this person truly an expert on this topic. Matthew McConaughey selling cars and the actor who played a doctor selling cough syrup both fail this test. Two: if they are an expert, is there a reason besides merit for the recommendation. Paid influencers pass question one and fail question two. Either failure unhooks the authority.

Nugget 5 · Authority

Be an authority, not in authority

What happened. Expert testimonials moved to the top lifted purchases 15 percent. Titles with threats cost goodwill.

Why it matters. Knowledge compounds. Coercion bills you later.

The principle. Lead with real expertise. Ask the two questions of every endorsement you meet.

~01:14

Principle 5: Scarcity

Source fact We want more of what we can have less of. Rare, scarce, and dwindling in availability all pull harder. FOMO is the folk name. Loss aversion is the engine: losing a value hurts about twice as much as gaining the same value pleases.

Source fact Uniqueness is scarcity without countdown timers. If no rival can match a feature, or a combination of features, say so. What they cannot get elsewhere is what they fear losing.

Source fact The e-commerce A/B tests, across six sites and 29 tested elements: the top six converters were all principles of social influence, and scarcity of supply ranked first.

Source fact Defense: verify. Real scarcity survives a check. False urgency does not.

Nugget 6 · Scarcity

Losses loom larger than gains

What happened. Loss aversion runs about two to one. Scarcity of supply, time, and uniqueness all trade on it.

Why it matters. Honest scarcity informs. Fabricated scarcity counterfeits.

The principle. State what is genuinely rare. Verify every countdown.

~01:28

Principle 6: Commitment and Consistency

Source fact People want to act consistently with what they have said and done, especially in public. Get a small, voluntary, public step in your direction and the larger step follows.

Source fact Gordon Sinclair's restaurant: 30 percent no-shows. His receptionist changed two words: her old please call if you have to change or cancel your reservation became will you please call if you have to change or cancel your reservation, followed by a pause. Every caller said yes. No-shows fell 67 percent and never rose again. The pause did the work. The yes was active, public, and voluntary.

Interpretation The management application: never let anyone leave a meeting without asking whether they will complete it by next time, then pause. A no is useful information: the person needs time, resources, or help. A yes is a commitment device that raises follow-through.

Source fact Unmooring works in reverse. To move someone off an old commitment, never call the old choice stupid. Say it could be right given what was known then, then introduce the new information. Framing them as a good decision maker who uses all the information lets them change without losing face.

Source fact Self-defense: when new information arrives about your own past choice, ask whether you would decide the same way knowing it then. If not, update. Consistency is a virtue until it becomes a cage.

Nugget 7 · Commitment

Small public yeses grow

What happened. Two words and a pause cut no-shows 67 percent. The yes was active, public, and voluntary.

Why it matters. Consistency binds people to their own words. The pause is what makes the yes theirs.

The principle. Ask will you and then pause. Let the silence do the work.

~01:36

Principle 7: Unity

Source fact The newest principle. If the communicator can arrange to be seen as one of us, sharing an identity, yeses follow. Not like us. Of us. Tribal identity is the strong form, but any genuine shared membership works.

Source fact The campus study: a student asking for United Way donations added one line: I am a student here too. Donations rose 400 percent. Four words of shared identity quintupled giving.

Source fact Cialdini's own Tim story is the applied version. He needed data from a colleague famous for refusing. Leading with the deadline already failed. He led instead with twelve years in the same psychology department. He reminded Tim that they shared twelve years in the same psychology department, then made the ask. He had the data that afternoon. Unity first, request second.

Nugget 8 · Unity

Of us beats like us

What happened. Four words of shared identity quintupled donations. Twelve years of shared membership unlocked the data.

Why it matters. Similarity attracts. Membership obligates. The difference is 400 percent.

The principle. Claim genuine shared identity before the ask. Unity first, request second.

Nugget 9 · Defense

The test is always verifiability

What happened. Every principle gets a specific counter, from the liking inventory to the two-question authority check.

Why it matters. Influence finds the levers. Manipulation forges them. Only verification tells them apart.

The principle. When a principle pushes you, check whether it genuinely exists before you obey it.
