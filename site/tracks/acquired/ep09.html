Nvidia 1993 to 2006: founding through the GPU wars

Why this episode matters

Every moat in this track started here. NVIDIA began in 1993 making graphics chips in a market with 90 undifferentiated competitors. By the mid-2000s it held an 83 percent share of standalone GPUs, had invented the term “GPU,” and had bet the entire company twice, nearly going bankrupt both times. Jensen’s own summary: “My will to survive exceeds almost everybody else’s will to kill me.” This episode is the playbook for how a startup wins a commodity market: ship twice as fast as physics says you should, control the software, and take the bets that look like suicide. The CUDA story in ep08 only makes sense once you see the company that was capable of making it.

The story

Jensen’s beginning

The story starts not in Silicon Valley but in the southern part of Taiwan, with the birth of Jen-Hsun Huang in February 1963, later Americanized to Jensen. He graduates in 1984 and moves to Silicon Valley, joining AMD as the equivalent of a chip-design product manager, engineering-heavy, junior management on a blazing-fast (for the time) 1 MHz CPU chip. After a couple of years he follows his office to a startup called LSI Logic, which had just gone public in what was then the largest venture return in IPO history: Sequoia’s Don Valentine made $153 million on day one.

LSI was one of the first ASIC companies (application-specific integrated circuits), custom-designed chips built for other companies. Jensen spent eight years there while finishing his master’s degree at Stanford (completed around Thanksgiving 1992). Two colleagues from Sun Microsystems, Chris Malachowski and Curtis Priem, “really, really fantastic engineers,” in Jensen’s words, were unhappy and had an idea.

The idea: 3D graphics for everyone

Late 1992. 3D graphics are becoming a thing. The way it works: you need SGI workstations, expensive machines from Silicon Graphics down the street. Jurassic Park is about to come out (1993). Meanwhile, kids on PCs are playing Wolfenstein 3D, and Doom comes out in 1993 from id Software in Texas, where John Carmack is doing incredible engineering feats to get 3D running on consumer PCs. The market loves it.

Chris and Curtis’s idea: make 3D graphics cheap and ubiquitous, not locked in workstations. They pitch Jensen over dinner at Denny’s, his favorite spot. He worked at one in high school and his go-to dish is the Superbird. He joins them. They found NVIDIA in 1993.

The $2M seed

Sequoia and Sutter Hill invest, about $1 million each, $2 million total, at a $6 million post-money valuation. Remember: this becomes the eighth most valuable company in the world. Between the seed and the IPO, they raise only about $20 million total. There were no stages of venture capital then, you raised venture money and then hopefully were profitable enough to go public.

Death #1: the quadrilateral bet

NVIDIA’s first chip bets on quadrilaterals as the fundamental 3D primitive. The industry standardizes on triangles instead (a triangle is the fewest vertices in a closed shape, the cheapest way to describe a surface). NVIDIA is on board with Microsoft’s ecosystem, which standardizes on the triangle paradigm. Sega, their one big customer, gets cold feet about quadrilaterals too, and in 1996 says they are switching horses.

Meanwhile, memory prices drop thanks to Moore’s law. NVIDIA’s chips were designed to be super tight on memory, memory cost about $200 in components per chip, while competitors with more generous memory designs paid about $50. Everything is going wrong at once: wrong primitive, wrong memory economics, one customer walking away.

The Riva 128: nine months or death

What happens next is the first bet-the-company moment (covered in detail in ep01). With Sega gone and cash running out, NVIDIA designs the Riva 128 in nine months, a schedule so compressed they debug in software on an emulator running about one frame per hour [uncertain: this episode says one frame every 30 seconds; ep01’s episode says about an hour per frame, which anchors the worked arithmetic there]. The tradeoff: mind-numbing testing slowness in exchange for shipping at all.

It works. The Riva 128 is a beast, and crucially it is designed with the same assumptions about available technology as competitors, but competitors need 18-24 months to ship their next generation. NVIDIA ships in six. They sell 1 million units of the Riva 128 within four months, in 1997.

The six-month cadence: doubling performance per dollar

Starting in 1997-1998, NVIDIA enters a cycle of doubling the performance delivered at a given price point every six months. Compare: Moore’s law says transistors double every 18-24 months. Competitors ship new architectures on that timeline. NVIDIA laps them. The episode’s framing: they are competing on a different vector than the CPU manufacturers, and the thing they are learning to do (execute more instructions simultaneously, parallelize) will turn out to have uses far beyond graphics. (Pin that thought. It detonates in ep08.)

Inventing the GPU

In 1999 NVIDIA rebrands its products. After the NV1 and Riva 128, they run a naming contest. The winner is “Geometry Force”, “force” as in “may the force be with you”, shortened to GeForce, still the gaming brand today. The first GeForce, the GeForce 256, delivers 5x better graphics performance than anything else on the market.

And they coin the category: before this, these were “graphics cards” or “graphics chips.” NVIDIA markets the GeForce 256 as the first GPU, the Graphics Processing Unit. Sony had used the term around the PlayStation, but nobody had marketed the idea. Naming the category is a power move: whoever names it, owns it.

The IPO and the Xbox deal

NVIDIA goes public at the beginning of 1999 at a $600 million market cap, a 100x return from the $6 million seed valuation. Then Microsoft comes calling with a secret project: the Xbox. NVIDIA signs a $500-million-a-year deal to supply the graphics, with a $200 million advance. The chip is a modified version of the new GeForce 3, the first with programmable shaders and programmable lighting on the GPU. Instead of fixed-function hardware spitting out triangles, developers can run little programs (shaders) on the chip.

This is the master-strategist move: the Xbox deal buys Microsoft’s support for programmable shaders, which protects NVIDIA from Intel. But Microsoft extracts its pound of flesh, console is a crappy, low-gross-margin business. Of all the game consoles in history, NVIDIA has powered exactly three: the original Xbox, the PlayStation 3, and the Nintendo Switch. When the Xbox 360 generation comes, NVIDIA loses it (to ATI), and a huge chunk of topline revenue walks out the door.

The revenue rocket and the plateau

The numbers trace the arc. Fiscal year ending January 31, 1999: $158 million in revenue. Next year: $375 million. Then $735 million. Then, in calendar 2001 (the Xbox year): about $1.4 billion, the fastest semiconductor company ever to reach a billion in revenue, and an S&P 500 addition, in the company’s ninth year.

Then the plateau. Revenue sits near $2 billion, flat for a few years. $2.8 billion by 2005, barely profitable, net income a couple hundred million or less each year, not adding meaningfully to the cash pile.

And in 2006, AMD buys ATI. The Canadian bootstrapped survivor (ATI started in the 80s, pivoted into graphics, grew outside the Silicon Valley ecosystem) now has AMD’s full weight behind it.

Gross margin tells the pricing-power story: 66 percent today versus only 29 percent back in 2004. The moat was still being built.

2006: shoot to kill

Forbes runs a cover story called “Shoot to Kill”, AMD is coming for NVIDIA, and with revenue flat and the machine-learning and scientific-computing markets invisible, the bear case is that this company needs adult supervision, maybe an acquirer. The bull case, which Jensen holds alone: bet the farm on scientific computing, parallel compute for researchers. It turns out to be so much more than scientific computing. That bull case is ep08.

Questions and answers

Because each cycle compounds. If you double performance-per-dollar every six months while competitors need 18-24 months, after two years you are roughly 8x ahead on the curve, not 2x. Competitors were designing against your last chip while you were already shipping the next two. Speed was not an advantage. It was the entire strategy. Follow-up: Map this to software: what is the equivalent of a six-month silicon cadence in your own work, weekly releases? Daily? What would you have to give up to ship 4x faster than your competitors?

The alternative was certain death, Sega gone, cash gone, wrong architecture. The nine-month schedule with a one-frame-per-30-seconds emulator was the only path with nonzero survival odds. Rationality here is relative: when all options are bad, the “reckless” one is the one that can work. Jensen’s will-to-survive quote is the operating system underneath. Follow-up: Think of a time you chose the safe slow path when the fast risky path was the only one with survival odds. What information would have changed your choice?

Categories are mental real estate. Before “GPU,” buyers compared graphics cards on specs. After it, NVIDIA defined what the thing was. Whoever names the category sets the buying criteria, and the criteria always favor the namer. It is the same move as “cloud computing” or “smartphone”, free, permanent positioning. Follow-up: Name a category in your industry that is still unnamed or badly named. What would you call it, and what buying criteria would your name smuggle in?

Because it is huge revenue at terrible gross margin, and the platform owner (Microsoft) holds all the bargaining power, they extract their pound of flesh, and when the next generation comes, they can switch suppliers. NVIDIA learned: revenue is vanity, margin is sanity, and control of the platform beats participation in someone else’s. Note the discipline: only three consoles ever, and they walked away rather than bid down. Follow-up: Audit your own revenue: what percentage is low-margin “console business” that a partner could take away? What is your plan to replace it with high-margin platform revenue?

Proliferation, then consolidation, the Buffett pattern. Ninety undifferentiated startups could not build power. The six-month cadence killed them one cycle at a time. The two survivors took opposite paths: NVIDIA through hellish venture-backed speed, ATI as a bootstrapped Canadian outsider. Consolidation rewards the already-winning. David’s investing rule is to pay up for the winner rather than buy the laggard cheap. Follow-up: Pick a crowded market today (AI dev tools, humanoid robots, LLM wrappers). Who is on the NVIDIA path and who is on the ATI path? Who is already running away with it?

Nothing broke, the S-curve matured. Programmable shaders, the great differentiator, got copied by ATI. The Xbox revenue rolled off to the 360 generation. And the company was spending heavily on the next S-curve (scientific computing/CUDA) before it produced revenue. Flat revenue plus heavy investment reads as stagnation, but it was a company reloading. The market could not tell the difference. That is why the stock lagged for a decade. Follow-up: How do you distinguish “reloading” from “dying” in a flat-revenue company? List three leading indicators you would demand before investing.

He finds “vision” exclusionary, it implies one person’s picture everyone else must follow. He prefers “our perspective is that,” as in: our perspective is that we want to enable graphics as a new medium to tell stories. It is a small language choice that reveals the management philosophy from ep01: the mission is the boss, stated as shared perspective, not founder prophecy. Follow-up: Rewrite your own team’s mission statement replacing every “vision” with “our perspective is that.” What changes about who owns it?

Build the capability before the market: NVIDIA learned parallel computing for graphics, which became the substrate for AI. But the prerequisite lesson is survival first, you cannot compound capabilities if you die. The Riva 128 (survive), the cadence (compound), the drivers and shaders (control the stack): survive, then compound, then control. In that order. Follow-up: Score your current project 1-10 on each: survive, compound, control. Where is the weakest link, and what is this month’s fix?

Memory aids

  1. 90 → 2. Ninety graphics competitors in 1993. Two survivors (NVIDIA, ATI). Consolidation is the default.
  2. $6M → $600M. Seed post-money to IPO market cap: the 100x. $2M raised from Sequoia and Sutter Hill.
  3. 1 frame per 30 seconds. The Riva 128 emulator speed. Nine months or death.
  4. Triangles beat quadrilaterals. Bet on the industry standard’s primitive, not your own cleverer one. Sega walked in 1996.
  5. $158M → $375M → $735M → $1.4B. The revenue rocket, FY1999 to calendar 2001. Fastest semiconductor to $1B ever.
  6. Three consoles. Xbox, PS3, Nintendo Switch, the only ones NVIDIA ever powered. Low margin, high negotiating advantage for the platform owner.
  7. 29% → 66%. Gross margin 2004 versus today. Pricing power is built, not granted.
  8. Never confuse: revenue (vanity, the $500M/year Xbox deal) with margin and control (sanity, the CUDA platform that followed).
  9. Trap card: “NVIDIA won because its chips were the best.” It won because it shipped every six months while competitors shipped every two years, wrote its own drivers, and named the category. Speed, control, positioning, in that order.
  10. “My will to survive exceeds almost everybody else’s will to kill me.” Jensen’s own summary of 1993-2006. Memorize it.

How to imbibe this

  1. This week: find your six-month cadence equivalent. Whatever your team’s ship cycle is, cut it in half for one cycle as an experiment. Measure what broke, the breakage points are your real constraints.
  2. Category naming: write the one-paragraph category definition for what you build, as if you were coining “GPU.” If you cannot define the category in one paragraph, you do not own it.
  3. Survival audit: list the three things that could kill your project in the next nine months (your Sega, your quadrilateral bet, your memory-cost squeeze). For each, write the Riva-128-style emergency plan, the ugly, compressed, do-it-in-software version.
  4. Observable behavior: the next time someone proposes a “strategic partnership” with a giant, run the Xbox test: what is the gross margin, who holds the bargaining power at renewal, and what is your walk-away price? Write the answers before the first meeting.

Think it yourself

Drills continue the escalation: you now quantify strategy, not just narrate it.

  1. Guided: NVIDIA doubled performance-per-dollar every 6 months. Competitors every 24 months. After 4 years (8 NVIDIA cycles, 2 competitor cycles), how many times ahead is NVIDIA on performance-per-dollar? (Answer: 2^8 / 2^2 = 256 / 4 = 64x.)
  2. Fermi: the Riva 128 sold 1M units in 4 months in 1997. Estimate the revenue if the average selling price was ~$200 (the episode does not state MSRP, the hosts admit they did not look it up). Now estimate what fraction of a $600M-IPO company’s annual revenue that represents. Show your work and flag the uncertainty.
  3. Journaling: “My will to survive exceeds almost everybody else’s will to kill me.” Write 300 words on whether this is a learnable skill or a personality trait. Use two moments from the episode (quadrilateral crisis, 2006 AMD threat) as evidence.
  4. Reasoning exercise: in 2006, Forbes says “shoot to kill” and AMD offers adult supervision. Steelman the bear case for selling NVIDIA then: flat revenue, copied shaders, lost Xbox 360, invisible future markets. Then write Jensen’s one-paragraph bull case using only facts available in 2006.
  5. Near-unaided: you are a 1993 founder with $2M at a $6M post-money valuation in a 90-player commodity market. Write your 10-year plan to become one of two survivors. You may use only strategies demonstrated in this episode, no hindsight about AI.

Skills you can now use

  • Design a cadence advantage: convert “ship faster” from slogan to math, compute the compounding gap between your cycle time and competitors’, and identify which organizational muscles (testing, tooling, decision latency) must change to sustain it.
  • Evaluate platform partnerships: run the Xbox test on any deal with a giant, margin, renewal bargaining power, walk-away price, before signing, and know the difference between revenue that builds you and revenue that rents you.
  • Name categories: practice the GPU move, define the category your product creates in one paragraph that sets buying criteria in your favor.
  • Read S-curves: distinguish a maturing S-curve (flat revenue, copied differentiation) from a dying company by checking whether the next S-curve is being funded underneath, the “reloading vs dying” test from Q6.
