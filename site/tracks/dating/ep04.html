Glover explains covert contracts, why pleasing women kills attraction, and how emotional tension and playfulness replace the Nice Guy script.

Episode 4: How Men Keep Sabotaging Themselves

Guest: Dr Robert Glover, therapist and author of the book on Nice Guy recovery. This episode aired 2024-02-01 and ran 6462 seconds.

The episode makes one claim: the Nice Guy pattern is not kindness. It is a covert strategy for managing anxiety, and it kills attraction because it hides the real man. The way out is not becoming a jerk. It is honesty, boundaries, and a tribe of men.

The episode in one paragraph

Glover defines the Nice Guy as a man who internalized the belief “I am not okay just as I am” when young, and now tries to become what others want while hiding his needs, wants, and sexuality. The result is inauthentic, dishonest, resentful, and passive-aggressive. The engine of the pattern is three covert contracts, unspoken if-then deals: be good and you will be loved, meet everyone’s needs unasked and yours will be met unasked, do everything right and life stays smooth. The diagnostic test is resentment: a man walking around resentful and pissed off runs covert contracts. Pleasing women does not attract them. Emotional tension and playfulness do. Rehabilitation runs through safe people, radical honesty, prioritized needs, boundaries learned late, and connection with men.

The Nice Guy is a strategy, not a personality

Start with the definition, because the label misleads. A Nice Guy is not a kind man. He is a man who inaccurately learned, usually in childhood, that he is not okay just as he is. From that belief he built a strategy: become what others want, and hide the needs, wants, and sexuality that might get him rejected.

The strategy produces four traits Glover names. People-pleasing. Seeking validation from the outside, especially from women. Failing to live up to his full potential. And a dull background depression. His prototype is the middle-management man, and his line is brutal: nice guys do not finish last, they rot in middle management. The pattern is not confined to romance. It is a whole-life operating system.

Three origins feed it. Temperament: some boys are simply conflict-averse. The two redemption arcs: the “I am so good” nice guy, and the “I am so bad” nice guy, the former rebel who found religion, the military, or marriage and rebuilt himself as good. And the father generations: tyrant fathers raised compliant nice-guy sons, who then raised the current generation with “do not upset your mother” as the prime directive.

The stickiness has a developmental root. Every boy’s survival depends on his female caregiver, so every boy learns to please women early. Historically, around age twelve, a masculine initiation pulled boys into the world of men and broke that dependence. That initiation is now missing. The boy never graduates. He keeps running the childhood strategy on adult women.

Then the paradox that powers the episode. Pursuing women and trying to please them does not make them interested. Not trying to please them tends to make a man more interesting. The feminine, Glover says, is attracted to a man who is comfortable in his own skin, knows where he is going, and looks like he is having a good time going there. And there is no standard of feminine approval to meet: a man does not mature until he quits seeking the love of a woman.

The three covert contracts

The engine room. A covert contract is an unspoken if-then deal the Nice Guy strikes with the world, usually without knowing it. Glover names three.

Contract one: if I am good, then I will be liked and loved, and my needs will be met. Goodness becomes currency. He pays in niceness and waits for love as change.

Contract two: if I meet everyone else’s needs without being asked, then they will meet my needs without being asked. Nobody knows about this contract, which is the problem. He gives unasked, then waits for unasked giving in return. When it never comes, he concludes people are selfish. The truth is simpler: nobody signed the deal.

Contract three: if I do everything right, then I get a smooth, problem-free world. This is the scoreboard mentality. He tracks his good behavior like points, expecting the universe to pay out in a friction-free life. Friction arrives anyway, and he reads it as betrayal.

The diagnostic test is one question: are you walking around resentful and pissed off? If yes, you run covert contracts. Glover borrows Neil Strauss’s line: “unspoken expectations are premeditated resentment.” Resentment is not a mood. It is the receipt for a contract nobody signed.

The honest price of this section: seeing the loop does not break it. Contracts run unconsciously, which is why the test is a feeling, resentment, not a thought.

Shame, anxiety, and why the jerk is not the answer

Glover puts the Nice Guy and the jerk on one continuum. Both manipulate people and situations to manage the same two drivers: shame and anxiety. The Nice Guy manages them with flight and freeze: avoid, placate, disappear. The jerk manages them with fight: dominate, intimidate, control. Same fuel, opposite direction.

This kills the most common escape fantasy. If nice is bad, the opposite of nice must be good, right? Glover says that is a low-resolution solution. The jerk is equally inauthentic and equally attached to outcomes. There is no healthy middle point between two dysfunctional extremes. There is only a different plane entirely: self-soothing, assertiveness, boundaries, and differentiation. You do not slide along the line. You step off it.

The safety mechanism explains why stepping off is hard. For every child, abandonment equals death. That wiring never fully retires. A wife’s cold look can trigger ancient, death-level anxiety in a grown man, and he will manage it the way he learned at five: please, freeze, or hide. The reaction is not about the look. It is about the old alarm.

Two more hidings complete the picture. Nice Guys hide their needs: the needless, wantless Golden Child who claims to want nothing. And they hide their sexuality: the first sexual experiences came wrapped in shame and guilt, so desire itself feels dangerous. They are also terrible receivers. Receiving, Glover argues, robs the other person of the joy of giving. His stories: the Nordstrom laundry run, the garbage taken to the gate. The Nice Guy refuses help, insists on carrying everything, and calls it strength. It is control wearing a nicer suit.

Tension, polarity, and playfulness: what replaces pleasing

If pleasing does not attract, what does? Three replacements.

First, emotional tension. Women need emotional tension to feel attraction, arousal, and attachment. Nice creates absolutely no tension. Glover’s sports analogy: men like positive tension on a clock, which is why every sport has one, and baseball added a pitch clock. Romantic comedies run on the same fuel: the man wants to leave at the first regulation, the woman wants the tension stretched, and Bollywood figured out the long arc. The practical rule is to stop relieving tension on reflex. Answering her text instantly relieves it. Listening to her vent for hours relieves it, with an inverse relationship Glover states crudely: the longer you listen to her problems, the less likely you are to end up in bed, and you finish looking like her girlfriend with a penis. His device is the five-minute timer: set the phone for five minutes, give her undivided attention, and she runs out of steam before the timer does. Then move on. Text and callback timing create tension the same way.

Second, polarity and leading. Attraction needs polarity, a charge between masculine and feminine. When the polarity flips and she leads everything, women ask why they cannot find a man who turns them on. Glover’s door story: he tells a woman “wait for me, I will open your door,” and across several hundred women exactly one pushed back, then, in his telling, jumped him in a public place. Leading is not controlling. Leading means setting the tone, stating what you want, and giving her a chance to follow. His dance-floor metaphor: the lead proposes the move. His script: lead with a proposal, then ask. “I was thinking a white with a little tint of gray, what do you think?” Direction first, invitation second.

Third, playfulness. Glover’s marriage to Lupita, seven years at recording time, runs on it. His device: “give me the guy version.” When she vents about her day, he asks for the blunt male cut of the story, which breaks the venting spell with laughter. His deeper rule: blurt it and act on impulse. His example: he told a woman he enjoyed watching her walk away from him. The nice script would strangle that sentence. The playful man says it, and the tension spikes.

Rehabilitation: the five moves

Glover rebuilt himself, and the episode gives the sequence. One: find safe people, a coach, therapist, or men’s group, with one non-negotiable filter: the leader must have done his own work. Two: radical honesty, practiced as “here is the lie I was going to tell you, and here is the truth.” Three: make your needs a priority, starting embarrassingly concrete: the gym, the dentist, rest. Four: boundaries, which Glover says he learned in his thirties, in his second marriage, to a woman with a PhD. Five: connect with men, a tribe, with constant contact. His term for healthy men is Teflon men: nothing sticks to them, because they process it with other men instead of carrying it.

His closing story reframes the whole project. George’s mother stopped the car to break up two boys fighting, an irrational act, and at her funeral the story everyone told was that irrationality. Only the irrational behavior survives, Glover says. The Nice Guy’s tragedy is a life so controlled that nothing in it was ever irrational enough to be remembered.

How to imbibe this

This week, run the resentment audit. First, carry a small log for three days: every time you feel resentful or pissed off, write what you gave and what you secretly expected back. Name the contract number, one, two, or three. Second, practice one radical-honesty rep per day: say the true thing you were about to wrap in a lie, starting with low-stakes truths. Third, book one need you postponed: the dentist, the gym session, the rest day. Fourth, set the five-minute timer once: next time someone vents at you, give five undivided minutes, then change the subject or leave. Notice how hard not relieving the tension feels. That difficulty is the old wiring.

Think it yourself

Brain first, no AI. The drills now turn the lens on you, which is harder than analyzing cases.

  1. Write your last resentment in contract form: “I did X, so I expected Y.” Which of the three contracts is it? What is the honest version?
  2. A friend says: “I am done being nice. From now on I say whatever I want and take what I want.” Use the continuum argument to show him the error, in three sentences.
  3. Reconstruct Glover’s argument for why listening to her vent for hours kills attraction. Then steelman the opposite: when, if ever, is long listening the right move?
  4. Script a “lead with a proposal, then ask” line for three situations: picking a restaurant, planning a weekend, ending a phone call. Each must state direction first and invite second.
  5. Glover says receiving robs the other person of the joy of giving. Describe one recent moment when you refused help. Rewrite the scene with you receiving well.
  6. The episode claims there is no standard of feminine approval. If that is true, what exactly should a man optimize instead? Answer in your own words, then check against “comfortable in his own skin, knows where he is going, looks like he is having a good time going there.”

Memory aids

Mnemonic: Good, Needs, Smooth. The three contracts in order: be good, meet needs, keep it smooth. Ask “which contract?” whenever resentment appears.

Never-confuse pairs: - Nice vs kind. Kind is honest and boundaried. Nice is a strategy for managing anxiety. One attracts, the other drains. - Lead vs control. Lead sets the tone and invites her to follow. Control removes her choice. The dance floor needs a lead, not a warden. - Tension vs conflict. Tension is the charge that fuels attraction. Conflict is the fight that kills it. Hold the first, resolve the second. - Jerk vs recovered. The jerk fights from the same shame as the Nice Guy. The recovered man stepped off the line entirely.

Trap cards: - The opposite trap. “Nice failed, so I will be a jerk.” Low-resolution. The answer is a different plane, not the far end of the same line. - The venting trap. More listening feels like more caring. Past five undivided minutes, it reads as girlfriend energy and kills the charge. - The receiver trap. Refusing help feels like strength. It starves the giver and keeps you isolated. - The approval trap. Chasing a standard of feminine approval that does not exist. There is no finish line, only the treadmill.

Q&A

Q1. Explain like I am new: what is a covert contract? It is a deal you make with someone who never signed it. You think: “If I am nice enough, she will love me.” She thinks you are just being nice. When love does not arrive, you feel cheated, but there was no deal. Glover says there are three standard ones: be good and get loved, meet needs unasked and get yours met unasked, do everything right and get a smooth life. The tell is resentment. If you feel resentful, you run one.

Q2. Why does trying to please women make a man less attractive to them? Because pleasing hides the man. She meets the performance, not the person, and the performance creates no emotional tension, which is the fuel of attraction. Worse, the pleasing is strategic: it is a bid for approval wearing kindness as a costume, and people sense the hidden invoice. Glover’s paradox holds: the man who stops trying to please becomes more interesting, because for the first time she meets someone real.

Q3. A man hears this episode and decides to become cold and dominant. Where does he go wrong? He slides along the same line instead of stepping off it. The jerk manages the same shame and anxiety with fight instead of flight, so the fuel never changes. He is equally inauthentic and equally attached to outcomes. Glover’s plane, self-soothing, assertiveness, boundaries, differentiation, is not between nice and jerk. It is perpendicular to both.

Q4. Applied: your date starts venting about her coworker at minute 20. Run the episode’s playbook. Set a silent five-minute timer and give her total attention: eye contact, no phone, no fixing. When it rings or she winds down, break the spell with playfulness or a lead: “Give me the guy version,” or “I have a plan for the rest of the night, you in?” If she restarts the vent, do not re-enter it. The playbook caps the vent, holds the tension, and moves the night forward. Log afterward whether you relieved tension on reflex.

Q5. What does “abandonment equals death” explain about adult overreactions? It explains why a small coldness triggers a huge response. The child’s brain learned that losing the caregiver means dying, and the alarm never fully uninstalls. So a wife’s flat look fires a death-level alarm, and the grown man pleases, freezes, or hides the way a five-year-old would. The reaction is real but misdated. Naming it, “this is the old alarm,” is the first step toward answering the present instead of the past.

Q6. Why does Glover insist the group leader “must have done his own work”? Because an unworked leader replays the same pattern with authority. A coach who still runs covert contracts will collect approval from the group instead of serving it. The filter is the whole method in miniature: the Nice Guy must stop outsourcing his okayness, and that starts with refusing to outsource it to a guru. Safe people are proven, not titled.

Q7. What is the difference between leading and controlling, in the episode’s terms? Leading sets the tone, states what you want, and gives her a chance to follow. The dance-floor image: the lead proposes the move, the partner chooses to take it. Controlling removes the choice: demands, ultimatums, sulking when she declines. The door story works because “wait for me, I will open your door” is direction plus invitation. She can say no. Almost none do, which tells you the difference is felt, not just defined.

Skills you can now use

  • Contract detection. Hear resentment, yours or another man’s, and translate it into the numbered contract behind it.
  • Honesty reps. Deliver the “here is the lie I was going to tell you” sentence in low-stakes moments until truth is the default.
  • Tension management. Run the five-minute timer on venting, time your texts and callbacks, and notice the urge to relieve tension on reflex.
  • Lead scripts. Open with a proposal before you ask, in dating and in daily life: direction first, invitation second.
  • Receiving practice. Accept help cleanly, once a day, and watch what it does to the giver.
  • Tribe building. Start or join a men’s group contact thread, and filter any leader by the “done his own work” test.
