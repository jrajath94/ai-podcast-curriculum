Episode 2: Women Are Basically Begging For Men to Approach Them

Episode 2: Women Are Basically Begging For Men to Approach Them

Guest: Blaine Anderson, a dating coach for men. This episode aired 2024-07-22 and ran 6984 seconds.

The episode makes one claim: women want to be approached far more than men believe, and approaching is a trainable skill with a manual. The manual covers reading signals, opening, profiles, texting, dates, touch, and charisma.

The episode in one paragraph

Anderson surveyed 13,000 single women in the US and UK: 97 percent would rather meet a partner in person than online, and 95 percent wish men approached them more often in real life. From there she builds the full stack. Read green lights like eye contact plus a smile, and skip women who look away with a turned back. Open with a comment on what she is holding, doing, or wearing, or with a direct honest line. Keep an approach journal the way dieters keep a food log. Build the profile like a product page: clear face photo first, full-body shot, no gym mirror selfies. Text little, ask out after 2 to 10 exchanges. On the date, aim for her talking 70 percent of the time, split the evening into thirds, and build to six physical touches, because a date with no touch is a meeting. Charisma is not a gift. It is lightweight, fun, flirty behavior, and improv drills train it.

The market wants you: the survey and the 47.5 percent

Start with the number that reframes everything. Anderson ran an informal survey of 13,000 single women in the US and the UK. Two results: 97 percent said they would rather meet their partner in person than online, and 95 percent said they wish they were approached more often by men in real life. Anderson jokes that she is not sure 95 percent of people would agree the sky is blue.

The host, Chris Williamson, then does live arithmetic that the chapter keeps because it matters. The 95 percent figure covers single women. If roughly 50 percent of women are in relationships and 50 percent are single, then the share of all women who wish for more approaches is 0.95 times 0.50, which equals 0.475, or 47.5 percent. So the man who fears that every approach annoys its target is wrong about roughly half the women he meets, and that is before he learns to read signals at all.

The men-are-rude objection gets a direct answer. Some women do not want to be approached. More women already have a partner, are not interested in men, or will not like you for reasons outside your control. That is a numbers game, not a verdict on approaching. If you approach appropriately, Anderson says, you will not get pepper sprayed or arrested or suffer some terrible thing. The catastrophic fantasy is the anxiety talking.

The honest price of this section: none of this removes rejection. It only moves rejection from “proof I am creepy” to “the expected miss rate of a working channel.”

Read the room: signals and the creepiness list

Before you speak, read. Green lights: she makes eye contact and smiles, or gives you a smile after you catch her eye. That is an invitation to approach. Red lights: she looks away, turns her back, or angles her body away from you. Skip those women. Anderson’s system is not “approach everyone.” It is “approach the women who signal.”

The creepiness research then deflates the main fear. In a study Anderson cites, 46 percent of men said fear of being creepy limited their interactions with women. But women’s actual list of creepy behavior is specific and extreme: excessive staring, unwanted physical contact, catcalling, and unwanted online contact, like adding her on Instagram after overhearing her name at a yoga class. The episode’s line: guys who worry about being creepy usually are not creepy. The worry and the behavior live in different men.

Where to meet them: go where your hobbies already take you. Pickleball, run clubs, group activities. Anderson adds a “1 to 2 degrees removed” rule: friends of friends beat strangers, because a little context travels with you. The gym has its own etiquette: group fitness classes beat the open gym floor, and never approach a woman mid-workout with headphones on. That is the creepiness list in action.

Her sex-ratio hypothesis is the advanced move. The rarer sex holds the power in a mating market. Her example: a client went to a mahjong meetup with 4 men and about 100 women and got 3 phone numbers in one night. Work the ratio: 100 divided by 4 equals 25 women per man. At a balanced event the ratio is 1 to 1. Same man, same lines, 25 times the structural advantage. Pick rooms where the ratio favors you.

The approach: openers, journals, and flirting with the world

What do you say? Two opener families. First, comment on something she is holding, doing, or wearing: concrete, in the room, impossible to fake. Second, the direct honest line: “I noticed you from over there and I wanted to say hello, I am Blaine.” Both work because both are true. Anderson’s advice on exits uses the peak-end rule: people judge an experience by its peak and its end, so leave on a high note instead of letting the conversation die.

The approach journal is the training device. Dieters who log food lose more weight than dieters who do not, because the log forces attention. Log your approaches the same way: where, what you said, what happened, what you will try next. Attention compounds.

“Flirt with the world” is the lifestyle version, credited to Charlie Hooper of Charisma on Command. Be warm and playful with everyone: the cashier, the barista, the stranger in line. Russell Brand is Anderson’s example of a man who flirts with the world by default. The point is practice volume. If every interaction is a rep, the approach to an attractive woman is just another rep.

The profile is the product page

Anderson borrows a frame from Neil Strauss and the Game era: your profile is the product, your approach is the marketing. Men obsess over lines, openers, and tactics, the marketing, while their photos, the product, do the real work. Fix the product first.

The photo rules, from her research: lead with a clear, high-quality, recent headshot where you look at the camera. No filters, no sunglasses. No selfies and no mirror photos. Gym mirror selfies are the worst offenders. A Tough Mudder or marathon photo shows the same body with a story attached. Include one full-body shot, lifestyle filler shots, and friends-and-family photos. Never post photos with a woman who could read as an ex. One group photo is fine, but avoid “Where is Waldo” group shots where she cannot find you. Every photo holding alcohol tells one chapter of your story. Vary the chapters. Smile. Professional dating-app photographers, Anderson says, are high ROI: the product photo shoot pays for itself.

The bio rules: keep it light-hearted, make her smile, write to entertain rather than to impress. It is not a resume and not a help-wanted ad. Specific beats generic: a line about a trip to Spain for dessert wines beats “I like burritos.” Spend at least an hour per prompt, write ten, keep the best three.

App choice matters and it is local. Hinge and Bumble carry the highest intent. Tinder skews toward spam and lower intent. The best app also varies by city: Austin leans Bumble, Seattle leans Hinge. Ask around your city instead of trusting national advice.

Texting and the first date

The texting rule is one sentence: you text yourself out of a date far more than you text yourself into one. Every extra message is a chance to bore her, offend her, or talk her out of it. Keep texting minimal and logistical. After 2 to 10 good exchanges, ask her out. Get the phone number, not the Instagram: a number signals romantic intent, while Instagram is vague. Ask for the date first, then the number.

Date zero is a vibe check, not a date. The first real date should maximize the ease of yes: casual drinks or a walk, somewhere easy to reach and easy to leave. Never a four-hour fancy dinner with a stranger.

On the date, run three systems. First, the 70/30 rule: aim for her talking 70 percent of the time. Pull conversational threads instead of running a question rally. Second, the thirds rule: spend roughly a third of the date building connection, a third assessing fit, and a third flirting and having fun. Third, physical touch: a date with no touch is a meeting, and she did not come to a meeting. Anderson’s target is six physical touches across the date, logged afterward in a date journal the same way approaches go in the approach journal. Start small and appropriate: a hug hello, guiding her to her seat with a hand on her back. Escalate only with comfort.

The kiss follows the touch, not the clock. If you touched her all evening, the kiss at the end feels natural. If you never touched her, it feels out of left field. Anderson’s line for the nervous: “I really want to kiss you right now.” And the rule behind the line: if asking feels too uncomfortable, it is too soon.

Dress for the date like the product page matters, because it does. Anderson names the number one American men’s mistake: clothes that are too big. Build one go-to first-date outfit. Consider a stylist the way you would consider the dating photographer: professional eyes on the product.

Charisma is a skill: improv, playfulness, and the three things

Anderson rejects the idea that charisma is a gift. Her definition: be lightweight, fun, and flirty. Flirting is adult play. Smart men, she says, overthink dating. They are great at intellectual connection and missing the sexual and emotional channels. The fix is physical touch plus playfulness.

The training tool is improv. Her drill, point-and-name, runs in three phases. Phase one: point at objects and name them straight. Phase two: name the object one behind, a parallel-processing load. Phase three: call everything anything but what it is. The drill trains fast, unfiltered responses, which is the raw material of wit.

The comedy principle behind it: an incorrect answer beats a correct answer. Russell Brand’s line, quoted in the episode: “I don’t know if it’s right but it’s definitely enjoyable.” Anderson’s example: a man delivers a serious treatise on Kyoto and Nagasaki, then lands one silly joke. The joke does the work. Watch comedy, take an improv class, join Toastmasters. Reps, not talent.

Then the three things women want, which reframes the whole episode. One: status and trajectory, not current position. Her example: the starving guitarist becomes Ed Sheeran. Nobody dates the position, they date the arc. Medical students and MBAs get treated as doctors and executives already. Two: to feel desired. Three: to have fun. The job-framing trick follows: never state the job, state the story. The accountant says he works for a firm that helps dogs find new homes. Same job, different movie.

Social media gets a realistic treatment. The top fold of your profiles matters, because she will look. But having no social media at all is attractive too. Anderson calls it the “barn find”: the untouched classic in the garage, more interesting for being undiscovered.

The loneliness numbers and the way out

Anderson closes with her survey of single American men, and the numbers are grim. 45 percent see friends less than once a month. 35 percent feel unmotivated or gave up on dating. 54 percent say they would rather play video games than have sex. Her verdict on turnaround: 100 percent possible, if the man does the work. Start embarrassingly small: a 30-minute walk without the phone, daily, for 30 days. Chat with the cashier. The “lonely chapter” is the gap between the old friend group and the new self, and the only way across is reps.

How to imbibe this

This week, run the product audit. First, pull up your dating profile and grade it against the photo rules: clear face photo first, one full-body shot, no mirror selfies, no could-be-an-ex photos. Delete or replace every rule-breaker. Second, start the approach journal: log every approach or dating-app conversation this week with what you said and what happened. Third, do one “flirt with the world” day: be warm and playful with five strangers you will never date, the cashier, the barista, the man in line. Reps without stakes. Fourth, text audit: open your longest text thread and count the exchanges before you asked her out. If the count is past 10, you texted yourself out of dates this week.

Think it yourself

Brain first, no AI. Harder than Chapter 1: this time you apply the rules to new cases, not just recall them.

  1. A woman makes eye contact with you twice and smiles the second time. A second woman looks away and angles her body toward her friend. State the correct action for each, in one sentence each.
  2. Your friend texts a woman 40 messages over four days and she goes cold. Diagnose the failure with the episode’s texting rule, then write the 2-message version to replace messages 3 through 40.
  3. Rewrite this bio line twice: “I like burritos and hiking.” First version: specific and funny. Second version: specific and vulnerable. Spend real time, the episode demands an hour per prompt.
  4. You are at a party with 30 women and 30 men. Your friend is at a pottery class with 18 women and 2 men. Use the sex-ratio hypothesis to predict who has the better night, and show the ratios.
  5. A smart, analytical man is great at deep conversation but gets no second dates. Prescribe the fix with the episode’s terms: which two channels is he missing, and what is the smallest rep he can do this week?
  6. Apply the peak-end rule to a date that goes well at minute 45 but fading at minute 75. When do you leave, and why?

Memory aids

Mnemonic: 70-30, thirds, six. She talks 70 percent. The date splits in thirds. Six touches. Say it as a phone number: 70-30-thirds-6.

Never-confuse pairs: - Green light vs politeness. A smile plus eye contact is a green light. A polite smile while turning away is not. Read the body, not the mouth. - Product vs marketing. Photos and life are the product. Openers and lines are the marketing. Men who fix the marketing first polish a product nobody wants. - Date zero vs date one. Date zero is a vibe check: short, cheap, easy exit. Date one earns more time. Skipping date zero is how you buy a four-hour dinner with a stranger. - Number vs Instagram. A phone number signals intent. Instagram signals maybe. Ask for the date, then the number, in that order.

Trap cards: - The 95 percent trap. 95 percent of single women is not 95 percent of women. Times 50 percent single, it is 47.5 percent of all women. Still huge. Not 95. - The texting trap. More texting feels like more connection. The episode says it is more chances to disqualify yourself. Connection is built in person. - The creepy trap. If you worry about being creepy, you are probably not creepy. The creepy list names staring, grabbing, catcalling, and cyber-stalking, not saying hello.

Q&A

Q1. Explain like I am new: what does “flirt with the world” mean and why does it work? It means being warm, playful, and a little bold with everyone, not just women you want to date. It works for two reasons. First, volume: the cashier, the barista, and the stranger in line become practice reps, so the approach to an attractive woman is rep number 40, not rep number 1. Second, signal: a man who is fun with everyone reads as safe and socially fluent, which is exactly what a woman screens for in the first ten seconds.

Q2. Why does Anderson insist on the phone number over Instagram? Because the medium is the message. Asking for her number says “I want to take you on a date,” which is clear romantic intent. Asking for Instagram says “I want to watch your stories,” which is vague and low-commitment. Vague gets vague results. The order matters too: ask for the date first, then the number to arrange it. The number then has a reason to exist.

Q3. A man says his openers never work. He opens with clever lines he memorized. What is the misdiagnosis? He fixes the marketing while the product stays broken. Anderson’s product-vs-marketing frame says photos and life do the heavy lifting. Openers are a small multiplier. A memorized clever line also fails the truth test: her two approved opener families are a true comment on the room and a true direct statement. Memorized lines read as performance. Tell him to fix three photos first, then open with something true.

Q4. Applied: walk through the first 10 minutes of an approach using every rule in this chapter. Minute 0: you catch her eye across the room and she smiles, a green light, so you approach. Minute 1: opener from family one, comment on what she is holding, or family two, the direct line: “I noticed you from over there and I wanted to say hello, I am James.” Minutes 2 to 8: lightweight, fun, flirty, her talking most of the time, one silly joke instead of a resume. Minute 9: peak-end rule, leave on the high note: “I have to get back to my friends, but I would love to take you for a drink Thursday. What is your number?” You asked for the date, then the number. Log it in the approach journal tonight.

Q5. What is the “barn find” and when does it beat having good social media? A barn find is a man with no social media at all, named for the untouched classic car found in a garage. It beats decent-but-tryhard social media because it signals a life too full to curate, plus mystery. It does not beat great social media with a strong top fold. The ranking: great profiles first, no profiles second, mediocre profiles last.

Q6. Explain the 70/30 rule and the failure mode it prevents. Aim for her talking 70 percent of the date while you pull conversational threads. It prevents the question rally, where the man fires interview questions and the date feels like a job screening. Thread-pulling means taking one thing she said and going deeper on it, which makes her feel heard. Feeling heard is what she remembers, not your answers.

Q7. Why six touches, and what goes wrong at zero? Six is Anderson’s target count for physical touch across a first date, logged in the date journal. At zero touches, the evening reads as a meeting, and a kiss at the end feels out of left field because the physical channel never opened. The touches also solve the smart-guy problem: analytical men connect intellectually and starve the physical and emotional channels. Small appropriate touches, hug hello, hand on her back guiding her to her seat, open the channel early so the end of the night has somewhere to go.

Skills you can now use

  • Signal reading. Classify any woman’s body language in three seconds: eye contact plus smile means approach, look-away plus turned back means skip.
  • Profile audit. Grade any dating profile against the photo and bio rules, and list the three highest-ROI fixes.
  • Texting discipline. Cap any pre-date thread at 10 exchanges, then ask out or drop it. Count, do not feel.
  • Date architecture. Plan a date with ease of yes, 70/30 talk ratio, thirds split, six-touch target, and a peak-end exit. Run it from the plan, then journal the variance.
  • Ratio scouting. Compute the sex ratio of any room before you invest the evening: women divided by men. Favor rooms above 2.
  • Improv reps. Run the point-and-name drill through all three phases as a daily five-minute practice for unfiltered wit.
