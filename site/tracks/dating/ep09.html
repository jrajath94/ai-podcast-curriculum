Episode 9: How relationships work: on value

Guest: Orion Taraban, psychologist and author of The Value of Others. This episode aired 2025-11-17 and ran 649 seconds, the shortest in the track.

The episode makes one claim: relationships run on wants, not on virtue. People approach those who seem to have what they want, stay with those who actually have it, and leave when every want is discharged or none ever is. The only durable path is gradual satisfaction plus retained means plus cultivated desire.

The episode in one paragraph

Taraban calls his principles axiomatic: self-evident, non-negotiable, true across all relationship dynamics. Axiom one: people approach people who have things they want or need. His book’s line: “it is neither the good nor the loving nor the virtuous who are desired, but the people whom others want things from.” If nobody wants anything from you, you are alone, because people do not move toward people they want nothing from. Axiom two: to be approached, you must at least seem to have the means. The person who has the means but does not seem to possess them gets approached no more than the person who lacks them. Seeming opens the door. Having keeps you in the room. Then the paradox: “if you give people what they want or need, they will no longer want or need you.” “No one continues to pay the plumber.” The game does not run on gratitude, so sacrifice does not buy permanence. Three obstacles follow: it ends before it begins without seeming, it ends if you give everything, it ends if you never give. The pathway between them: gradually satisfy some wants while retaining the means to satisfy others, and actively cultivate new desires, because in the act of satisfying any want, you must ensure other wants remain satisfiable.

Seeming opens, having keeps

Start with the double liability. Approaching someone costs effort and carries opportunity cost: the time spent with you is time not spent with someone who could satisfy the want. So people only approach those they believe can pay off. That belief is about seeming. A man with deep resources who shows none of them gets approached exactly as often as a man with nothing. The market cannot price what it cannot see.

But seeming alone does not hold. To maintain the relationship, you must actually possess the means. The sequence is strict: seem to be approached, have to remain. Men who polish only the seeming get first dates and no seconds. Men who build only the having, in private, get no first dates at all.

The honest price: this axiom has no exception for virtue. Good, loving, and virtuous lose to wanted-from, every time, in his model.

The paradox and the three deaths

The tragedy, in his word, is the paradox. Give people what they want or need, and they will no longer want or need you. You undermined the basis of the relationship in the dischargement of it. “No one continues to pay the plumber.” The plumber was precious during the leak and invisible after. The game does not run on gratitude.

This is where most people’s strategy breaks. They reason that great expense or sacrifice will secure the future: I gave so much, so she owes continued relationship. Taraban says nothing guarantees continued relationship, and sacrifice buys none of it. Past payment is not a subscription.

Three obstacles, stated as three ways relationships die. First, it ends before it begins: you do not have it, or do not seem to. Second, it ends if you give everything: you satisfied every want, made yourself obsolete and redundant. Third, it ends if you never give: frustration builds, competitors promise satisfaction, and she leaves for the better likelihood. Note the symmetry of two and three: total giving and total withholding both kill. The survivable region is the narrow middle.

The pathway: gradual, retained, cultivated

The prescription follows by elimination. Gradually satisfy some wants and needs. Simultaneously retain the means to satisfy others. And in the act of satisfying any want, ensure other wants remain satisfiable. Since people do not arrive with inexhaustible wants, this often requires the active cultivation of desires in others.

Taraban says the quiet part himself: “If that sounds a little nefarious, that is because it is.” The chapter keeps his framing and adds the honest label: this is the episode’s descriptive claim about how people operate, presented as an axiom, not as a moral code the chapter endorses. The alternative he offers is the belief that people with nothing left to want from you will stay out of gratitude for the past. “This, my friend, is not how people operate.”

The corporate analogy makes the mechanism concrete. Clever employers diversify into complex hierarchies so they can promote gradually: never promote and the employee jumps ship, promote to the top and the carrot is gone. So each consumed carrot is immediately replaced with a new one. The workforce stays dedicated. All voluntary relationships, the episode says, work the same way: every satisfied desire must be accompanied by a cultivated new one. Satisfy everything once and for all, and you transformed yourself into a liability, still paying her costs while she pays nothing back.

His closing line: “Understand these principles and thrive. Ignore these principles and suffer. It is that simple.”

How to imbibe this

This week, audit your value ledger. First, list what people currently want from you, in dating and outside it. If the list is empty, the episode says you are invisible, and building starts there. Second, check your seeming: for each item on the list, ask whether a stranger could see it. Hidden value scores zero approaches. Third, find one place where you are over-giving: a want you satisfy instantly and fully, training the other person to price you at zero. Slow it down deliberately. Fourth, name one desire you could cultivate honestly, a trip, a project, a shared goal, that gives the future something to want. The episode’s word is nefarious. Your constraint is honesty: cultivate real desires, not manufactured ones.

Think it yourself

Brain first, no AI. Short episode, sharp drills. The difficulty is now unaided: no worked examples below, only the questions.

  1. State the three obstacles from memory, then place your last ended relationship on them: which death was it?
  2. A friend gave everything, expensive gifts, total availability, and she left anyway. Explain it with the paradox, then tell him the one thing he must stop doing.
  3. Design the carrot hierarchy for a two-year relationship: name three successive desires, each satisfiable only after the last, with the means retained throughout.
  4. The episode says seeming is necessary. Where is the line between seeming and deception? Draw it in two sentences, then test it against the dating-profile advice from Chapter 2.
  5. Argue against the axiom: give the strongest case that gratitude does sustain some relationships, with a concrete example. Then give Taraban’s reply.
  6. Apply the three obstacles to friendship, not romance. Which obstacle kills most male friendships, and what does the pathway prescribe?

Memory aids

Mnemonic: Seem, Have, Gradual. Seem to be approached. Have to be kept. Gradual to survive.

Never-confuse pairs: - Seeming vs deception. Seeming makes real value visible. Deception displays value you lack. Gate one needs the first, and gate two punishes the second. - Gradual vs withholding. Gradual satisfies some now and more later. Withholding satisfies nothing ever. One paces, the other starves. - Gratitude vs payment. Gratitude is a feeling about the past. Payment is value for the present. The game runs on the second.

Trap cards: - The sacrifice trap. “I gave so much, she owes me.” Nothing guarantees continuation. The ledger does not carry forward. - The virtue trap. “But I am good, loving, virtuous.” The desired are the wanted-from. Virtue is not the currency. - The hidden trap. Real value, invisible. Approached as often as nothing. Seeming is not vanity, it is the entry ticket. - The total trap. Giving everything feels like love. It manufactures obsolescence. The plumber gets paid during the leak.

Q&A

Q1. Explain like I am new: why do people approach anyone at all? Because they want or need something and believe that person can provide it. Nobody moves toward someone they want nothing from. That is the whole axiom. It sounds cold, and Taraban says it is axiomatic anyway: self-evident, non-negotiable, true whether you act with it or against it. The practical upshot is a question to ask about yourself: what do people currently want from me? If the answer is nothing, expect solitude.

Q2. What is the difference between the two gates, and why do men fail each? Gate one tests seeming: you must appear to have what others want, or you are never approached. Men fail it by hiding real value, assuming quality speaks for itself. It does not. Gate two tests having: you must actually possess the means, or you are not retained. Men fail it by performing value they lack, passing gate one on credit. The sequence punishes both errors in order: invisible men get no dates, fraudulent men get no seconds.

Q3. State the paradox precisely, then give the mechanism. “If you give people what they want or need, they will no longer want or need you.” The mechanism: wants are the basis of the relationship, so discharging every want removes the basis. The plumber image: precious during the leak, forgotten after. Gratitude does not convert into future payment because the game runs on present value, not past service. This is why total devotion can end a relationship faster than neglect.

Q4. Applied: a man does everything for his girlfriend and she pulls away. Prescribe with the chapter. First, diagnose: he races toward obstacle two, giving everything, manufacturing his own obsolescence. Second, stop the full discharge: pull back availability and favors to a sustainable gradual pace. Third, check gate one in reverse: did he ever cultivate new shared desires, or did the relationship run on old wants for a year? Fourth, rebuild the carrot ladder: one new joint desire, honestly cultivated, with his means to satisfy it retained and visible. The prescription is not games. It is pace plus future.

Q5. What does “active cultivation of desires” mean, and why does the episode call it nefarious? It means deliberately creating new wants in the other person, trips, goals, experiences, so that future satisfaction has somewhere to go. It is nefarious because it treats the partner’s desire as something to manage rather than something to discover. Taraban admits the charge and keeps the prescription: the alternative is the belief that gratitude sustains relationships, which he says is false. The chapter’s guardrail: cultivate real desires you share, not artificial scarcity you stage.

Q6. How does the corporate analogy map onto dating, point by point? The hierarchy is the relationship’s future: levels of promotion are levels of shared desire. Gradual promotion is gradual satisfaction: each step rewards without ending the game. The replacement carrot is cultivated desire: every consumed carrot gets a successor. Never promoting is obstacle three: the employee jumps ship to a competitor. Promoting to the top instantly is obstacle two: no carrot remains, dedication dies. The employer who runs this well keeps a dedicated workforce. The partner who runs it well keeps a living relationship.

Skills you can now use

  • Value ledger. List what others currently want from you, and mark each as visible or hidden.
  • Gate check. Test any approach situation: do I seem to have it, and do I actually have it?
  • Pacing. Convert total giving into gradual giving: satisfy some now, retain means, cultivate next.
  • Death diagnosis. Classify any failing relationship into the three obstacles and name the exit it heads for.
  • Carrot design. Build a ladder of successive shared desires with the means to satisfy each.
  • Sacrifice audit. Catch yourself pricing past sacrifice as future credit, and reprice in present value.
