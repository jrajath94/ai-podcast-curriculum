The arrival fallacy

Source fact The episode asks: what if the reason success does not feel like success has nothing to do with what you achieved, and everything to do with how your mind is wired? It explores what Dave Evans calls the arrival fallacy: why reaching a goal so rarely delivers the happiness we expect it to.

Published fact The term was coined by Tal Ben-Shahar, a Harvard-trained positive psychology expert, in his book Happier. He coined it partly from his own experience as an elite squash player: winning again and again, feeling great for a night, then watching the emptiness move back in. The fallacy is the false belief that reaching a goal will produce lasting happiness or completion.

Published fact The mechanism underneath is hedonic adaptation: the well-documented tendency of humans to adjust to a new normal fast. A new salary, a new title, a harder season of life, the baseline quietly levels up with it, so the gap you chase never actually closes. You do get a real hit when you win. It is short-lived by design.

Nugget 1 · Arrival fallacy

Happiness is in the process, not the destination

What happened. Ben-Shahar won as an elite athlete and felt the emptiness return each time. The achievement delivered a night of joy, not a life of it.

Why it matters. If arrival never pays, the whole strategy of postponing life until the next milestone is a bad trade you keep repeating.

The principle. Treat goals as means, not ends. Let the goal liberate you to enjoy the climb.

Source: published accounts of Ben-Shahar work. Published fact

Dave Evans on designing your life

Source fact The episode guest is Dave Evans: a Stanford lecturer, co-author of Designing Your Life, and the engineer who led the design of Apple first mouse.

Source fact The episode covers why achievement does not satisfy and what to do instead.

Interpretation The resume matters because it is the argument. Evans spent a career designing physical products, then turned the designer toolkit on life itself. A designer does not ask what the perfect life looks like and then despair at the gap. A designer prototypes: small tests, fast feedback, no precious plans. Applied to the arrival fallacy, the advice writes itself: stop designing your life around the summit photo and start prototyping the climb.
