The midnight phone call

Imagine a phone call in the middle of the night with news of a death in the family. How would you expect to feel? Most of us have an answer ready: devastation, tears, weeks or months of deep, unrelenting grief. We expect the world to stop.

The episode asks a harder question. What if the most common response to loss is not that at all? What if, for most people, the world keeps turning, and they turn with it?

Source fact What follows is three decades of research from Columbia psychologist George Bonanno, work that challenges nearly everything our culture says about how humans react to tragedy, loss, and suffering.

George

Bonanno interest in the subject did not begin in a lab. He grew up in a Sicilian immigrant neighborhood in Chicago. His father was hard-working, but his rages scared the household. As a boy George would find his father lying in the dark and try to talk to him. His father pushed him away.

At 17 George left home. His father told him that if he left, he should not come crawling back. George drifted through his early twenties. At 23, living in Colorado, the phone rang. His older brother told him their father was dead.

George braced for devastation. It never came.

“What I experienced was essentially silence.”

George Bonanno, via the episode

People moved cautiously around him, as if he might splinter if they said the wrong thing. He felt grateful that his father pain was over, and confused. He was supposed to feel a certain way, and he did not.

Nugget 1 · The silence

Not feeling destroyed is data, not denial

What happened. Bonanno expected shattering grief at his father death and instead experienced something like silence, then felt confused about his own reaction.

Why it matters. The confusion came from a script about how grief must look, not from the grief itself.

The principle. When your feelings break the expected script, question the script first.

Julia

Years later, as a researcher, Bonanno kept meeting people who skipped the expected script. One was a student he calls Julia. Home from college, cooking dinner with her mother, she heard her mother cry out. Her father rode his bicycle home from work when a car hit him, and he died in the ICU as Julia and her mother arrived.

After the shock, Julia returned to school and threw herself into her work. Friends asked if she wanted to talk about it. She said she did not. She wanted to be with her friends and live her life.

Her mother grew worried that Julia forgot her father, accused her of denying her grief, and suggested a grief counselor. Note the direction of the worry. Nobody sent Julia to therapy because grief knocked her off balance. They sent her because she did not grieve enough.

Jed

Then there was Jed, an aspiring musician working at an upscale restaurant in the West Village. One freezing December night he stepped into an intersection. A garbage truck made an illegal right turn, clipped him, and pulled him under. It crushed his left leg and hip to a mass of blood and bone.

Jed stayed conscious through it all. The cold slowed his bleeding enough to save his life. He spent six weeks in a medically induced coma, underwent roughly 20 surgeries, and lost his entire left leg and part of his hip.

He braced for the psychological aftermath. For about a week, vivid memories of the accident played on a loop. Then they stopped. Jed was confused, not by the suffering, but by its absence. Years later he became a university professor, and his question stayed with him:

“Why was I doing okay?”

Nugget 2 · Jed question

The haunting question is why am I okay

What happened. After losing a leg and surviving six weeks in a coma, Jed intrusive memories stopped after a week, and he could not understand his own recovery.

Why it matters. George, Julia, and Jed all asked some version of what is wrong with me. The culture had taught them that resilience is suspicious.

The principle. Treat recovery as information. The mind often knows the way back before theory does.

The prediction

Before the research, the episode runs an exercise. When a person goes through a serious loss, what share of people will develop chronic, long-lasting psychological damage? Hold your number.

After the 9/11 attacks, mental health experts went through the same exercise. The consensus was that a mental health crisis of unprecedented proportions lay ahead, with not enough resources to meet it. FEMA allocated about 130 million dollars for emergency funds in New York, mostly for free therapy.

Early surveys seemed to confirm the worst. Nearly 8 percent of Manhattan residents met the criteria for post-traumatic stress disorder. For those near the World Trade Center, 20 percent. For those directly affected, possibly 30 percent or higher. But as time went on, the numbers dropped precipitously, and by six months they were more or less back to normal.

The pattern repeated with COVID. Experts predicted a catastrophe. Instead, suicides stayed the same or declined globally.

Four trajectories

In a landmark 2018 study published in Clinical Psychology Review, researchers analyzed decades of data on how people respond to adversity. They found four distinct trajectories.

The first is chronic suffering: people who cannot move past the loss. It is real, painful, and deserves serious care, but it characterizes at most about 10 percent of people. The second is recovery: significant struggle at first, then gradual improvement over a year or two. The third is a delayed response: barely getting by, then slowly worsening.

The fourth is the resilience trajectory: short-term distress and upheaval, then a return to normal functioning. Early in his career Bonanno found that almost everyone assumed hardly anybody showed this pattern, and that something was wrong with those who did. When researchers actually looked, the majority showed it. Almost always the majority.

Researchers replicated the finding after mass shootings, spinal cord injuries, divorce, job loss, and financial ruin. The resilience trajectory is not the exception. It is the norm.

Nugget 3 · Four trajectories

Resilience is the norm, not the miracle

What happened. Decades of data sorted into four paths after adversity. Chronic suffering capped at about 10 percent. The resilient majority felt distress, then kept functioning.

Why it matters. Most of us carry an inverted map: we treat the 10 percent as the rule and the majority as lucky outliers.

The principle. Plan for the resilient case and reserve intensive help for the minority who truly need it.

Source: episode transcript, 00:08:10. Study: Clinical Psychology Review, 2018. Source fact

Why we believe the fragility script

If most people are resilient, why does our culture tell a different story? Part of the answer is a theory you have heard of. In the late 1960s, psychiatrist Elizabeth Kubler-Ross proposed five stages: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance. The model described people facing their own mortality. Over decades it was adopted as the map for how all of us are supposed to grieve.

There is one problem. The research never supported it. The model is far too neat to be true, and study after study contradicts it. Worse, the theory prescribes reality. Miss the depression stage and you must be stuck in denial. Reach acceptance quickly and the model says you hide something.

“strong emotions do not equal psychopathology”

Patricia Resick, PTSD researcher, via the episode

Being upset after a terrible event is not a disorder. It is what the stress response system was designed to do. The mistake is to confuse initial distress with lasting damage.

Three forces keep the wrong script alive. First, the resilience blind spot: in the middle of intense distress, you cannot imagine ever feeling different, so you mistake the moment for the future. Second, therapist sampling: therapists spend their days with people who struggle and rarely meet the resilient majority who never walk through the door, so they overgeneralize. Third, social media, where trauma dumping is common, and an asymmetry of judgment: warn of disaster and be wrong, and people forgive your good heart. Predict resilience and be wrong, and people call you heartless.

The wrong script has real costs. Trigger warnings were built on the idea of hidden traumas lurking raw inside people. Research shows they either do not help or they cause harm: warned people often feel more anxious. Teaching people to brace for emotional damage may undermine the flexibility they need to cope. And walking on eggshells builds an invisible cage: we stop laughing around the bereaved and treat them as if they might break.

Nugget 4 · The five stages

A map for the dying became a cage for the living

What happened. Kubler-Ross five stages described the dying, not the grieving. Culture adopted it anyway, and research never backed the transfer.

Why it matters. A descriptive model became prescriptive. People now get diagnosed by a checklist that was never true.

The principle. Ask of any famous model: who was it built for, and does the evidence cover my case?

Laughter in grief

Working with psychologist Dacher Keltner, Bonanno interviewed people who had recently lost a spouse. The majority showed genuine laughter and smiling during the interviews. A person might cry with head in hands, head in hands, and thirty seconds later laugh through tears at a memory.

That laughter is not denial and not heartlessness. It is connection, with the listener and with the person lost. Genuine laughter and smiling during grief correlate with better long-term mental health. The human heart can hold grief and joy at the same time.

Nugget 5 · Laughter

Joy during grief predicts healing

What happened. Most recent widows and widowers laughed genuinely in interviews, and those who did fared better in the long run.

Why it matters. The fragility script reads laughter as denial. The data reads it as a sign of life returning.

The principle. Do not police your own joy after loss. It is part of the recovery, not a betrayal of it.

Source: episode transcript. Research with Dacher Keltner. Source fact

Talking with his father

Years after his father death, Bonanno became a parent himself. In the exhausting work of raising children, he found himself longing for a relationship with his father that he never had. So he created one. He began to talk with his father, imagining the conversations, telling him about the struggles with money and parenthood, picturing how his father might respond.

Death had paradoxically made the relationship possible: no rages, no fear, no ultimatums. This is what resilience looks like. Not the absence of sadness, not the denial of loss, but the ability to hold grief and growth in the same hand. To cry on a Tuesday and laugh on a Wednesday.

Interpretation The danger was never that terrible things would happen. They inevitably will. The danger lies in carrying a script that says you are supposed to be destroyed by them. The trauma script says you are easily broken. The science says you are not.
