Part 1

Beliefs Are Grayscale

SOURCEBayes rule tells you how a probability should change when new evidence arrives. As a habit of mind it says: your confidence in an idea should shift, up or down, whenever relevant evidence arrives. Nothing sits at 0 percent or 100 percent. Everything lives somewhere in between, and the position moves.

SOURCEGalef opens with the job hunt. You apply and wait. News arrives that they wanted exactly your degree, and your confidence rises. News arrives that applicant numbers ran high this year, and it falls. Nobody finds this strange. Nobody insists on 0 or 100. The belief is grayscale and it moves with evidence, naturally. We run this machinery on job hunts and abandon it on ideologies.

SOURCETo use the rule on real beliefs you need probabilities for things that are not dice. Galef's bridge: talk about subjective confidence, called credence, and cash it out as betting odds. To say “I am 67 percent confident” means “I would accept 2-to-1 odds.” If you would not take the bet, your stated confidence is a pose.

SOURCEThe trick coin snaps it together. A coin is flipped, covered, unseen. What is the probability of heads? Galef peeks and covers it again. Your answer should be one half. Hers is near zero. She saw tails. Two different probabilities, neither wrong. Probability lives in the head, not the world. It describes the knower's information, not the coin.

INTERPRETATIONThe cash out rule for the reader: ban 0 and 100 from your working vocabulary for a week. Every claim gets a number. The claims that resist numbering are the possessions, and the possessions are the problem.

Nugget

Grayscale beliefs

What happened. SOURCEGalef replaces binary belief with a slider from 0 to 100 that evidence pushes around.

Why it matters. INTERPRETATIONA belief that never moves while evidence arrives is not a probability. It is a possession.

The principle. INTERPRETATIONNumber every belief. Cash it as odds you would accept. Move the slider by the evidence.

Part 2

The Two-Worlds Test for Evidence

SOURCEThe Bayesian definition of evidence compares how likely an observation is in two worlds: the world where the hypothesis is true, and the world where it is false. Evidence is strong when the observation is much likelier in one world than the other.

SOURCEGalef's hair example: someone says your new haircut looks good. From a generic person, that is almost no evidence, because most people say it whether they mean it or not. From Jane, a friend renowned for brutal honesty, it is strong evidence. Same sentence, different weight, decided by the two worlds comparison.

SOURCEThe car crash case: you crash in a way that is arguably not your fault. The human move is to build the exonerating story. The Bayesian move asks the two worlds question. Bad drivers crash more often on average, so the crash is evidence against your driving even with a true exonerating story. Your confidence should downgrade at least a little. The story explains this crash. It does not erase the base pattern.

SOURCEGalef catches herself live. A colleague Bob complains that Amy runs late. Her first thought: this proves Bob is jealous of Amy, a hypothesis she already held. Then the two worlds test. Bob dislikes lateness generally and complains generally, so the complaining is quite likely in the non jealous world too. Verdict: weak evidence. She updated hard on fitting evidence and ignored that it also fit the alternative.

INTERPRETATIONThe fix for the reader: never ask only does this fit my hypothesis. Always ask the second question: does it also fit the alternative? The update shrinks to the difference between the two worlds.

Nugget

The two-worlds test

What happened. SOURCEGalef weighs every observation in two worlds and updates only by the difference.

Why it matters. INTERPRETATIONIt fits my hypothesis is not evidence. Evidence is the gap between the worlds.

The principle. INTERPRETATIONRun the test before celebrating. If the evidence fits both worlds, shrink the update.

Part 3

Base Rates: The Prior You Forgot

SOURCEThe classic trap, given to doctors who mostly get it wrong. A screening test is 90 percent accurate. A patient tests positive. What is the chance she has the disease? The missing piece: how common is it? Suppose 1 in 1,000 has it. Work it by hand for 1,000 patients. One has it, and the test catches her: 1 true positive. Of the 999 healthy, the test wrongly flags 10 percent: about 100 false positives. Total positives: about 101. True cases among them: 1 of 101, under 1 percent.

A 90 percent test returns a 0.9 percent answer

Hover any bar for detail. The positives come from two groups. Count both.

Source: Galef interview, ep073, via the track chapter. Original toy chart built from episode claims. Exact arithmetic: 0.0009 divided by 0.1008, about 0.9 percent.

SOURCEDoctors who remember the base rate still guess 70 or 80 percent. The prior is that easy to underweight. Galef's real life version: renting with colleagues, they hired a plumber who snooped in unrelated rooms. Instead of fleeing, they ran the numbers. Base rates for robbery in the area: low. Evidence strength: moderate. Low prior times moderate evidence means stay. They stayed. The episode's word for the whole discipline is gloriously nerdy and correct:

they looked up base rates.

The episode's verdict on the plumber decision

SOURCEGalef names the counter trap too: the fallacy of gray. It is a mistake to see everything as black and white, and also a mistake to see everything as the same shade of gray. People abuse uncertainty as a shield, saying we just cannot know when the evidence strongly favors one side. The discipline asks honestly how much likelier the evidence is in each world, and accepts the lopsided answer.

INTERPRETATIONThe plumber rule for the reader: before your next consequential judgment, write the base rate first, one line, then look at the evidence. Low prior times moderate evidence means stay. The vivid new fact does not get a vote until the prior is on the table.

Nugget

The base rate

What happened. SOURCEA 90 percent accurate test at a 1 in 1,000 base rate gives a sub 1 percent answer. The prior decides.

Why it matters. INTERPRETATIONVivid evidence crowds out the quiet prior. The crowd out is the error.

The principle. INTERPRETATIONLook up base rates before consequential judgments. Count the population, not the story.

Part 4

Your Brain Is Not a Bayes Net

SOURCEA Bayes net reprices every related probability the instant one moves. Galef's example: learning Bob got into Harvard raises both the IQ probability and the legacy probability. Then learning Bob is a legacy lowers the IQ probability somewhat. In the AI, every related node reprices instantly. The human brain does this sometimes and usually does not.

SOURCEThat gap explains McRaney's deepest frustration. Arguing with someone, you catch them in a contradiction and expect a cascade. One domino falls, the network follows. It never works that way. Galef: the brain is not a Bayes net. A person can lose the God node entirely while the neighboring nodes sit untouched. Beliefs that look logically connected are stored independently. Full propagation would be computationally ruinous.

SOURCESo the arguer's Jenga strategy fails on the architecture. There is no single block to pull. Galef's replacement: the source check habit. When a belief comes up for a decision, trace it to its source. Ask whether you still endorse that source, or whether it is long defunct and you never noticed. If the source died, downgrade the belief. Manual propagation, on demand, for the beliefs that matter.

INTERPRETATIONThe honest cost: full updating on millions of beliefs would freeze you. Deploy the engine on medium and large decisions, and on any belief carrying heat. Grayscale is not grayness: lopsided evidence demands lopsided confidence.

Nugget

No Jenga block

What happened. SOURCEBrains skip propagation. Catching one contradiction does not cascade through the network.

Why it matters. INTERPRETATIONThe Jenga strategy wastes arguments on towers that cannot fall that way.

The principle. INTERPRETATIONTrace the belief to its source. Dead source, downgraded belief. Argue nodes, not towers.
