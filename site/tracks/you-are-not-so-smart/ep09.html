Episode 9: Tribal Psychology with Lilliana Mason and Dan Kahan

2018-02-26 · Instructor: Lilliana Mason, Dan Kahan · You Are Not So Smart

Mason and Kahan on how tribal psychology scrambles public discourse, and why being right loses to group loyalty. Episode page

Why this episode matters

Episode 8 showed how fringe communities form. This one shows the same machinery eating the mainstream: science, elections, and shared reality. Tribal psychology turns evidence into identity badges, and once an issue becomes a badge, being a good member beats being correct, every time, measurably, even in arithmetic. This is the hardest chapter because its conclusion is bleak and its fixes are slow. It is also the most practical: it tells you exactly how issues get polluted and how to keep them clean.

The machine: social identity theory

Groups from nothing

In the 1970s, Henri Tajfel built social identity theory on a disturbingly small foundation. He sorted strangers into groups over nothing and watched them discriminate instantly. His question: strip away every real difference, add meaningless distinctions one at a time, and find the point where favoritism starts. The answer: there is no point. Any noticeable difference triggers it.

The dots experiment: subjects saw a page of 40 dots for half a second and estimated the count. Tajfel ignored the answers and randomly labeled each person an overestimator or an underestimator. Then came the money. Subjects allocated funds between other participants’ groups, labeled overestimator and underestimator, choosing between splits like everyone gets $5, or your group gets $4 and the other gets $3.

They chose $4 and $3. Read that again: subjects gave up a dollar for their own group to take two dollars from the other group. Maximum ingroup advantage beat maximum total welfare, from people sorted by a coin toss minutes earlier, allocating money they would never receive. Tajfel repeated it with paintings, with random group numbers, with everything. Same result. The minimal group paradigm: humans form groups over anything, then immediately favor us and punish them.

Why: inclusion plus exclusion

Marilyn Brewer’s account: humans need both inclusion and exclusion. We need to belong to something, and we need the something to have boundaries, or belonging means nothing. Underneath is self-esteem: the group’s status becomes your status. Threaten the group and you threaten the self, so group conflict is self-defense wearing a uniform.

McRaney’s preferred evolutionary story: our primate ancestors ate young leaves, which demanded large territories, which made them territorial, which favored troops that could tell us from them. The signaling changed (smells and barks became memes and outrage retweets). The wiring did not.

Affective polarization: it is not about disagreement

The new polarization

Mason’s distinction: the old polarization literature measured attitude extremity, how far apart the policy positions sit. The new literature measures affective polarization: do partisans like each other, can they get along. Disagreement is optional. Mason’s finding: people can agree on substance and still discriminate on identity alone.

The Pew numbers in the episode: 92 percent of Republicans sit to the right of the median Democrat, and 94 percent of Democrats sit to the left of the median Republican. The medians that nearly touched in 1994 had split apart by 2014. About 20 percent are hardcore enough to say they would never vote for anyone backing an idea from the other side. 27 percent of Democrats call the Republican Party a threat to the nation’s well-being. 36 percent of Republicans say the same about Democrats. People do not want to marry across the line or live near it.

The anger is about the party, not the policies

Mason’s experiment: threaten Democrats’ cherished positions (abortion rights ending, marriage and healthcare guarantees) without naming the party, and measure anger and outgroup dislike. Then threaten the party itself. The position threats moved people less. The party threat moved them more: more anger, stronger partisan attachment, more outgroup dislike.

The conclusion she draws: “We all think we have these very logical thought processes,” but the emotional engine runs on the group, not the issues. The issues are the vocabulary. The tribe is the sentence.

The welfare flip

Geoffrey Cohen’s experiment, Mason’s favorite ever conducted: give people a welfare policy position, but experimentally vary which party supposedly holds it. Subjects’ positions flip with the label, all the way across the policy spectrum, a 100 percent move. Then ask them why they hold the position. They do not say “because my party does.” They invent reasons. The position was rented from the tribe. The justification was manufactured after delivery.

Cultural cognition: the math bends

The expert who becomes a quack

Dan Kahan’s Robert Linden study: subjects meet a distinguished professor of meteorology at MIT, Harvard doctorate, member of the American Meteorological Society and the National Academy of Sciences. Everyone agrees: expert on global warming. Then half hear Linden concluded warming is real and human-caused. Half hear he concluded it is not.

Republicans who heard the pro-warming conclusion withdrew the expert title: clearly a quack. Democrats who heard the anti-warming conclusion did the same. The credentials never changed. Only the fit with the tribe changed. Expertise is a badge the tribe issues and revokes.

Motivated arithmetic

Kahan’s gun-control math study: subjects interpret identical numerical results, first labeled as a skin-cream effectiveness trial, then as a gun-control effectiveness trial. On skin cream, better math skills meant better interpretation for everyone. On gun control, better math skills meant worse interpretation, but only when the numbers opposed the subject’s party. Republicans good at math became bad at math when the numbers favored gun control. Democrats good at math became bad at math when the numbers opposed it. Nobody knew they did it. Everyone felt they tried their best.

Kahan’s summary of the mechanism: on identity-fused issues, the individual interest in conforming to the group’s view dominates by far the person’s interest in getting the right answer. His cost-benefit explanation: your climate belief changes nothing about the climate, but getting it wrong in front of your tribe can cost you your standing, your job, your relationships. The incentives point at belonging. The math follows.

Converging on facts, diverging on meaning

Two cases that prove facts are not the bottleneck. During the Iraq war, Brian Gaines tracked Democrats’ and Republicans’ factual beliefs: both sides updated with evidence, converging on what was true (no WMDs found, casualty counts). But interpretations diverged completely: Republicans inferred the weapons were moved. Democrats, that they never existed. Same facts, opposite meanings.

The 1950s original: Dartmouth and Princeton students watched a film of their violent football game. Ninety percent of Dartmouth students said Princeton started it. Princeton students marked half as many infractions as Dartmouth did. Same film, same events, two realities. Memory was not the issue. The viewing happened minutes before. Construction was.

The 2017 version: the Comey testimony. Both parties watched the same hearing. Republicans heard exoneration. Democrats heard condemnation. A Reddit user summarized: one, Republicans think it exonerates. Two, Democrats think it condemns. Three, politics makes me want to shoot myself in the face with a bazooka.

The information deficit model is dead

The 19th-century dream

Definition: the information deficit model is the belief that people are wrong because they lack facts, so supplying facts fixes them.

The episode buries it with history. Nineteenth-century rationalists thought public education would end superstition. Benjamin Franklin thought public libraries would make the common man the aristocrat’s equal. Timothy Leary thought the internet would remove information gatekeepers. Every generation’s version: give everyone the same facts and agreement follows. It has never worked, and the failure has never dented the faith that next time it will.

The model fails because of episode 2: it assumes anyone who reads what you read will interpret it as you do. That assumption is naive realism wearing a lab coat.

Post-truth and the word of the year

Late 2016: Oxford named “post-truth” the word of the year after a 2,000 percent usage spike around Brexit and the US election. The Washington Post lamented that truth was dead. Kahan’s dry response: he would like to know when this supposed truth era ran. The dread is real. The golden age is invented. We were always motivated reasoners. The Comey hearing did not create the split. It revealed it.

The HPV story: how issues get polluted

Two vaccines, two fates

Kahan’s case study in issue pollution: the HPV vaccine versus the HBV (hepatitis B) vaccine. Both prevent sexually transmitted infections. Both are given similarly. The numbers for HPV: up to 75 percent of sexually active people in their twenties and early thirties encountered it. It is essentially the sole cause of cervical cancer, which kills about 3,000 US women a year.

The HBV vaccine: 95 percent acceptance, no controversy. People learned about it from their doctors. It never entered politics. The HPV vaccine: the manufacturer sought early approval and mandatory status, which meant congressional debate and state-legislature fights. The public met the vaccine through MSNBC and Fox News segments framed as moral combat. Overnight it became us-versus-them: whose side are you on. Same medicine type, opposite fates. The difference was the track it traveled into public awareness.

The lesson

Kahan’s prescription: depoliticize evidence-based issues before they go public, because any issue can be polluted. Dark matter, volcanoes, net neutrality, self-driving cars: all are pollutable. Once the tribe claims an issue, “we will choose to be wrong if it keeps us in good standing with our cultural peers,” and 98 percent scientific consensus cannot move it.

His tactical advice for the already-polluted: share only sources the other tribe trusts. A link from their enemy’s outlet is rejected on sight, whatever it says. Frame the goal as restoring a working science-communication environment, not converting Group X. “I am the problem. You are the problem. We all do this.” Treating one side as the patient guarantees the treatment fails, because they watch you do it.

What helps

Science curiosity

Kahan’s hopeful finding: people high in science curiosity do not show the perverse pattern. They do not grow more polarized as their comprehension rises. They find science genuinely interesting, and that interest survives tribal pressure. His proposal: find the curious in every group and let them carry the message in the tribe’s own language. People trust “people like them” who find the science convincing far more than they trust Bill Nye, whose contempt-signaling marks the issue as tribal territory.

Admit the bias out loud

Mason’s prescription: stop treating “you are biased” as an insult. It is natural psychology, present in every mind. The research on prejudice shows awareness plus active resistance works: keep the knowledge that the bias exists in the front of your mind, and in each interaction, deliberately humanize the opponent. Her concrete drill: when you see the outgroup member, remind yourself the reaction is automatic party bias, then attach a humanizing detail: a family, a favorite recipe, roller skating. Fight it in the open, every time.

The fast-food analogy

McRaney’s closing image: fast food lowered the cost of satisfying hunger and we grew fat. Then we figured it out. Social media lowered the cost of tribal signaling and we drift apart. The drive is not going away. The environment around it can be redesigned. “We can figure this out too.”

Questions and answers

What is the minimal group paradigm, and why does the $4 versus $3 choice matter?

Tajfel sorted strangers by coin toss (dot overestimators versus underestimators) and let them split money between the groups. Subjects chose $4 for their group and $3 for the other over $5 for everyone. They paid a dollar of their group’s welfare to widen the gap. It matters because it proves favoritism needs no history, no grievance, no real difference: the label alone is enough.

Follow-up: What is Brewer's explanation? Humans need inclusion and exclusion both. A group with no boundary is not a group. Status flows from the group to the self, so group conflict is self-esteem defense.
What is affective polarization, and how is it different from disagreeing?

The old polarization measured how far apart policy positions sit. Affective polarization measures whether partisans like and can tolerate each other. Mason’s point: the two are separable. People can agree on substance and still discriminate on identity. The anger data prove it: threats to the party mobilize more than threats to cherished policies.

Follow-up: What are the Pew numbers? 92 percent of Republicans right of the median Democrat, 94 percent of Democrats left of the median Republican. 20 percent hardcore refusers. 27 versus 36 percent seeing the other party as a national threat.
How did Cohen's welfare experiment work?

Subjects got a welfare position with the party label experimentally flipped. Positions moved with the label, 100 percent across the spectrum. Asked why, subjects invented reasons. None said “because my party does.” The position was rented. The justification was manufactured on delivery.

Follow-up: What does this imply about policy debates? Much of what sounds like policy reasoning is post-hoc narration of a tribal rental. Change the label and the "principles" follow. Argue the label and you argue nothing.
Walk me through Kahan's Linden experiment.

A distinguished MIT meteorology professor is introduced. Everyone grants expert status. Half hear he affirms human-caused warming, half hear he denies it. Each side revokes the expert title exactly when his conclusion mismatches their tribe. Credentials constant, verdict flipped. Expertise is a badge the tribe issues.

Follow-up: And the gun-control math? Identical numbers: as skin-cream data, math skill helps everyone. As gun-control data, math skill hurts whoever's party the numbers oppose. Skill does not protect against bending. It improves the bending.
What do the Iraq war and the football film prove together?

That facts are not the bottleneck. On Iraq, both parties converged on the facts (no WMDs, casualty numbers) and diverged on what they meant. On the football film, watched minutes earlier, 90 percent of Dartmouth blamed Princeton while Princeton saw half the infractions. Same inputs, different realities, with memory ruled out. Interpretation is where the tribe lives.

Follow-up: Why does this kill the information deficit model? The model predicts convergence on shared facts. The cases show convergence on facts with divergence on meaning. More facts feed the divergence.
What is the HPV versus HBV lesson?

Two similar vaccines, two fates. HBV: introduced by doctors, 95 percent acceptance, no politics. HPV: introduced via Congress and cable-news moral combat, became a tribal badge. The substance was equivalent. The track into public awareness decided. Kahan’s rule: depoliticize before going public, because anything can be polluted.

Follow-up: What is the tactical advice for already-polluted issues? Share only sources the other tribe trusts. Frame the goal as fixing the communication environment, not converting them. "I am the problem. You are the problem. We all do this."
What actually helps, per Mason and Kahan?

Three things. Science curiosity: the curious do not polarize with comprehension. Recruit them as in-tribe messengers. Bias admission: treat “you are biased” as meteorology, not insult, and actively humanize opponents per interaction. Environment design: fast food taught us drives can be managed. Redesign the channels that make tribal signaling cheap.

Follow-up: Why does Bill Nye fail as a messenger? His contempt-signaling marks the issue as tribal territory. The audience processes the signal (this is a badge issue. Dissent is dangerous), not the content. The messenger is the message's enemy.
Apply this to a technical organization splitting into camps (e.g., two frameworks).

Diagnose first: is the split about the frameworks or the teams? Run Mason’s test: threaten the framework’s merits versus threaten the team’s status, and watch which angers more. If it is the team, stop benchmarking (information deficit model) and start depoliticizing: shared demos, mixed-team ownership, curiosity-led evaluation. Never make one camp the patient to be fixed.

Follow-up: What is the Cohen risk in your org? Positions rented from team identity with invented justifications. Flip the advocacy assignment experimentally: have each camp argue the other's framework. What survives the flip is real. What evaporates was rental.

Memory aids

  • The machine: any label makes a tribe. The tribe immediately favors us over them, even at a cost.
  • The $4 versus $3: people pay to widen the gap. Ingroup edge beats total welfare.
  • Affective polarization: dislike without disagreement. The anger is about the party, not the policies.
  • The rental: positions follow labels 100 percent. Reasons are manufactured after.
  • The bending math: skill serves the tribe. Better math, better bending.
  • Same facts, different meanings: Iraq, the football film, Comey. Interpretation is the tribe’s home.
  • The deficit model is dead: 19th-century dream, never worked, still believed.
  • The track decides: HBV 95 percent via doctors. HPV tribal via cable news. Depoliticize before launch.
  • Never-confuse pair: disagreement versus dislike. You can fix disagreement with evidence. Dislike needs a different toolkit: contact, curiosity, humanizing. Diagnose before prescribing.
  • Never-confuse pair: the position versus the badge. A position can be argued. A badge can only be worn or removed, and removal costs standing. Check which one you debate.
  • Trap card: “More facts will fix polarization.” Iraq converged on facts and diverged on meaning. Facts are not the bottleneck.
  • Trap card: “Smarter people are less tribal.” Kahan’s math study: the smarter are better at bending. Skill is the tribe’s instrument, not its antidote.
  • Trap card: “The other side is the problem.” Kahan: “I am the problem. You are the problem. We all do this.” Treating one side as the patient guarantees failure.

How to imbibe this

  • This week: run Mason’s drill on one opponent (political, technical, familial). When the automatic dislike fires, label it: “party bias, automatic.” Then attach one humanizing detail: family, recipe, hobby. Do it every time for a week. Log the temperature change.
  • Catch yourself: the next time you feel certain about a contested factual claim, ask Kahan’s question: would I still believe this if my tribe believed the opposite? Write the honest answer. If the answer is no, the belief is a badge.
  • Behavior change per major idea:
  • Minimal groups: notice the labels forming tribes around you this week (teams, tools, diets). Name three. Watch the favoritism start.
  • Affective polarization: separate your dislikes from your disagreements. List five opponents. Mark which you dislike versus which you merely disagree with. Different toolkits for each.
  • Rentals: pick one strong policy or technical position. Argue the opposite side in writing for 20 minutes. What survives is owned. What evaporates was rented.
  • Bending math: on your next data dispute, check whether your skill serves the conclusion or the question. Ask: what would these numbers say to a neutral?
  • Depoliticize: before your next proposal goes public, route it through a trusted member of the skeptical camp first. Their fingerprints on it are the inoculation.
  • Monthly: audit one belief for badge status. The test: does changing it cost you standing anywhere? If yes, it is a badge. Handle with the badge toolkit, not the evidence toolkit.

Think it yourself

Do these with your own brain before touching AI. Write answers by hand.

  1. Guided: in the $4/$3 versus $5/$5 choice, compute what each group sacrifices and gains relative to the even split. Then state in one sentence what the choice proves about human preferences. (Answer: your group sacrifices $1, the other sacrifices $2. The gap goes from $0 to $1 in your favor at a net cost of $3 total welfare. It proves relative standing beats absolute welfare, even for strangers sorted by coin toss.)
  2. Fermi: 20 percent of the public are hardcore refusers, and 27 to 36 percent see the other party as a threat. In a town of 50,000 adults, roughly how many hardcore refusers is that, and how many threat-perceivers? (Answer: about 10,000 refusers. About 13,500 to 18,000 threat-perceivers. Now ask: how many does it take to poison a school board meeting? Far fewer than the totals.)
  3. Journal: “My badges.” List five beliefs you hold. For each, answer: would changing this cost me standing with anyone? Mark the badges. For the worst badge, write what the evidence alone would say.
  4. Argue both sides: “Affective polarization is just disagreement plus heat” versus “it is a separate thing that needs no disagreement.” Use Mason’s party-versus-policy experiment for the second side. What experiment would settle it for you?
  5. Observe: for one week, in every group you are part of, note one us-versus-them moment: the label, the favoritism, the cost. Tally them. Which of your groups runs the hottest minimal-group machine?
  6. First principles: derive the HPV outcome from the tribal machinery alone. Two identical vaccines, two introduction tracks. Predict the fates before checking against the episode. Then state Kahan’s depoliticization rule as a general design principle for any contested rollout, technical or political.

Skills you can now use

  • Spot minimal-group formation in real time and name the label.
  • Separate dislike from disagreement and choose the right toolkit.
  • Detect rented positions: flip the label mentally and watch what moves.
  • Catch your own math bending: ask what the numbers say to a neutral.
  • Depoliticize a rollout: route through the skeptical camp first.
  • Share only tribe-trusted sources across a divide.
  • Run Mason’s humanizing drill on live opponents.
