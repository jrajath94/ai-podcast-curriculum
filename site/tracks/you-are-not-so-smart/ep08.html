Episode 8: QAnon and Conspiratorial Narratives with Joseph Uscinski and Annie Sternisko

2021-07-25 · Instructor: Joseph Uscinski, Annie Sternisko · You Are Not So Smart

Why this episode matters

Every previous chapter studied the individual mind. This one studies what happens when the minds from those chapters find each other. The episode’s founding move is a redirect: stop cataloging what conspiracy believers believe. The beliefs are the least interesting part. Study why they believe, and why this theory and not another. The answer is a funnel, a set of motives, and a community machine. No other episode gives you the measured truth about QAnon underneath the media mirage.

The redirect: believers, not beliefs

The least interesting thing

Uscinski’s complaint: public discussion fixates on the conspiracy theory’s content, the catalog of claims, the supposed evidence. That focus puts the spotlight on the theory and takes it off the believer. But the content is, in his phrase, the least interesting and least informative thing about conspiratorial thinking. The psychology that leads a person to entertain these claims is the same across flat earth, moon hoaxes, and QAnon. Study the psychology and you understand all of them. Study the claims and you understand none of them.

McRaney’s framing for the whole show applies: “you are not so smart” means everyone, including the host. Pattern-seeking in noise, order in chaos, meaning in ambiguity: these are how brains make sense of the world, not defects of the foolish.

Narratives, not theories

Sternisko, following German researchers she admires, prefers the term conspiracy narrative over conspiracy theory. A theory suggests evidence and falsifiability. A narrative is cherry-picked, usually unfalsifiable, and absorbs counterevidence by integrating it. That is why the episode leans on movie metaphors throughout: choosing a conspiracy resembles choosing a film. You will see why below.

The three-question test

Uscinski’s instrument for measuring conspiratorial thinking is three statements, each rated 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree):

The three questions, each rated 1 to 7: one, much of our lives runs on plots hatched in secret places. Two, even in a democracy, only a few people run things anyway. Three, the people who really run the country stay unknown to the voters.

Most people score in the middle. They do not strongly endorse all three, but under the right conditions most are prone to conspiratorial thinking. A small part of every population scores high on all three. They exist in every town, institution, and neighborhood.

McRaney’s Dungeons and Dragons analogy: before the internet, a D&D fan in a small town might never find enough players. After the internet, finding your people became the default activity. High scorers on the three-question test can now find each other, gather in groups where everyone strongly agrees, and form communities around the shared inclination, whatever the specific beliefs turn out to be.

The hourglass

The funnel

Sternisko’s model, from her paper with Alexandra Chichoka and Jay Van Bavel, pictures the process as an hourglass. The fat top holds everything a person brings: attitudes, beliefs, values, anxieties, the whole psychological profile from nature and nurture. The bottleneck is one conspiracy theory. The fat bottom is what comes out the other side: new beliefs, attitudes, and values absorbed from inside the conspiracy, which become markers of the new group identity.

The fandom analogy: someone inclined toward spaceship movies might land on Alien or Star Wars and join that fandom. Once inside, they pick up the fandom’s shared attitudes, costumes, and lore. A different person might enter through horror and land on Alien or The Exorcist. Same destination, different doors. Inside, everyone starts sharing things they did not share before. Conspiracy communities work the same way: the entry reasons differ, the exit identity converges.

Content versus quality

Sternisko splits every conspiracy’s appeal into content and quality, like films split into plot and genre. The content is the narrative: topics, characters, setting, conflicts. The quality is what being a fan signals: the community, the shared reality, the fandom itself.

Two motives then select among theories. The social identity motive draws people to the content: the theory makes your group look good and the other group look bad. A strongly identified partisan loves any narrative where the opposite party is nefarious. The takeaway, not the plot, is the appeal. The uniqueness motive draws people to the quality: the theory is contrarian, non-normative, a way to feel special, to be in the know, not a sheep. For these believers it hardly matters whether the theory is lizard people or flat earth. What matters is that it makes them stand out.

Under both sit three base motives every conspiracy serves: feeling good about yourself and your groups, making sense of the environment, and feeling safe and in control.

Why the machine resists debunking

The logic loop

Two features make conspiracy narratives nearly debunk-proof. One is the conspiratorial logic loop: counterevidence gets reinterpreted as part of the cover-up, which makes it evidence for the conspiracy. Missing evidence gets the same treatment: the absence of proof is proof of how deep the cover-up goes. Every input becomes support.

The other is the absence of peer review. Dozens of internal contradictions can coexist because no mechanism rewards consistency or punishes incoherence. There is a standing assumption that the real evidence sits behind a curtain, so there is always more work to do, and the work never converges.

Collective sensemaking and information voids

The episode grounds the loop in normal psychology. Crisis researcher Kate Starbird’s information voids: when people sense missing information that could matter, they are compelled to fill the void. Under high uncertainty plus high anxiety, groups do collective sensemaking: trading rumors, updating the shared picture, converging on a workable reality. That machinery saves lives in a flood. Fed with rumors in an uncertain information environment, it manufactures shared delusion with the same machinery.

The distrust version: once accepting an outlet’s reporting would create cognitive dissonance, the outlet stops looking objective. The person shops for sources that confirm instead, and when those betray them, descends further into the fringe. QAnon becomes the last stop: a place where every day brings confirmation that the fears were justified. Anyone trusting the abandoned sources looks like part of the conspiracy.

QAnon, measured

What it is not

Uscinski’s research delivers three corrections to the media picture. One: QAnon is not growing. His feeling-thermometer polling in Florida scored QAnon at 24 out of 100 in 2018, two points above Fidel Castro at 22, in a state where that is no endorsement. National follow-ups through the next years showed no increase. Other polls put believers at 5 to 7 percent at most, on par with the fringiest theories polled.

Two: it is not a far-right conspiracy. Partisanship and ideology drop out of the models. The predictors that survive are conspiratorial thinking, the dark triad personality traits (narcissism, Machiavellianism, psychopathy), and deep antipathy toward political institutions. Supporters liked Trump not for conservative ideology but as an anti-establishment outsider who felt like one of them.

Three: it is not big. Online magnitude is not real magnitude. The hashtag #QAnon matched #MeToo in volume while changing nothing in society. Rallies drew 40 to 50 people. A “big” Washington rally was dozens in person. Tweets are not believers.

The bar metaphor

Uscinski’s summary image: QAnon is a bar. A certain kind of person likes hanging out there. If a ruckus breaks out, it is a mistake to say the bar made them crazy. The people were already like that. The bar collected them. The beliefs of QAnon, minus the anonymous poster named Q, existed for centuries: satanic panics, secret cabals, blood libels. QAnon invented nothing. It gathered the already-convinced.

McRaney’s numbers on harm, from the Center for Strategic and International Studies: 22 violent incidents tied to QAnon believers in three years, none classified as terrorism, 3 fatal. In the same three years, far-right extremists not tied to QAnon committed 139 violent acts, religious attackers 34, far-left attackers 37. The FBI’s 2019 bulletin classified QAnon as a potential domestic-terrorism threat while noting most believers never act. The threat is real and, in the comparative data, small.

Why the mirage

If the numbers are small, why does it feel enormous? Uscinski’s answer is attention, not growth. His Google alert for “conspiracy theory” went from about 5 articles a day a decade ago to 50 to 100 a day. The New York Times went from about 50 conspiracy-theory articles in a year to 750 in 2020. Journalists declared a golden age of conspiracy theories every year for decades, without evidence. Belief levels, tracked across historical surveys, show no increase. What changed is visibility: the water-cooler conversation vanished into air, while the tweet persists forever. We confuse seeing it with there being more of it.

And the genuinely new thing is speed. The internet did not make conspiracy communities more likely. It lowered the barriers to community formation, so anything, including a conspiracy fandom, can gather faster. The beliefs are old. The assembly line is new.

The 45 percent

One more number that reframes everything: about 45 percent of Americans endorse some form of Kennedy-assassination conspiracy. But half of them cannot say who did it. They were not compelled by a narrative. They hold a free-floating preference for conspiracy-shaped explanations. The theory is interchangeable. The inclination is the constant.

What to do

Meet the needs, not the claims

Sternisko’s prevention agenda starts upstream of any specific theory. People turn to these communities for unmet needs: belonging, validation, control, a comprehensible world. A society that leaves people feeling left behind manufactures the demand that conspiracy communities supply. Arguing the claims treats the symptom.

Her communication advice: condescension backfires. Ridiculing the cranky uncle severs another real-world tie and pushes him deeper into the community that welcomes him. Empathetic dialogue, hearing the person out while disagreeing completely, keeps the tie alive. She admits this is brutally hard in practice and sometimes unrealistic. It is still the only individual-level move the research supports.

Uscinski’s media advice is parallel: stop feeding the mirage. A whole journalism beat now hunts the weirdest claim in any chatroom and publishes it, giving the claim more attention than its dozen believers ever could. Coverage is not neutral. It recruits.

Communicate complexity honestly

The covid-19 lesson: scientists updated daily, guidance contradicted itself, retractions happened in public. Confusion is a recruiting sergeant for simple narratives. Sternisko’s prescription is better communication of complex, changing matters, so people do not have to choose between bewilderment and a story that explains everything.

Questions and answers

Why does the episode insist on studying believers instead of beliefs?

Because the content is the least informative part. The same psychology, pattern-seeking, agency detection, motive-driven selection, runs under flat earth, moon hoaxes, and QAnon. The beliefs are interchangeable: half of JFK-conspiracy believers cannot name the conspirator. Study the inclination and you understand all of them. Study the claims and you understand none.

Follow-up: What is a conspiracy narrative, in Sternisko's preferred term? Not a theory but a story: cherry-picked, unfalsifiable, absorbing counterevidence. The movie metaphor is deliberate. You choose it the way you choose a film.
What is the three-question test, and what does it measure?

Three statements rated 1 to 7: much of life is run by secret plots. Only a few people run things despite democracy. The real rulers are unknown to voters. Most people land in the middle: not committed, but prone under the right conditions. A small slice of every community scores high on all three. It measures the inclination, not any specific belief.

Follow-up: What is the D&D analogy? Before the internet, the high scorer in a small town stayed isolated. Now high scorers find each other and form the community first, with the specific beliefs negotiable afterward.
Explain the hourglass model.

Fat top: the person’s full profile of attitudes, values, anxieties. Bottleneck: one conspiracy theory. Fat bottom: the new group identity and shared beliefs absorbed inside. Entry doors differ, Alien through spaceships or through horror. The exit identity converges, costumes and lore shared. The model predicts which theory: the one whose motives match the person’s.

Follow-up: What are the three base motives underneath? Feeling good about yourself and your groups, making sense of the environment, feeling safe and in control. Every conspiracy narrative promises all three.
What is the difference between the content motive and the quality motive?

The social identity motive selects on content: the narrative makes my group look good and the other look bad. The uniqueness motive selects on quality: the theory is contrarian and makes me special, not a sheep. The first cares which theory. The second barely cares, lizard people or flat earth, as long as it stands out.

Follow-up: How do you diagnose which motive drives someone? Ask what they defend when challenged: the specific claims (content) or their specialness for seeing through it all (quality). The defense reveals the motive.
What makes conspiracy narratives nearly debunk-proof?

Two mechanisms. The logic loop: counterevidence is reinterpreted as cover-up, missing evidence as hidden proof, so every input supports the theory. And no peer review: contradictions coexist because nothing rewards consistency. Add collective sensemaking under uncertainty and anxiety, and groups fill information voids with shared stories that feel like convergence.

Follow-up: Why does debunking sometimes backfire? In a distrust community, the debunker is read as part of the conspiracy. The correction arrives through a distrusted channel, so the loop absorbs it as more cover-up.
What does the data actually say about QAnon? Give the numbers.

Not growing: feeling thermometer 24 of 100 in Florida 2018, no national increase since. 5 to 7 percent believers at most. Not far-right: ideology drops out of models. Dark triad traits and institutional antipathy predict instead. Not big: rallies of 40 to 50. Hashtag volume without societal impact. Harm: 22 violent incidents in three years versus 139 far-right non-QAnon in the same window.

Follow-up: Then why does it feel enormous? Attention, not growth: conspiracy-theory news volume exploded while belief levels stayed flat. And speed: the internet lowered community-formation barriers for everything. Old beliefs, new assembly line.
What is the bar metaphor, and why does it matter?

QAnon is a bar where a certain kind of person hangs out. The bar does not make them crazy. It collects them. The beliefs predated QAnon by centuries. Blaming the bar mistakes selection for causation, and it suggests the wrong fix: closing one bar just moves the regulars to another.

Follow-up: What is the right fix under this metaphor? Reduce the demand for the bar: belonging, validation, control supplied elsewhere. And stop advertising the bar: coverage recruits.
What should you actually do about a believer in your life?

Sternisko’s prescription: empathetic dialogue, not contempt. Ridicule severs the real-world tie and deepens the community’s hold. Hear them out while disagreeing completely. Upstream: meet the needs the community supplies, and communicate complex changing matters honestly so bewilderment does not recruit.

Follow-up: What is the honest limit? She admits empathy is brutally hard and sometimes unrealistic given the beliefs and the harms. The prescription is the best-supported move, not a guaranteed one. Some doors do not open.

  • The bar: selection, not causation. Close the bar, keep the regulars.

  • Never-confuse pair: the theory versus the community. Theories are interchangeable (half the JFK believers cannot name the plotter). Communities are the active ingredient: identity, rewards, costs. Fight the community’s supply, not the theory’s claims.
  • Never-confuse pair: growing versus visible. QAnon is not growing and not big. It is visible. Policy built on the mirage misfires.
  • Trap card: “Debunk the claims and the believers will leave.” The loop absorbs debunking as cover-up, and the believer’s motive was never the claims. Debunking treats the symptom.
  • Trap card: “It is a far-right conspiracy.” The models say otherwise: dark triad plus institutional antipathy, ideology washed out. The Trump attachment was outsider affinity, not conservatism.
  • Trap card: “The internet creates conspiracy believers.” It lowers community-formation barriers for everything. The believers and the beliefs predate it. Speed is new. The psychology is not.

How to imbibe this

  • This week: take the three-question test yourself, honestly, 1 to 7. Write which conditions would push your scores up. Those conditions are your vulnerability map.
  • Catch yourself: the next time a bizarre claim fascinates you, stop cataloging the claims and ask Sternisko’s question: what motive would this serve in me? Identity, uniqueness, control, or sense-making? Write the answer.
  • Behavior change per major idea:
  • Believers not beliefs: in your next encounter with a fringe claim, ban yourself from discussing the claims for ten minutes. Ask only about the believer’s world: what they fear, who they trust, what community they found.
  • Hourglass: map one community you belong to (fandom, profession, team) through the hourglass. What did you bring in? What identity came out? Notice the funnel worked on you too.
  • Content versus quality: for one belief you hold strongly, diagnose your motive honestly: do you defend the claims or your specialness for holding them? Write the verdict.
  • The loop: when you feel an urge to debunk someone, check the channel first. Are you a trusted source to them? If not, your debunking feeds the loop. Find a trusted channel or stand down.
  • The mirage: before reacting to a scary trend story, ask for the base rate: how many believers, growing or flat, online or in person? Demand the three numbers.
  • Monthly: re-take the three-question test. Note any drift and what conditions moved it. The inclination is measurable. Measure it.

Think it yourself

Do these with your own brain before touching AI. Write answers by hand.

  1. Guided: 45 percent endorse a JFK conspiracy, but half cannot name the conspirator. What does that split prove about content versus inclination? Compute the share holding a mere inclination. (Answer: about 22.5 percent hold the inclination without the narrative. The theory is interchangeable. The constant is the preference for conspiracy-shaped explanations.)
  2. Fermi: QAnon drew 40 to 50 people to rallies while its hashtag matched #MeToo in volume. Suppose hashtag volume overstates believers by a factor of 1,000. If #MeToo-scale volume implies millions of posts, how many real believers does the factor imply, and does that match the 5-to-7-percent poll figure? (Answer: millions of posts divided by 1,000 is thousands of believers, far below 5 percent of adults. The two measures disagree wildly, which is itself the finding: online volume is not a population measure. Now ask: what would a valid measure look like?)
  3. Journal: “My hourglass.” Pick a community you joined as an adult. Write the fat top (what you brought), the bottleneck (what pulled you in), and the fat bottom (what identity came out). Which of your current beliefs came from the bottom, not the top?
  4. Argue both sides: “Coverage recruits, so journalists should stop covering fringe theories” versus “sunlight disinfects, so coverage is the duty.” Use Uscinski’s beat-criticism for the first side and the FBI-bulletin public-safety case for the second. Where is the line between informing and advertising?
  5. Observe: for one week, note every claim you encounter that gets absorbed as cover-up rather than weighed. Tally the loop in the wild: politics, workplace gossip, product debates. Where does your own thinking run the loop?
  6. First principles: derive the “last stop” dynamic from dissonance alone. A believer meets counterevidence from a trusted source. The brain can update the belief or the trust. Show why repeated cycles end at the fringe, and predict what breaks the cycle. Then check your derivation against the episode’s account.

Skills you can now use

  • Administer the three-question test and read the inclination, not the claims.
  • Map any community through the hourglass: doors in, identity out.
  • Diagnose content motives versus quality motives in a believer.
  • Spot the logic loop: every input becomes support.
  • Demand the three numbers before reacting: how many, growing or flat, online or in person.
  • Choose the channel before the correction: trusted source or stand down.
  • Replace contempt with empathetic dialogue where the tie is worth keeping.
