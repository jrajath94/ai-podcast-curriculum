Tools multiply judgment

Nivi sets up the finale of the tweetstorm's skill sequence. They covered specific knowledge, accountability, and tools. The last skill is judgment, and Naval's tweet frames the relationship: tools are a force multiplier for your judgment.

The order matters. The first part of your career is spent hustling to obtain tools. Once you have them, the game changes completely, because the multiplier now applies to every decision you make.

Wisdom nugget 1

Source fact

Earn the multiplier, then respect it

What happened. Naval says tools multiply judgment, so the career splits into two phases: acquiring tools, then applying judgment.

Why it matters. Most people never switch phases. They keep hustling with a tanker as if it were a sailboat.

Principle. Hustle early for tools. Once tooled, slow down and think. Every decision now carries a payload.

00:02

From sailboat to tanker

We live, Naval says, in an age of nearly infinite tools, and all the great fortunes are created through them. You get tools with permission, by getting people to work for you or raising capital, or without permission, by learning to code or becoming a communicator who podcasts, broadcasts, writes, and makes video.

Then the image: your vessel grows from a sailboat to an ocean liner or a tanker. More at risk, more to gain, a much higher payload. In an age of nearly infinite tools, judgment becomes the most important skill.

In an age of infinite leverage, judgment becomes the most important skill.

Naval Ravikant, from the episode

From sailboat to tanker

The episode's central image: tools multiply your payload, so your steering matters more.

Wisdom nugget 2

Source fact

Slow down when the payload grows

What happened. Naval says the tooled-up operator must slow down because judgment now steers a tanker, not a sailboat.

Why it matters. Speed is a sailboat virtue. With a tanker, one bad turn is a catastrophe and one good turn is a fortune.

Principle. Match your decision speed to your payload. Big tools, slow steering.

00:04

The Buffett proof

Warren Buffett is the existence proof. He is wealthy because of his judgment. Take away all his money tomorrow, and investors would come out of the woodwork and hand him a $100 billion, because his judgment is that good, and they would give him a big chunk of it to invest.

Nobody asks Buffett how hard he works, when he wakes up, or when he sleeps. The instruction is simple: Warren, just do your thing. Demonstrated judgment with high accountability and a clear public track record is the scarcest asset in the market.

Wisdom nugget 3

Source fact

Build a public record of being right

What happened. Naval says Buffett's judgment is so credible that capital would flow back to him instantly even from zero.

Why it matters. Judgment you cannot demonstrate is a rumor. Judgment on the record is a magnet for infinite tools.

Principle. Make predictions in public, keep score honestly, and stay accountable. The record is the asset.

00:06

Why CEOs earn what they earn

The CEO-pay debate gets a cold answer. Yes, crony capitalism exists: CEOs capture boards and boards overpay. But some CEOs earned it, because their judgment is better. Steer Google or Apple with judgment 10 or 20 percent better than the next person's, and society will pay you hundreds of millions more, because you steer a $100 billion ship and the compounding of being right more often dwarfs your pay.

Demonstrated judgment plus credibility is the whole game. People throw infinite tools behind it.

Wisdom nugget 4

Source fact

Get paid for the delta

What happened. Naval defends high CEO pay for the few whose judgment is measurably better, because the compounding on a huge ship dwarfs the salary.

Why it matters. It reframes the career goal: not a bigger title, but a bigger demonstrable edge in judgment.

Principle. Aim to be 10 percent righter than the alternative on big decisions. On a big enough ship, that is worth a fortune.

00:08

Judgment is foresight about consequences

Nivi offers a definition: knowing the long-term effects of your decisions. Naval agrees and links it to wisdom: "Wisdom is just judgment on a personal domain." Wisdom applied to external problems is judgment. Both come down to "knowing the long term consequences of your actions" and then making the right decision to capitalize on that.

Everything else you do, he says, is setting you up to apply judgment. The skills are the runway. The decisions are the flight.

Wisdom nugget 5

Source fact

Think in decades, decide in minutes

What happened. Naval defines judgment as foresight about long-term consequences and equates wisdom with judgment turned inward.

Why it matters. Most decisions optimize the quarter. The edge lives in the decade.

Principle. For every big decision, write down the 10-year consequences first. If you cannot, you are not ready to decide.

00:10

Intellect without experience is worse than useless

Judgment is hard to build because it needs both intellect and experience. The ivory-tower intellectuals fail here: as Nassim Taleb argues, they have no skin in the game, so they apply pure intellect with borrowed confidence and throw darts. "Without experience, judgment is often less than useless."

The real world is far more complex than any model of it, especially at the fast-moving edge. The fix is iteration: not 10,000 hours, but 10,000 tries. Smart, fast, skin in the game.

10,000 tries beat 10,000 hours

Naval corrects the famous rule: hours logged do not build it. Iterations attempted do.

Source. Episode: you take 10,000 tries at something. The comparison reframes the 10,000-hour rule.

Wisdom nugget 6

Source fact

Earn judgment the expensive way

What happened. Naval says intellect without real-world accountability produces confident dart-throwing, and prescribes 10,000 tries instead of 10,000 hours.

Why it matters. Reading about risk is not risk. Only iterated exposure with consequences builds the pattern library.

Principle. Shrink the bet, multiply the tries. Keep skin in every game that teaches you.

00:12

The best judgment is the least emotional

The people with the best judgment are among the least emotional. The best investors look almost robotic. The passionate-entrepreneur archetype has truth in it, they must care, but they must also see clearly, and emotions cloud the seeing. In investing, companies, and products, emotions get in the way until reality breaks the fantasy and forces suffering.

Nivi connects the chain: you are accountable for your judgment, judgment is the exercise of wisdom, wisdom comes from experience, and experience accelerates through short iterations.

Wisdom nugget 7

Source fact

Cool the emotions, clear the lens

What happened. Naval argues that emotions prevent seeing what is actually happening, and that the best decision-makers look unemotional.

Why it matters. Every strong emotion is a tax on perception. You pay it before you decide.

Principle. Never decide at peak emotion. Wait for the flat state, then look again. The truth did not move. You did.

00:14

Top investors sound like philosophers

Read Jeremy Grantham, Warren Buffett, or Michael Burry and you find philosophers, or at least heavy readers of history and science. Investment books are the worst place to learn investing, Naval says, because investing is a real-world, multivariate, constantly-competed activity. What you need is broad-based judgment, and the way there is broad study of everything, including philosophy, which also makes you more stoic and less emotional.

Philosophy is not decoration here. It is exercise equipment for the judgment muscle.

Wisdom nugget 8

Source fact

Study everything, especially philosophy

What happened. Naval says the best investors read philosophy and history rather than investing manuals, because edge comes from broad judgment.

Why it matters. Domain manuals teach the current meta, which gets competed away. Broad judgment compounds across metas.

Principle. Read outside your field every week. Philosophy for stoicism, history for pattern, science for method.

00:16

Outrage is the tell

The closing heuristic is a weapon. The more outraged somebody is, the worse their judgment. Half of Twitter is outraged at something at all times. Read someone's feed and you can see what it is like inside their head.

The test is practical. You would not hand an enraged stranger your car keys. Do not hand them your company, your capital, or your attention either.

The more outraged somebody is, I guarantee you, the worse their judgment is.

Naval Ravikant, from the episode

Wisdom nugget 9

Source fact

Read the feed, judge the mind

What happened. Naval says chronic public outrage reliably signals bad judgment, and offers it as a filter for trust.

Why it matters. Outrage is cheap to fake and expensive to follow. The signal is free if you look.

Principle. Before trusting someone's judgment, scan their public emotions. Constant outrage is a disqualifier.
