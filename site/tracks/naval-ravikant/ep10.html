Episode 10: Judgment Is the Decisive Skill

2019-04-29 · Instructor: Naval Ravikant · Naval Ravikant

Naval on judgment as the force multiplier: definition, the Buffett case, and why the least emotional decide best.

Research metadata note: upload dates, video lengths, and view counts in this chapter come from research/naval-ravikant/episodes.json (October 2026 snapshot). They are YouTube metadata, not claims from the episode transcript.

Why this episode matters

This is the capstone of the wealth system and the finale of the track. Every earlier chapter built one input: specific knowledge, accountability, leverage. This episode names what those inputs are for. Leverage without judgment is a faster way to be wrong at scale. The episode’s claim is absolute: in an age of nearly infinite leverage, judgment becomes the most important skill. Everything else was setup.

The age of nearly infinite leverage

Get leverage first

The sequence matters. Your first job is to obtain leverage. Two ways:

  • Permissioned: get people to work for you, raise capital. Someone must say yes.
  • Permissionless: learn to code, become a good communicator, podcast, broadcast, write, make videos. No one must say yes.

The first half of a career is the hustle to get leverage. That is the setup phase. Everything in ep01 through ep09 lives here.

Then slow down

Once you have leverage, slow down, because now your judgment really matters. The episode’s image: you traded the sailboat for an ocean liner or a tanker. More at risk, more to gain, a much higher payload.

“Leverage is a force multiplier for your judgment.” A force multiplier does not care about the direction of the force. Good judgment, multiplied, builds empires. Bad judgment, multiplied, sinks them faster. That is why the order is leverage first, judgment second, and why the second half of a career looks calmer than the first.

What multiplies Effort Decisions

Judgment, defined

Knowing the long-term consequences

Nivi’s definition: judgment is knowledge of the long-term effects of your decisions, plus the ability to predict them.

Naval’s refinement: his definition of wisdom is knowledge of the long-term consequences of your actions. The two are barely different. Wisdom is judgment on a personal domain. Wisdom applied to external problems is judgment. Then make the right decision to capitalize on what you see.

Definition: judgment predicts the long-term consequences of actions and chooses correctly on the prediction.

Everything else is setup for judgment

The episode’s strongest claim: ultimately, everything else you do is setting you up to apply your judgment. Knowledge, accountability, leverage: all of it exists so that when the big decision arrives, you decide well.

The CEO pay case: people rail against CEO pay, and crony capitalism is real. But some CEOs earn it. Steer Google or Apple with judgment 10 or 20 percent better than the next person’s, and society will pay you hundreds of millions of dollars more. The compounding on a $100 billion ship dwarfs the salary. Being on course 10 or 20 percent more often, compounded over hundreds of billions, is worth more than any paycheck.

The hand-worked sense of it: 10 percent better judgment on $100 billion is $10 billion of value direction. A $200 million pay package is 2 percent of that. The pay looks obscene until you do the multiplication.

Demonstrated judgment plus accountability

The Buffett case: “Even if you were to take away all of Warren’s money, tomorrow, investors would come out of the woodwork and hand him a $100 billion because they know his judgment is so good.”

Why him: massive credibility, built from demonstrated judgment with high accountability and a clear track record. Right over and over in the public domain. A reputation for very high integrity, so you can trust him. People throw infinite leverage behind that combination. Nobody asks him how hard he works or when he wakes up. They say: Warren, do your thing.

The formula underneath: demonstrated judgment times accountability times integrity equals leverage offered freely. Miss any term and the product collapses.

How judgment is built

Intellect plus experience, and the Taleb warning

Judgment is very hard to build. It needs both intellect and experience.

The warning, via Nassim Taleb: intellectuals in the ivory tower have no skin in the game and no real-world experience. They apply pure intellect. Intellect without experience is often worse than useless: you get the confidence intellect gives and some of the credibility, but with no skin, no experience, no accountability, you throw darts.

The reason: the real world is far more complex than you can intellectualize, especially in fast-moving edge domains. You cannot think your way to judgment about things you have never touched.

10,000 tries, not 10,000 hours

The build method: if you are smart and you iterate fast, forget 10,000 hours. Count 10,000 tries. Smart, quick iterations, emotions kept out.

Hours repeat. Tries vary. Each try is a small experiment with feedback: a decision, an outcome, a correction. Ten thousand hours of the same motion builds muscle. Ten thousand tries across a domain builds judgment. This is the same iteration logic as ep01’s learning chapter, now aimed at decisions instead of skills.

Nivi’s chain, connecting the track’s concepts: you are accountable for your judgment. Judgment is the exercise of wisdom. Wisdom comes from experience. Experience accelerates through short iterations.

The least emotional decide best

The people with the best judgment are among the least emotional. The best investors look almost robotic. The best entrepreneurs, behind the passionate archetype, see clearly.

The mechanism: emotions cloud what is actually happening. You do not see the truth until it becomes undeniable, and then you suffer. Suffering, in the episode’s framing, is the breaking of the fantasy you had built. Every emotional decision is a fantasy with a bill attached.

This does not mean not caring. The archetype says they must care about the work. It means the caring does not get to edit the facts.

Top investors sound like philosophers

Read Jeremy Grantham, Warren Buffett, Michael Burry. They sound like philosophers, or are philosophers, or read history and science books constantly. They are not reading investment books.

The episode’s provocation: investment books are the worst place to learn investment. Investing is a real-world, highly multivariate activity. Every edge gets competed away. It lives on the cutting edge. What you need is very broad-based judgment, and the way to get it is to study everything, including a lot of philosophy.

Philosophy does double duty: it broadens the judgment base, and it makes you more stoic. More stoic means less emotional. Less emotional means better decisions. The chain closes.

The outrage test

The field diagnostic: “The more outraged somebody is, I guarantee you, the worse their judgment is.”

Scroll someone’s feed. Half of Twitter is outraged at something at all times. Read enough of one person’s outrage and you can see what it is like inside their head all the time. Do not hand that person the keys to your car, let alone the keys to your company.

The logic: outrage is emotion made public and permanent. If the least emotional decide best, the most publicly emotional decide worst. The feed is a judgment audit anyone can run.

Figure 3. The judgment pipeline. AI Podcast Curriculum, ep10 (Naval, 2019-04-29). Shell 3. Apply the one rule. Source: original toy, from episode claims.

Stage Input Output
1. Study everything History, science, philosophy Broad base
2. Iterate fast 10,000 tries with skin in the game Experience

The rule: each stage feeds the next. Skip the tries and you get dart-throwing intellect. Skip the stoicism and emotion edits the facts.

Questions and answers

Q1: What is judgment, exactly? Knowing the long-term consequences of your actions, then making the right decision on that knowledge. Wisdom is the same thing aimed at your personal life. Judgment is wisdom aimed at external problems.

Q2: Why does judgment matter more now than before? Because leverage is nearly infinite and nearly free. When one decision reaches millions through code and media, the quality of the decision dominates the quantity of effort. Leverage multiplies judgment, good or bad.

Q3: How is CEO pay connected to judgment? Ten to twenty percent better judgment on a $100 billion ship directs $10 to $20 billion of value. A few hundred million in pay is a small fraction of that. The market pays for the multiplication, not the hours.

Q4: Why would investors hand Buffett $100 billion with no money? Demonstrated judgment, public track record, high accountability, and a reputation for integrity. That combination makes leverage flow to him unsolicited. Nobody audits his calendar because the record already did.

Q5: Why is intellect without experience worse than useless? It gives confidence and credibility without the feedback that earns them. You throw darts while sounding certain. The real world, especially at the edges, is more complex than intellect can model from outside.

Q6: What is the difference between 10,000 hours and 10,000 tries? Hours repeat one motion. Tries run experiments with feedback. Judgment comes from varied attempts with skin in the game, corrected fast, not from time served.

Q7: How does philosophy improve investing? Two ways. It broadens the base: investing is multivariate and edges get competed away, so narrow study fails. And it builds stoicism: less emotion, clearer sight, better decisions.

Q8: How do I audit someone’s judgment quickly? Read their public outrage. The more outraged, the worse the judgment, guaranteed by the episode’s rule. Then check the pipeline: broad study, real tries, accountability on record.

Memory aids

Mnemonic: J = L x D x A. Judgment times Leverage times Demonstrated accountability. Or the vessel rule: sailboat first, tanker second.

Never-confuse pairs: - Judgment vs wisdom: external problems vs personal domain. Same muscle, different target. - Intellect vs judgment: intellect models, judgment decides with skin in the game. - Stoic vs unfeeling: stoic sees clearly, unfeeling does not care. Care, but do not let caring edit facts. - 10,000 hours vs 10,000 tries: repetition vs iteration. - Outrage vs passion: outrage clouds, passion fuels. One edits facts, the other moves hands.

Trap cards: - “I read the investment books, so I can invest.” The episode says those books are the worst place to learn. - “I am smart, so my judgment is good.” Intellect without tries is dart-throwing. - “Strong feelings mean strong conviction.” Strong feelings mean cloudy sight. The best look robotic. - “I need more leverage.” You need better judgment first, or leverage multiplies the error. - “Outrage shows I care.” It shows the opposite of judgment.

How to imbibe this

  • This week: run the outrage audit on yourself. Scroll your last 50 public posts or messages. Count the outraged ones. Each is a judgment downgrade made public.
  • Catch yourself: the next time you feel certain and emotional about a decision, stop. Write the long-term consequences in three horizons: 1 year, 5 years, 10 years. Decide only after writing.
  • Behavior change per major idea:
  • Tries: convert one repeated routine into an experiment. Change one variable, record the outcome. That is one try banked.
  • Broad study: add one non-field book to your reading: history, science, or philosophy. One chapter a week.
  • Stoicism: the next time reality breaks a fantasy, write what the fantasy edited out. That edit is the tuition.
  • Accountability: put your name on one judgment call this week, in writing, before the outcome. Start the track record.
  • Monthly: review your decisions log. Which calls were right? Which were emotional? Compute your hit rate honestly. That number is your judgment, measured.

Think it yourself

The finale drills: unaided reasoning from first principles. Do these with your own brain before touching AI. Write answers by hand.

  1. Reason: leverage is a force multiplier for judgment. Formalize it: if judgment quality is q and leverage is L, what is the sign of the outcome when q is wrong? At what L does a small error become catastrophic? Derive the case for slowing down after acquiring leverage.
  2. Reason: the episode claims the least emotional decide best. Steelman the opposite: when does emotion improve a decision? Under what conditions does the episode’s rule fail? Be precise about the boundary.
  3. Design: build your own 10,000-tries program for one domain you care about. Define a try, define feedback, define the correction loop. How many tries per week is sustainable? How long to 10,000 at that rate? What would you have to give up?
  4. Argue both sides: “Investment books are the worst place to learn investment.” Defend the books using the strongest case for structured knowledge. Then defend the episode. Which wins for a beginner versus a practitioner, and why the difference?
  5. First principles: Nivi’s chain runs accountability to judgment to wisdom to experience to iterations. Reverse it: start from iterations and derive each link upward until you reach accountability. Where is the weakest link in the chain, and what would break it?
  6. Synthesize the track: write the one-page operating system combining all ten episodes. Wealth, money, status. Specific knowledge. Accountability. Three leverages. Judgment. Luck. Desire. Happiness. No notes, from memory, then check against the chapters. What did you forget? That gap is your next month of practice.

Skills you can now use

  • Define judgment and wisdom and tell them apart by domain.
  • Run the CEO-pay multiplication: percent edge times ship size.
  • Price demonstrated judgment: track record plus accountability plus integrity.
  • Tell 10,000 tries from 10,000 hours and design the tries.
  • Audit judgment from public outrage in under five minutes.
  • Slow down on purpose after acquiring leverage.
  • Build the pipeline: broad study, fast tries, stoicism, accountable calls.
