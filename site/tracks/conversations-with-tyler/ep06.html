Episode 6: Jhumpa Lahiri on Translation, Blindness, and Belonging

2017-01-11 · Instructor: Jhumpa Lahiri · Conversations with Tyler

Tyler interviews Jhumpa Lahiri on language, translation, and identity.

Why this episode matters

This is the WATCHING GREAT MINDS chapter on language as fate. Lahiri’s production function is translation in the deepest sense: taping her mother reading Bengali she cannot read, writing novels in Italian she chose not to master, living as a simultaneous interpreter between worlds. Tyler traces one continuous problem across her whole career: how to belong without a native country, a native language, or a native culture. You learn the taped-translation method, the voluntary blindness, and why a book cover can feel like a trauma.

“Uncomfortably”: the Rhode Island problem

Tyler frames her: born in England, Rhode Island from age three. “I think of you as a Rhode Islander. How would you react to that?”

Lahiri: “Uncomfortably.” With all due respect. The arithmetic: age 3 to 18, fifteen years, then Barnard at 18. She lived as long in New York as in Rhode Island, but childhood is formative as later life is not. “I have always had a very uneasy relationship with the place.”

Naming it in The Lowland

She wrote the State by State anthology essay on Rhode Island partly to get over the discomfort. It broke the block: The Lowland is the first book where she names Rhode Island. The earlier books are set in a fake Rhode Island-slash-Massachusetts, “this terrain that really is Rhode Island,” but she kept calling it some suburb of Boston. She could describe the ocean, the small campus, the little town she knew perfectly, but she could not say the name. “She calls that fact revealing.”

The immigrant lens on everything

At 15 she read Russian literature: Dostoyevsky and Tolstoy in Norton Critical Editions spotted on a family friend’s older daughter’s desk. She loved Hawthorne even then. Tenth grade brought The Great Gatsby. The next year brought Hardy, who became central, Tess above all.

Against the category

Tyler’s observation: she reads The Scarlet Letter as an immigrant novel. The characters would not behave that way in a home country. Tess reads the same way: countryside to strange city. Her fiction rewards reading through that lens.

Lahiri agrees and widens it: she responds to displacement in almost any author. Willa Cather reads this way. Homer reads this way. Years ago the New York Times Book Review asked about her problem with immigrant literature, and she said she did not believe in it. Not cheeky: true. If literature could not cross those boundaries, those writers could not have fed her. “Literature does the opposite. It allows us to cross over those boundaries in a beautiful way, in a magical way.”

The taped translation

Tyler: the internet says three master’s degrees, creative writing, comparative literature, English, and for one degree she translated six Bengali short stories by Ashapoorna Devi, “maybe the most famous woman writer or even most famous writer in Bengal for much of the 20th century.” What connected her, and what problem was she solving?

The connection runs through her mother, a devoted reader of Ashapoorna Devi. Her mother read little in English and much in Bengali. Lahiri remembers the drama: the list given to her uncle, the booksellers on College Street in Kolkata, ordering from publishers, waiting, the rickshaw piled with books back to the United States. Those books “simply were not available here, and they were, in some sense, her lifeline.”

Ashapoorna Devi struck her as prolific, incisive on domestic life, and unfairly classified as a writer for women. “I do not think she is at all. I think she has a much more universal power and vision.”

The method

The method: in a graduate translation workshop she needed something to translate. She had Latin, some ancient Greek, some French, and Bengali as her first language. But she could not read Bengali script and still really cannot, and could not write it either. The workaround: she asked her mother to read stories aloud, taped her, played the tapes back and back, and translated from the sound. She could read the text painstakingly, slowly, enough to check structure and breaks. She even caught her mother skipping paragraphs and called to ask about the missing description.

Architecture as narrative

Tyler notices the architectural sense: Ashapoorna Devi sets scenes in space, describing the home before the action, imposing structure. Lahiri thinks the thesis commentary made that lens explicit. Her doctoral dissertation extended it: Jacobean English drama set in corrupt Italian palazzos, and what the setting means. As a student she was attuned to where things are set and why, “what it meant to have that literal architecture being an element of narrative.”

Palladio and the love-horror of Italy

At George Mason, Tyler notes Gunston Hall, the Palladian house, and asks about her Renaissance dissertation. What drew her to Palladianism?

Scruton’s seminar

Step one: a broad graduate seminar where Roger Scruton taught the language of architecture, Italian architecture in particular. Tyler mentions debating Scruton once on friendship. “I won.” The idea of reading space, the vocabulary implicit in architecture, struck her.

Step two: Florence. First trip to Italy. Touring the buildings she knew from slides as a Boston grad student, connecting image to space. That trip started the whole Italian phase of her life.

Shakespeare’s Italy

Step three: Shakespeare’s Italy. Why does Renaissance England set English political drama on foreign soil? What did Italy represent: symbol of corruption, object of desire? “A sort of love-horror, love-hate, attraction-repulsion, contradictory attitude.”

The through-line, she says, is translation as crossing over. Renaissance travelers saw Venetian and Florentine architecture, translated Vitruvius and Alberti, and built the ideas into English buildings, and from there into American ones.

Mr. Kapasi, the interpreter

Tyler reread Interpreter of Maladies and proposes: Mr. Kapasi, who interprets Gujarati symptoms for the doctor, is the author herself. The book’s people are disconnected in different ways, and she does the interpreting. He stands at the center as her stand-in.

The continuum

Lahiri: “Probably not, but I do not think I was aware of it at the time.” The pattern emerged in hindsight. In antiquity, the Latin word for translator is interpreter. She teaches translation now as “the most intimate form of reading.” There was a time when translating, interpreting, and analyzing were one activity. Now they are split into professions. The title came to her in “this strange flash.” Years later she begins a new phase as a translator. “It is all one continuum. But one cannot realize these things in the moment. It is only looking back that you see certain patterns.”

Ferrante, in hindsight

Tyler sees Ferrante in the book before she had read Ferrante: the need to set everything right for so many people, the inability, the sadness, and books everywhere, powerful and impotent at once. Lahiri recognized it when she read Ferrante and wrote her two public letters, read in Rome, about books as characters, about reading and language, about how books shape and form us “and how they betray us on the other hand and cannot really contain what life is.”

Is the later work darker, more Hardy? Yes. The vision has become a little less forgiving, less tolerant, a little less bittersweet and more just bitter. Her editor called The Lowland “really grim sometimes.” Lahiri’s reply: “Would you really have said that to Hardy?” The editor said nothing else. Readers miss the bittersweet Namesake quality. “That is fine.”

Kolkata: the city that believes in its poets

Tyler’s travel advice: anyone visiting India must go to Kolkata. Why?

Lahiri: “It is one of the most fascinating places on earth… with a life, a cultural life, a history utterly its own, and hard, and beautiful. Its beauty is not conventional.” She knows it only as family, never as a tourist: grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins. “It is like not knowing New York City in the American context. It is just its own thing, and it is so strong in its flavor, and its power, and its energy.”

The city that believes

Why do Sen and Banerjee, so much of India’s intelligentsia, come from Bengal? She hates sweeping generalizations. But: “It is a city that believes in its poets, that believes in its politics, believes in humanity in some sense. And life is so extreme there, in so many ways. People are put to the test.” Nothing can be taken for granted there. “It is a jolt to your consciousness, but a fundamental one, an essential one.”

On Kolkata’s lost centrality after the British: the other big cities have more Western overlay now. Kolkata changed radically since the 1970s, five-star hotels, companies, banks, fancy roads. The distance shrank. But it keeps its flavor, “because of this, maybe,” the marginality itself.

The 1905 wound

On the 1905 Curzon partition of Bengal: her relationship to Indian history has two categories. Formally she studied none of it, raised in America, “India just was not on any map anywhere ever.” But the family talked constantly, and she absorbed “these incredibly traumatic events,” above all Partition, and what it meant for her father’s family and neighborhood, described a little in The Lowland. “When Mr. Pirzada Came to Dine” grew from a vague memory of a Bangladeshi scholar in 1970s Rhode Island during the civil war.

Then the residue: people “deeply scarred,” “actively hostile, resentful,” toward East Bengal and Muslims. Her daughter is named Noor, a Persian name, and family members questioned the choice. “This kind of hatred, intolerance, prejudice… is still very much in my family’s kitchen, at their dinner table.” Her parents taught her to be free of it. Easier for her, she admits: she never lost ancestral property.

Tagore, the family member

Tagore: “if you are Bengali, he is like a member of your family in some sense.” A god in a pantheon-like place, yet not a god, a person. Her parents were not religious but taught respect for great minds. Her grandfather was a painter, her uncle was a painter. Portraits of Tagore covered the house. She keeps her uncle’s watercolor and a photograph her great-uncle took of Tagore’s last public speech beside her as she writes. “I feel this constant presence.”

The limitation

The limitation: she reads him mostly in translation, never fluent in Bengali. Fiction, poetry, and the songs, “24 hours a day, in my house.” No conscious influence she can name. “But again, what is conscious, what is unconscious?”

Lalla Romano: blindness as a point of view

In Other Words is Tyler’s favorite of her books: the memoir of learning Italian and writing in it, “a remarkably brave thing to do.” He names lesser-known Italian writers for her reactions.

Finding her

Lalla Romano: Lahiri had never heard of her until Rome, when a newspaper article caught her eye. The books were hard to find. Through the article’s author she reached Romano’s husband, who kept a warehouse of the books in the house. She wrote, he sent a packet from Milan, and it never arrived. “They are still on a train, somewhere,” says Tyler. Eventually in Milan she visited the house, saw the desk, the things, the paintings, and left with a cart full of books.

Romano: incredibly modernist, essential language. Nei mari estremi, a two-part memoir of losing her husband, written long before grief memoirs became a culture. Novels about homecomings to places that no longer exist.

Blindness as a point of view

At life’s end Romano was almost blind, yet the drive to write stayed intense. Her second husband described the final phase: enormous sheets of paper, a couple of words, a sentence or two a day, “burning with life.” The fragments became The Last Diary. One entry: “La mia quasi cecita = un punto di vista.” “My blindness is a point of view.”

Lahiri marvels: it explains her Italian project. Romano was partially blind, not totally. Lahiri is partially blind in Italian, not totally. Romano did not choose her condition and suffered it. Lahiri’s is voluntary. “But it is this kind of voluntary blindness, right?” Tyler: the partial blindness gives a new lens. Lahiri: “Well, it makes you look harder.”

Pavese and Kristof: the translator-writers

Pavese

Cesare Pavese: huge. He translated Moby-Dick. Sailing the Aeolian Islands, their skipper claimed Pavese’s translation surpasses Melville. Lahiri has not read it yet but wants to. Pavese: the colossal writer of Turin, the city of Primo Levi, Natalia Ginzburg, and Einaudi. Autobiographical before it was fashionable. Tragic life, suicide, a writer’s notebook “very, very dark, very, very true.” Slim novels, short stories, and relentless translation: many Americans, plus oversight of translation projects. Translating Melville gave his Italian “a different energy.”

What America lacks: fiction writers who translate. Poets do it, Pound, Merwin, Strand. Fiction writers rarely engage. Lahiri just finished translating her first novel from Italian, “such a formative experience,” deepening “my awareness of what words are.” “Someone like Pavese blazed the way.”

Kristof

Agota Kristof: Hungarian, fled the invasion for Switzerland with small kids, her husband, and her dictionaries. The Illiterate, “L’analphabete” in French, tells it. She taught herself French. All her literary work is in French. Lahiri read her in Italian translation: “my life was never the same after reading her.” Dark, human, forever altering. The difference: Kristof had an antagonistic relationship with French, learned under necessity in Switzerland. Lahiri chose Italian, “this crazy thing that everyone discourages me from doing.”

The clothing of books

The Clothing of Books is her essay on book covers. Tyler’s experiment: at Borders, ignore everything but the cover, buy the most appealing book on the front table. He bought Kate Christensen’s The Great Man. One trial, a success.

Lahiri’s essay is about the disconnect between outside and inside, and the anguish of it: “you cannot really disconnect yourself from what you look like.” Tyler finds he cannot unsee the Unaccustomed Earth cover, the floating tiara, even knowing she did not choose it. Lahiri points to her wrist: the cover was designed after her bracelet. Pretty, but “too pretty?”

On blurbs: she says what she feels in the essay, to some publishers’ discomfort. Writing in Italian freed her: “I do not pretend anymore. And I am not concerned about making everybody happy.” The hardest line: “Once the cover is on it, it is not my book.” In Italy, authors choose covers. “Imagine that.”

The uniform

Reclam’s plain German covers, color only, publisher-uniform: she loves them. The essay meditates on the uniform versus dressing yourself. She would choose “the safety of the uniform,” and the essay opens with the childhood trauma of choosing clothes. Economic ramifications, cultural ramifications: the $40 Nike sneakers her parents called a waste, the gap with schoolmates, “traumatizing.” She envied her Kolkata cousins’ school uniforms: “I dreamed of being able to wake up in the United States and just put on my blue skirt and my white shirt and my black shoes, and going to school, and nobody judging my clothes.”

The ambiguity tax

Tyler: a cover forces your hand on the identity question when you would rather hover in ambiguity. Lahiri: the uniform has “a protective quality to the lack of specificity.” America is Freedom with a capital F, and she felt its danger as a 1970s child of immigrants: “Where do you stand between wanting to express yourself and be free and being afraid of that freedom, being actually vulnerable to that freedom?”

Coda: music, Starnone, Ferrante, and the method

The music she came from

Indian classical music came from her mother: sitar above all, and the family’s sarod passion, Ali Akbar Khan and Amjad Ali Khan. At Princeton she heard raga-inspired miniatures on headphones: “That music is a part of me. It is part of my formation.”

The Ties translation

Next work: the English translation of Domenico Starnone’s Ties (Lacci), “the finest Italian living author” in her judgment, out in March, plus quiet short stories in Italian.

The Ferrante unmasking

On Ferrante’s unmasking: she personally knows the accused woman, Starnone’s wife. The article was “violent,” “inappropriate,” its language “disturbing and offensive.” Whoever wrote the books committed no crime. “The world is no better off knowing who the actual person is.” The cult of the signature is recent. Italy moved on. America cannot stop. “If someone goes out of their way to say ‘I would like to write in anonymity,’ why that cannot be respected in our culture is really kind of mystifying to me, and also distressing.”

Bilingual selves

On bilingual selves, from a Polish writer in the audience: “If you know more than one language, you have more than one soul.” Lahiri always lived divided, two languages side by side. Italian gives “the sense of freedom that a learned language might provide you to set to the side certain baggage.” She would never write In Other Words in English.

Gogol as Mediterranean

On Gogol passing as Mediterranean: not a conscious decision. It is about projection: “how we like to project sameness onto other people to make them more comfortable to us and to dilute the sense of difference.” She is mistaken for many things worldwide. “It is more based on the context and not on the person.”

Her parents

On her parents: she never sat them down for the immigrant interview. “I lived that experience… There was no moment where I was not aware.” Every relationship outside the family was informed by it. She was “like being a simultaneous interpreter all the time,” interpreting for herself, not for them.

Inhibition

On inhibition: “Writing always scares me and intimidates me… if I ever stopped feeling that, I probably should not write anymore.” Even in English she began with trepidation. The work is “an investigation, an experiment, a challenge,” questioned at every step.

The Exchange

The most autobiographical thing she ever wrote: “The Exchange,” her first story in Italian, “a very weird, abstract story,” lifted from a real experience. And the oracle for all of it: “if you really want to talk about this stuff and think about it, read Agota Kristof because she is a genius.”

The process

On process: she writes about loss, not nostalgia. “I go through a million drafts and I am constantly reworking everything.” The linear sentence-then-next-sentence method is “the opposite” of how she works.

Questions and answers

What is the taped-translation method, step by step?

One, the mother reads Ashapoorna Devi aloud in Bengali. Two, the daughter tapes her. Three, the daughter replays the tapes until the sound lives in her ear. Four, she translates from sound, checking the text painstakingly where she can. Five, she phones her mother about skipped paragraphs. The method turns illiteracy in the script into no obstacle at all, because the ear compensates.

Follow-up: What is the general principle? Translate from the strongest channel you have, not the official one. Sound beat script. What is your strongest channel, and what are you avoiding because the official channel is weak?
Explain "My blindness is a point of view."

Romano, nearly blind, wrote a sentence or two a day on enormous paper, burning with life. The limitation became the lens: partial sight forced harder looking. Lahiri borrows the structure voluntarily. Partial Italian forces harder looking at English problems she no longer saw. Blindness, full or partial, chosen or suffered, is not the absence of a view. It is a view.

Follow-up: What is the trap? Romanticizing the limitation. Romano suffered hers. The point is not that blindness is good. The point is that any stable limitation reorganizes attention, and you can choose yours.
Why does Lahiri distrust the phrase "immigrant literature"?

Because the displacement she responds to lives in Hawthorne, Hardy, Cather, and Homer too. Ghettoizing it as immigrant literature builds the barrier literature exists to dissolve. The immigrant lens is a way of reading, not a shelf in the bookstore. Apply it to Tess and the strangeness becomes sense.

Follow-up: Take one book you love. Read it through the displacement lens for ten pages. What changes? If nothing changes, ask whether you chose the wrong book or the wrong lens.
What is the difference between Kristof's French and Lahiri's Italian?

Necessity versus choice. Kristof fled with dictionaries and learned French antagonistically because a writer in Switzerland had to. Lahiri chose Italian against everyone’s discouragement because English carried baggage she wanted to set aside. Both prove the same thing: a learned language is a new soul, a new point of view. The motive changes the texture but not the mechanism.

Follow-up: Which of your skills was forced and which was chosen? Do they feel different from the inside? Should they?
Why does a book cover cause her anguish?

Because it ends the hovering. Lahiri’s whole identity is ambiguity: Rhode Island unnamed, Bengali unread, Italian chosen. A cover forces a statement about where she rests on the identity question. The uniform, Reclam plain, school blue skirt, protects by saying nothing. The costume, the floating tiara, says something she did not choose. “Once the cover is on it, it is not my book.”

Follow-up: Where in your life do you pay for other people's covers? Name one uniform you could adopt this month. What would its protective silence buy you?
What does Kolkata believe, in her account?

In its poets, its politics, and humanity. The city puts life to the test constantly, so nothing can be taken for granted. That extremity is a jolt to consciousness, fundamental and essential. It is why Sen and Banerjee are Bengali, in her non-sweeping account: the city does not let you protect yourself from reality.

Follow-up: What is your Kolkata? Name the place that will not let you take things for granted. When did you last go?
Why did she never interview her parents about immigration?

Because she was the interview, live, every day. There was no moment she was not aware of the distance between her parents’ world and the American one. Every friendship was formed in that awareness. Asking was redundant: she was a simultaneous interpreter full-time, interpreting for herself.

Follow-up: What are you living that you keep trying to research? Stop researching it. Start transcribing it.
What is her writing process, in her own terms?

Loss, not nostalgia, at the core. A million drafts, constant reworking, the opposite of linear. Trepidation as method: if the fear stops, stop writing. Every step questioned: what, how, why. Investigation, experiment, challenge, never “Sure, I can do that.”

Follow-up: Audit your process against hers. Which of the four do you skip: the drafts, the fear, the questioning, the reworking? Add the missing one this week.

How to imbibe this

  • This week: run the tape method on something you cannot read. Find a text in a script or jargon you do not command. Get a native speaker to read it aloud. Tape it. Translate from sound. Note what the ear catches that the eye misses.
  • Catch yourself: the next time you shelve a book by identity (“immigrant literature,” “women’s writing”), unshelve it. Read ten pages through the displacement lens. Note what the shelf hid.
  • Catch yourself: the next time a cover, a title, or a label forces your identity statement, ask the uniform question. What would protective silence buy here?
  • Behavior change per major idea:
  • Translation: pick one work problem and translate it into a foreign medium: a drawing, a recording, a different language. The medium shift is the interpretation.
  • Blindness: choose one voluntary limitation for a month. Write without adjectives. Read without reviews. Note what you look harder at.
  • Architecture: describe the room before the scene in your next three pieces of writing. Let space structure the action.
  • Drafts: multiply your drafts. If you write two, write six. The opposite of linear is the method.
  • Monthly: audit your languages. Which ones do you command, which command you? Add one hour a week to the one that would give you a new soul.

Think it yourself

Fermi and journaling with less scaffolding. No answers shown.

  1. Fermi: six stories translated from tapes. Estimate the hours: replay counts, checking, phone calls about skipped paragraphs. What is the cost per story? What does the number say about ear versus eye?
  2. Journal: write your Rhode Island. The place you can describe perfectly but cannot name. Name it, once, in one sentence. What shifts?
  3. Choose a voluntary blindness for one project. Define the limitation in one rule. Predict what you will look harder at. Then test the prediction.
  4. Take the uniform test: list every cover you wear, literal and professional. Which ones did you choose? Which were assigned? Price the assigned ones.
  5. Interview yourself as the simultaneous interpreter: transcribe one day of your own code-switching. Who are you interpreting for? What gets lost?
