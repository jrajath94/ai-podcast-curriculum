Episode 2: Camille Paglia on Cycles, Brazil, and the 24/7 Eye

2016-04-25 · Instructor: Camille Paglia · Conversations with Tyler

Video not loading? Watch on YouTube

Tyler interviews Camille Paglia on art, feminism, Brazil, music, and the life of a professional observer.

Why this episode matters

This is the WATCHING GREAT MINDS chapter on attention itself. Paglia’s whole production function is visible in one sentence: the job runs 24/7 and the eye never closes. Tyler spends the episode testing whether that eye sees clearly: on culture cycles, on Brazil, on Lucas, on the Stones, on feminism, on the pay gap. You learn a method of total observation, a theory of cultural cycles, and a dissident feminism built from Amelia Earhart instead of the 1960s.

The problem: who is Camille Paglia?

Tyler’s introduction credits her with the best essays ever on Edmund Spenser, Alice in Wonderland, and the Marquis de Sade, notes that she understands Bob Dylan and Susan Sontag, and praises a career of great integrity.

The episode then fills in the biography through stories. She did her graduate work at Yale, wrote a dissertation on androgyny in literature and art, spent 20 years getting Sexual Personae into print, taught for 40 years, and became famous at 43 without changing a thing about her life. Everything else in the episode is downstream of two facts: she never stopped looking, and she never stopped refusing.

The Bloom saga: 7, 5, 20, 43

The meeting

Tyler asks for her favorite Harold Bloom story. Her answer starts with a correction: she never took a course with Bloom. She was in graduate school at Yale and did not know him. He heard she had trouble finding a dissertation director for a study of androgyny in literature and art, a subject on which nobody wrote dissertations at the Yale Graduate School at the time. He summoned her to his office. “My dear, I am the only one who can direct that dissertation.” She said, “OK.” That was the whole meeting.

Definition: a dissertation director is the senior scholar who supervises a PhD student’s thesis. In the early 1970s, a dissertation on sex at Yale was professionally dangerous. Bloom understood everything she wanted to do and backed her through decades of rejection.

The numbers

Sexual Personae was rejected by seven publishers and five agents. By the time it appeared, Paglia was 43 years old. The book took 20 years. Her moral: “I am a great role model, it seems to me, for people to just soldier through adversity and rejection and just continue to develop the craft.”

The hand-worked ledger: 7 plus 5 is 12 professional rejections. Twenty years of work. One book. Age 43 at publication. The ratio of labor to recognition is the whole point. Rejection, she says, infuriates her rather than discouraging her. Her private vow is revenge in the afterlife.

Culture moves in cycles

The avalanche of fragments

Tyler opens with a reader question about Silicon Valley’s dominance and asks if any tech guru interests her. Her answer: her last big tech guru was probably Marshall McLuhan, with his prophetic insight into what lay ahead. She worked on the web from the first issue of Salon in 1995. A major political reporter at the Boston Globe once pressured her not to write for the web: “No one takes the web seriously.” Now, she says, “I think we are kind of on the downside of that right now.”

Tyler then asks about Glittering Images, her book on the iconic in Western and Eastern culture, and poses the hard question: if the old spectacular culture was so splendid, why did people give it up so quickly for tiny smartphone screens?

Paglia’s answer: she wrote the book because of “an avalanche of fragmented visual impressions, disconnected, glaring, tacky, badly designed” that young people grow up inside. She believes the avalanche reshapes children’s brains, and that the standard forms for logic, sequential information, and reasoning fade. She wrote the book so people would stare at one image for a length of time. Web design, which her school teaches, is “in the pits.” The miniaturization of the image is terrible.

Her contrast case: the 1950s, when Hollywood fought television with gigantic screens. The Ten Commandments, Lawrence of Arabia. “There is no sense of the large. Young people have no sense whatever of the expansive, of the big gesture.”

The cycle

Tyler presses: do we overrate the large? If it gave way so quickly, what was wrong with that culture to begin with?

Paglia: “I would say that a culture always moves in cycles.” Periods that esteem the colossal, like the Bernini Renaissance and Baroque, alternate with periods of the small. The Rococo is an evaporation of the big Baroque swirls into “little tiny things like on a Valentine’s card.” She feels lucky to have an epic imagination, raised on The Ten Commandments and Ben-Hur: “I could watch that 300 times.”

Brazil: nine visits

What Brazil has

Tyler: in the green room they discussed Brazil. She went there nine or ten times. What does Brazilian culture have that North American culture lacks?

Paglia: “It is such a polyglot of cultures and ethnicities.” Beyond that, Brazilians understood her work from the first moment she published. What they understood was artifice: Carnival, costuming, masquerade, baroque exuberance, and the syncretism of Christianity with the Yoruba cults of West Africa in Salvador de Bahia. They understood her vision of art and beauty as “an incredibly important human principle,” at a time when her fellow feminists trashed beauty.

They also understand nature, “the grandeur of nature, the power of nature. It is much larger.” Tyler mentions Iguacu. Paglia: anyone who talks about climate change ending the world “does not understand the grandeur and the power of nature. To imagine that we can make a change in it is so absolutely absurd.”

Tyler asks for her theory of modernity: what puts Brazil on one part of the curve and North America on a more decadent part? Paglia: “Brazil, it is in its own world. It is not been part of the world wars. It does not have this huge militaristic superstructure. It does not have a messianic view of itself politically. The politics are always chaos and drama. It is like in grand opera. It is like another planet, really, Brazil.”

The sexes in Brazil

Which country comes closest to healthy relations between the sexes? Paglia: “I would say that Brazil has the healthiest view of sexuality.” But among the upper middle class, the sexes are not exactly getting along. The women are magnificent: “They have such style, and assertiveness, and so on.” The men: “The men are like gnomes. It is strange.” In the United States, successful upper-middle-class men go to the gym. In Brazil, “the men just seem to sag and get plumper and plumper, and duller and duller, and lose their hair and nobody minds.” Her theory: “they assume that woman rules. Woman is the cock of the walk down there.” Her verdict: “I love Brazilian women. They are so bossy.”

Lucas and the prequels

Tyler: in Glittering Images she called George Lucas “perhaps, or maybe definitely, the greatest artist of our time.” Tyler does not disagree. But The Force Awakens has come out, and it is Disney, not Lucas.

Paglia: “It has nothing to do with George Lucas.” She has not seen it: “I would not dream of going. When it is on TV, I will look at it. Please. Do you think I want to sit in a theater and be tortured by the contamination of my ideals?”

On the prequels, which many people hate: after the great volcano planet climax of Revenge of the Sith, “I think it is one of the greatest sequences in all of modern art.” Her defense against dismissal: once she wrote about it, she realized how few people had actually seen the movie, because they gave up on the prequels long before. Anyone who dismisses her judgment of the scene’s “sublime quality, the vision, the execution, the emotions, and the passions” argues from ignorance. In her words, they never exposed themselves to it.

The method on display: judge the work itself, scene by scene, not the reputation around it. This is the same move she makes with Hitchcock’s The Birds later in the episode.

Stones versus Beatles

Tyler notes the two albums Hot Rocks and More Hot Rocks, and confesses he counts the Stones’ endless continuation as a mark against them: he never hears new things in the music anymore.

Paglia has not followed them for many years. But the original revolution: “the Rolling Stones were a revolution when they happened, in that period when the Beatles were all upbeat. Then, here come these surly guys sneering, and spitting, and so on.”

Tyler: the Beatles were dark and subtle too. Paglia: “Not like the Stones.” The difference is the root. “The Rolling Stones are inspired by, animated by, to this day, by the blues tradition. The Beatles really were more almost Broadway and musical comedy.” British music hall and Tin Pan Alley. Tremendous songsmiths, “but there is nothing dark about them.” Paul McCartney is a wonderful bass player, but “you are not getting the big, roaring sounds of Bill Wyman’s bass at the beginning of the Stones’ career.”

She has not felt it was the Stones she knew since Bill Wyman left. Arena shows are “not musical experiences. They are social experiences now.”

What stays fresh

Tyler asks what classic rock reveals more on every listen. Paglia: “Jimi Hendrix is one of the great geniuses of any instrument in the last a hundred years.” Also Crosby, Stills and Nash’s “Wooden Ships,” which she teaches, and Led Zeppelin’s “When the Levee Breaks,” much of it built in the studio, not just live.

Today? Tyler reminds her she called Taylor Swift “a pestilence.” Paglia: “Taylor Swift is like a nightmare.” She was hopeful about Rihanna for a while, but the new album “is an atrocity. It is really terrible.” The cause: a formulaic music industry. Young musicians cannot develop their chops over time the way her generation drew on the folk tradition.

Tyler teases that she sounds like a cultural conservative. Paglia’s answer is the cycle theory again: there are “certain magic moments, of fertility or creativity,” a confluence of influences and richness. Shakespeare would not be Shakespeare today. He left Stratford for London “at a magic moment when theater flourished, which was only for a few decades, and then it was out again.” The right person at the right time in the genre. Kanye West? “Oh my God. The bloat.”

Minerva and the A-minus seminar

Tyler describes Minerva: four years, each in a foreign country, small classes, all online, no library, no campus, about two years old. Prediction?

Paglia: sending young people abroad is great, “a proper use of the money that goes down the tubes at the major universities right now.” Primary education is “absolutely appalling in its lack of world history and world geography.” After 40 years of teaching students from every background, “it is unbelievable how little they know. It is absolutely shocking how little they know. This is a recipe for a disaster.”

The online part she rejects: “you need a live person, and you need a live person who can talk extemporaneously and respond to the moment. Not just people who read the same old damn lecture over and over again.” And the Ivy League seminar model: students learn “how to talk in a slightly pretentious way about things and impressing each other,” while the schools produce “all these clones, these polished, bourgeois clones, witless, knowing nothing.” Tyler names it: “The A-minus seminar.”

Underrated or overrated

Tyler runs the lightning round. Her calls:

  • Economics as a field: probably underrated. Economists are figures of fun in cartoons, which is her evidence.
  • William Faulkner: gone. “Thanks to Kate Millett and all these philistine feminist types in the early 70s, there was a great sweeping away of many, many major male figures,” including Hemingway and D. H. Lawrence, who influenced her hugely. In Mississippi Faulkner still lives. Elsewhere, years since she heard him mentioned. He should be on the reading list.
  • Yoko Ono: “One of my least favorite people in the universe. Yes, I blame her for the breakup of the Beatles.” But she gave Ono her due in Glittering Images as an important figure in 1960s conceptual art. “Very innovative… but what a dreary, humorless person.”
  • Alice Munro: “Oh, I do not read fiction. I do not read contemporary fiction.” Last contemporary fiction of interest: Auntie Mame. Fiction writers “do not see the world as it exists now… Their English is utterly stale and cloistered.”
  • Harry Potter: she refused to write on it for the Wall Street Journal. They asked Bloom instead. “Harold Bloom became known for it. He got that because of me.” Same with Madonna and Esquire: Norman Mailer got the cover interview “because Madonna said no to me.” HBO wanted a My Dinner with Andre format with Madonna. Madonna was afraid.
  • Parenthood: parenting is in crisis. “We have all these whiny, super sensitive girls on campus that will run shrieking at the slightest thing,” because parents raised them in a “bourgeois, pampered cocoon” and did not prepare them for real life.
  • Most underrated Shakespeare play: she cannot answer offhand, but notes Antony and Cleopatra, a great favorite of the 60s generation, recedes, perhaps because it is about imperialism.

Amelia Earhart: a feminism before feminism

The research

Tyler: she wrote or started a book on Amelia Earhart in high school. The appeal?

In 1961, age 14, she found an article in the Syracuse Herald Journal. She spent three years researching Earhart in the Syracuse Library, when the 1930s newspapers were still not on microfilm. “That is how I became a feminist before feminism revived.” She discovered the 1920s and 1930s career women: Earhart, Dorothy Parker, Dorothy Thompson, Clare Boothe Luce, Margaret Bourke-White, all after women won the vote.

Then second-wave feminism revived with Betty Friedan’s cofounding of NOW in 1967, and Paglia was out of sync. “I tried to join second-wave feminism. They would not have me because I would not bad mouth men.” The Earhart generation “did not bad mouth men. They admired men. They admired what men had done. What they said was, ‘We demand equal opportunity for women,’ which gave us the opportunity to show that we can achieve at the same level as men who did all these great things.”

The fights

At a 1971 feminist conference at Yale Law School, Kate Millett present, Rita Mae Brown said to her: “The difference between you and me, Camille, is that you want to save the universities, and I want to burn them down.” She had the knock-down Rolling Stones argument with the New Haven Women’s Liberation Rock Band: “Yes, the Rolling Stones are sexist, but they made great music.” Their reply: “Art! Art! Nothing that demeans women can be art!” Paglia: “Now that is a Stalinist view of art!”

First job at Bennington in 1972. A dinner with the new women’s studies department at SUNY Albany ended in a screaming argument about hormones: “They deny that hormones have the slightest impact on human life. They said hormones do not even exist.”

And the 1963 letter: at 16, she wrote to Newsweek protesting that Valentina Tereshkova was celebrated as the first woman in space on an anniversary of Earhart’s ocean flight. Her line: “Obviously, Amelia Earhart’s lifelong fight for equal opportunity for American women remains to be won.” It was their number one letter. Her verdict on Gloria Steinem: “Gloria Steinem can lick dirt, as far as I am concerned… She is a fraud, that woman. A fraud.”

The pay gap: the lie and the averages

An audience member, a master’s economics student, notes it is Equal Pay Day and asks about pay disparity. His theory: culture matters more than policy, and Zuckerberg publicizing paternity leave does more than mandates, because as long as only women demand flexibility for children, it hurts women on the margin.

Paglia’s answer has three layers.

Layer one: the 72 or 75 cents figure. “There may be cases of outrageous disparity in pay for doing the same work,” but they are rare. “There is all this propaganda pumped out about this issue,” when in fact, in her words, if women earn 72 cents or 75 cents on the dollar, it is not for the same job. The slogan is a lie, in her phrase. A woman doing the same job as a man does not get 75 cents for the dollar he gets.”

Layer two: the averages. Women, “of their own volition, for whatever reasons,” take jobs that have more flexibility as opposed to the around-the-clock, seven days a week, night thing. Women shy away from commission sales jobs with heavy road time, “and that is where a lot of men have very high earnings.” Women make choices, preferring to be closer to their children. “These disparities are ultimately based in biological differences.”

Layer three: the biology. Against Susan Faludi’s answer, “Men must do more”: “There is an ineffable, indefinable, biological tie between a child and the mother in whose body the child developed into a full being.” An infant wants the mother: “the smell is the mother… This is why Freud said every child wants to kill the father and marry the mother.” She speaks from inside “the world of the moms”: she has a child adopted from her former partner. The mother is “the generalissimo of the household,” with “the master strategist mind,” issuing “the master plan for the week,” while the husband “shrinks down to merely one counter.” “To ask men to do more, seems to me to ask them to do something that they are not biologically prepared to do.”

The method: 24/7 observation

Tyler: she consumes a remarkable number, amount, and diversity of culture products. What is the method? The diet?

Paglia: “It is a lifestyle of observation.” The basis of her work is care with writing plus observation. “It is 24/7. I am always observing. I do not sit in a university. I never go to conferences. That is a terrible mistake. A conference is like overlaying the same insular ideology on top of it. I am always listening to conversations at the shopping mall.”

She adores radio: talk shows, political shows, and sports shows, “the only place that you can hear on radio actual working class voices calling in.” Her writer’s voice is influenced by how English is actually spoken by people on radio, “this high impact sound,” against novelists with their “recherche lingo.”

Vindaloo, LSD, and psychedelic criticism

Tyler quotes her line: “My substitute for LSD was Indian food,” meaning lamb vindaloo. She stands by it. It is “a horrible rut. It is a 40-year rut.” Every visit to an Indian restaurant, she plans to try something new, then returns to the lamb vindaloo. “All I know is it is like an ecstasy for me, the lamb vindaloo.” The effects: “I attain nirvana.”

On LSD itself: “I never took it, thank God. I never took drugs.” Her objection was empirical: wine and beer have thousands of years behind them. LSD was untested. Her judgment on those who took it was blunt. LSD gave vision, then deprived people of the ability to translate that vision into material form for the present and for posterity. Brilliant minds she knew in graduate school and at Bennington never got anything done. When rejected, they stopped.

Yet she remains “very oriented toward the LSD vision”: the music, Jefferson Airplane, the Byrds’ “Eight Miles High.” “I have sometimes said that what I do is psychedelic criticism. Because it is metaphysical, it is visionary. I have a vision. I have a vision that is bigger than society.”

That is her problem with the Marxist approach, though she calls Arnold Hauser’s The Social History of Art one of the most influential books on her. Marxism is useful and her work stays attentive to social context, “but what Marxism lacks is that larger vision of the universe… It does not see nature. What kind of a vision does not see nature, could only see society?” Her line on multiculturalism: “if you want true multiculturalism, you have to present world cultures, including religion. Religion is extremely important. The most complex systems human beings have ever devised were the great religions of the world.”

Criticism is attention to the work

The Birds

Her BFI book on Hitchcock’s The Birds (1998) “was universally panned by the film journals,” because it was not poststructuralist or postmodernist. Poststructuralism reads every work as a structure of power. Postmodernism treats truth as made, not found. It cited no male gaze. “All this book does is go through the film The Birds from beginning to end, scene by scene by scene, and pays attention to the film itself.” Slowly it made its way. Her mission statement: “I believe that paying minute attention to the actual work itself is the mission of criticism. I am hopelessly old-fashioned.”

Foucault

“Because that is not what you are supposed to do. You are supposed to mention Foucault 59 times in one paragraph, et cetera. What a windbag that guy is. Foucault is nothing. He is nothing.” Her evidence: Foucault pretends to originality but is “just a collection of influences,” one of the biggest being the Philadelphia sociologist Goffman, who wrote The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life. “All the things that were an influence on me influenced Foucault.” Every gender studies department in the United States is “impregnated with Foucault,” which “is why we have graduates who know nothing.”

Sontag

Susan Sontag “began in graduate school… did a little teaching and then went off and became a luminary. She was a big luminary, a big giant dirigible luminary her whole life. Floating above the continents.” Over time her work got less meaningful. “You try to quote her on anything… The only sentence was the one she regretted, ‘The white race is the cancer of history.’” She retracted it when she got cancer. Paglia’s best-of-Sontag: “The Imagination of Disaster,” on 1950s horror films. “If she only stayed like that, unpretentious and really engaging with actual materials.”

The schoolmarm

The contrast is herself: “My life is completely mundane. I am a schoolmarm. That is all I am. I had the wisdom, hello, having been raised Catholic, that once I finally became known, at age 43, I did not change one thing about my life. Not one thing. I did not move to New York. I did not go chasing around. I did not get a speakers’ bureau.” A cousin is a nun. The family has bishops, priests, and sextons. “I hate the elites. I hate parties.” Forty years a classroom teacher, and “the kids have no idea that I write books.” Harvard, Princeton, and Yale professors have graduate students paying court for letters of recommendation. Her students want nothing from her, which keeps her close to ordinary life, “the basis of my work.”

The Eloi

That distance lets her name what she sees: on masculinity, “the masculine has no honor whatever in our culture.” Working-class culture retains an idea of the masculine. On campuses anything masculine reads as toxic, “intrinsic to rape culture.” The imagined utopia is “a utopian future… where there are no men. We are all genderless mannequins.” The Ivy League graduate: “They are the Eloi” of The Time Machine. “They are completely bland. They have no ideas. They all get along very well with each other because they are nothing.” They eat the fruits the Morlocks, the industrial class, give them. On androgyny: she began her career on its imaginative complexity, but “today’s androgyny, it is just boring.” David Bowie at his height was “absolutely brilliant, electrifying, kabuki.” The pallid androgynes of today have nothing creative.

Feminism’s ledger

Asked whether feminists will ever feel the job is done, Paglia calls herself “an equal opportunity feminist”: all obstacles to women’s advancement in politics and professional life should go. But “there are huge areas of human life that are not political that have to do with our private spiritual nature, and that is a place where legislation will always be helpless and hopeless and indeed, intrusive.”

Her ledger: “Capitalism made women’s emancipation possible,” a fact university curricula demean. Women went from total dependence on father, husband, and brother to self-support. The remaining problem is not men or the patriarchy. It is the modern mismatch: “we lived as a species for tens of thousands of year, where mating occurred early, where you left your parents’ house, and had your own household, and your own children. Juliet, in Romeo and Juliet, is 13 going on 14, and already, she is ready for marriage.” Now there is “a very long, an unnaturally long period here, before women can attain some sense of who they are as women.”

In the agrarian period, “there was the world of women, and the world of men, once. They did not have that much to do with each other once. All the problems started when we began to deal with each other.”

Loose ends

  • Her unrealized dream: to meet Catherine Deneuve. She once ran into her on Fifth Avenue, pursued her into the glove department at Saks, and got her to sign a Fillmore East ticket envelope. She still wants “a civilized conversation.”
  • Tippi Hedren invited her to lunch on Rodeo Drive after the Birds book and kept a stack of 12 copies to sign. Suzanne Pleshette, she says, was underutilized by Hollywood. Compare “the sophisticated Suzanne Pleshette” with Lena Dunham to see “the decline that we are in the middle of right now.”
  • Her late period: eight years on Native American explorations around the end of the ice age, glacial withdrawal, tiny artifacts. “Absolutely no one, especially anyone in Manhattan, has the slightest interest in my work.” She objects to the eternal projection of genocide and disaster onto Native American studies and wants to show “the actual vision of Native American culture which is a religious vision, a metaphysical vision.” The through-line to Bahia: forces of nature perceived as spirit entities, “an early animism.”
  • On language: young people’s sense of language degenerates. She used to write down every unknown word and study etymologies. Newspaper space limits once forced crispness. Web writing is “blah.”
  • Her career arc, in her words: “My early period was total failure, flop… Then, all of a sudden, I started to burst out, like a jack-in-the-box. It is been like blabber, blabber, blabber ever since.”

Questions and answers

What is Paglia's theory of culture cycles? Give the mechanism.

Culture alternates between esteeming the colossal and esteeming the small. The Bernini Baroque’s big swirls evaporate into Rococo valentine cards. Hollywood’s giant 1950s screens, built to beat television, evaporate into smartphone fragments. Each phase contains the seed of its opposite, and the wheel turns regardless of which phase you prefer.

Follow-up: Why did the large give way so fast, on her account? Because the small was already inside it: an avalanche of fragmented, badly designed impressions that reshaped children's brains and their capacity for sequential reasoning. The cycle does not need a conspiracy. It needs a screen that fits in a pocket.
Why does Brazil understand Paglia's work?

Because of Carnival, costuming, masquerade, baroque exuberance, and the syncretism of Christianity with Yoruba cults in Salvador de Bahia. Brazilians already live inside artifice and take beauty as an important human principle, while her fellow feminists trashed beauty. They also live inside the grandeur of nature, Iguacu, which makes the smallness of Western anxieties visible.

Follow-up: What is her political read of Brazil? Outside the world wars, no militaristic superstructure, no messianic self-image. Politics as chaos and drama, grand opera, another planet. Structure shapes sensibility.
Walk me through the Bloom saga as a lesson in persistence.

Zero courses taken with Bloom. One summons: “My dear, I am the only one who can direct that dissertation.” Seven publishers and five agents reject the book. Twenty years of work. Publication at 43. The lesson is not talent. It is the response function to rejection: infuriation instead of discouragement, craft development instead of stopping, revenge in the afterlife instead of surrender.

Follow-up: What killed her brilliant contemporaries? Not lack of vision. Lack of the ability to translate vision into material form, plus discouragement at the first rejection. Vision without translation is the LSD problem.
What is her defense of the Star Wars prequels?

Judge the scene, not the reputation. The volcano planet climax of Revenge of the Sith is “one of the greatest sequences in all of modern art,” with sublime quality in vision, execution, emotion, and passion. Most dismissals come from people who never watched the films, having given up on the prequels early. You cannot judge what you have not exposed yourself to.

Follow-up: What is the general critical rule? Pay minute attention to the actual work itself, scene by scene. That is the mission of criticism, and the reason her Birds book outlived its panning.
Explain the Stones versus Beatles distinction.

Root, not surface. The Stones are animated by the blues tradition: surly, sneering, dark, with Bill Wyman’s big roaring bass. The Beatles are Broadway, music hall, Tin Pan Alley: tremendous songsmiths with nothing dark about them. McCartney is a wonderful bass player, but you do not get the roar.

Follow-up: Why is she done with the Stones? Since Wyman left, it is not the Stones she knew, and arena shows are social experiences, not musical ones. The root left with the bassist.
What is her answer on the pay gap? Steelman it, then name its weakest point.

The steelman: 72 to 75 cents is an average across different jobs, not same-job pay, and calling it same-job pay is “the lie they tell.” The average reflects choices: flexibility over around-the-clock work, avoidance of road-heavy commission sales, closeness to children. The root is biological: the ineffable tie of mother and infant, the mother as generalissimo of the household. Asking men to do more asks what they are not biologically prepared to do.

Follow-up: The weakest point, from inside the episode: the audience member's own proposal. If flexibility demand came from both sexes, the margin penalty would shrink without denying any biology. Culture can move the margin even if biology sets the center.
What is the 24/7 method, exactly?

A lifestyle of observation. Never sit in the university. Never go to conferences, which overlay the same insular ideology. Listen to conversations at the shopping mall. Adore radio, especially sports shows, the only place to hear actual working-class voices. Let the writing voice absorb living spoken English instead of recherche lingo. Pair it with quality control in prose.

Follow-up: What does the method cost? Everything Paglia refuses: conferences, parties, New York, the speakers' bureau, the dirigible life. Attention is a budget. She spends it all on the mall and the radio.
What does she mean by "psychedelic criticism," and why does she attack both LSD and Marxism?

Psychedelic criticism is metaphysical and visionary: a vision bigger than society. LSD gave vision but deprived users of the ability to translate vision into material form, which is why brilliant minds produced nothing. Marxism, via Hauser, is useful on social context, but it lacks the larger vision and does not see nature. Both fail the same test: vision without the universe, or the universe without translation.

Follow-up: Apply the test to her own late work. Eight years on ice-age Native American artifacts, with no audience in Manhattan. Is that vision with translation, or vision without an audience? Her answer: it is substantive, and substance outranks interest.

How to imbibe this

  • This week: run the 24/7 protocol for three days. No conferences, no panels, no seminar rooms. One hour at a mall listening to conversations. One hour of sports radio. Write down ten sentences in the exact words people used. Notice how your own writing voice changes.
  • Catch yourself: the next time you dismiss a work by reputation (a prequel, a genre, a pop star), stop and apply the scene test. Watch or read the actual thing, one scene, with full attention. Write one paragraph on what is actually there.
  • Catch yourself: the next time you cite an average as a same-unit comparison (pay, test scores, crime), decompose it. Name the mix effect before you name the moral.
  • Behavior change per major idea:
  • Cycles: identify one domain where you live in the “small” phase (fragmented feeds, tiny screens). Schedule one weekly “colossal” session: a big screen, a long work, full attention. Log what the scale restores.
  • Rejection: start a rejection log. For every rejection, record the date, the decider, and one craft improvement it funds. Review quarterly. The target response is infuriation, not discouragement.
  • Observation: replace one conference or panel this quarter with field time: a mall, a market, a radio show, a bus route. Bring back ten verbatim sentences.
  • Language: revive her dictionary habit. Write down every word you do not know this week. Look up each etymology. Ten words minimum.
  • Artifice: find one local Carnival-equivalent, a place where people dress, mask, and perform. Attend. Note what beauty does there that argument cannot.
  • Monthly: audit your attention budget. Hours in insular rooms versus hours in living rooms. Move ten percent from the first to the second.

Think it yourself

Guided drills with answers shown. Do them with your own brain before touching AI.

  1. Hand-worked: the ledger. Twelve rejections, twenty years, publication at 43. Suppose she had quit at rejection 6, the median quit point. How many years of work would the world have lost? (Answer: all twenty, plus the book.) Now compute your own ratio: years on your longest project divided by rejections survived. Is your response function closer to infuriation or discouragement?
  2. Argue both sides: “Culture always moves in cycles.” For: Baroque to Rococo, giant screens to phones, colossal to small. Against: some changes look one-way (literacy, anesthesia, the web). Decide: which parts of culture cycle and which ratchet? Name one of each from your own lifetime.
  3. Decompose an average: find one publicized average gap (pay, grades, health). Write the slogan version in one sentence. Then list three mix effects that could produce it without any same-unit gap. What data would settle it?
  4. Fieldwork: spend one hour in a place Paglia would approve (mall, market, sports bar, bus). Transcribe ten verbatim sentences. Then write one paragraph in that voice about your own work. What changes?
  5. The scene test: pick a work you dislike by reputation. Watch or read one scene with full attention. Write what is actually there, no reputation words allowed. Did anything survive?
  6. First principles: she says conferences overlay “the same insular ideology.” Design a test. Attend one insular room and one living room in the same week. For each, list the three most frequent nouns you heard. Compare. What does the noun list reveal about each room’s ideology?

Skills you can now use

  • Run the cycle test on any cultural change: name the colossal phase and the small phase, and ask which way the wheel turns.
  • Decompose any average gap into mix effects before assigning a moral.
  • Apply the scene test: judge the work itself, not its reputation.
  • Use the root test on music and art: name the tradition animating the work, not the surface style.
  • Keep a rejection log with a craft improvement per rejection.
  • Practice 24/7 observation: malls, radio, verbatim sentences, living voice.
  • Distinguish vision from translation, and audit yourself on the second.
  • Name the Eloi room: consensus, blandness, idea-free politeness, and leave it.
