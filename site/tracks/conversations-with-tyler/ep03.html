Episode 3: Stephen Kotkin on Siberia, Stalin, and How Power Works

2024-12-04 · Instructor: Stephen Kotkin · Conversations with Tyler

Watch on YouTube

Video not loading? Watch on YouTube

Tyler interviews historian Stephen Kotkin on Siberia, Stalin, Magnitogorsk, and his Stalin biography.

Why this episode matters

This is the WATCHING GREAT MINDS chapter on how a historian thinks with his feet. Kotkin learned Russian in his third year of a PhD, lived under 24/7 surveillance in a closed steel city, walked cemeteries to earn archivists’ trust, and refused every source written with hindsight. Tyler uses him to show a production function: go there, read the archives prospectively, and let power explain itself. You learn why Siberia’s water may matter more than its oil, why censorship is promotion as much as suppression, and why Stalin is best explained by power, not personality.

Siberia: water, rivers, and three countries in one

The secession question

Tyler asks whether Siberia could peel off from European Russia for cultural or historic reasons. Kotkin: not much in it for them, and there are not very many people there. It is a colossal space, “far more empty than Canada.” His image: Canada with a far less dense population than it has now, “with effectively no Toronto, no Montreal, no Vancouver, but only provincial cities and unpopulated or sparsely populated areas in between.” Connectivity, governance, and economy would be supreme challenges without the orientation toward European Russia.

Three Siberias

Tyler heard it gets colder moving east, not just north. Kotkin: Western Siberia, Eastern Siberia, and the Russian Far East are dramatically different in terrain, flora, fauna, and weather. The rivers are the determining factor: “The rivers are colossal in Siberia, and they set the living patterns.” They shaped the history.

Then the water argument. Lake Baikal is the largest freshwater lake in the world. Almost every important Asian river comes from the Himalayas. As those glaciers shrink, the Siberian river systems become “supremely important as a strategic resource, not just as a basic resource.” Water-starved populations south of the mountains, above all China, could grow much more interested in Siberian water. Everyone thinks of coal, oil, gas, gold, the whole periodic table. “But it is the water, over time, that might prove to be among the most and maybe even the most important resource.”

Tyler: if desalination gets cheap, does Siberia become irrelevant? Kotkin: potentially, but scale is everything. “Something the size of Israel is a neighborhood in a Chinese urban agglomeration.”

Dull and poor?

Colin Thubron called Siberia “dull and poor.” Kotkin: right about poor, wrong about dull. Real taiga, dense ancient forest, is a wonder from a purely ecological point of view. “Dull would be almost a criminal appellation for Siberia.”

And the butter: yes, Siberian butter is very good, “especially when it is sold under the Danish label.” Russian exiles feasted on Siberian butter in European exile before the revolution, packaged as Danish butter.

The Ob valley: history as travel

Tyler asks how Kotkin got interested in the Ob River Valley. Kotkin quotes G. K. Chesterton: history is really travel. Travel widens horizons and teaches you about your homeland. He went there and found a palimpsest: multiple layers, not visible and not well known, including a Buddhist layer.

The layers: a Qing genocide against the Dzungars in inner Asia, which shaped the origins of the Dalai Lama as a pan-Buddhist institution. Agricultural settlement turning land into farmland. Soviet industrialization, “that toxic civilization that they built.” Plans to reverse the rivers with exploding nuclear devices: “the scientific lunacy that you see often in these projects where the rivers do not flow the way you want them to flow, and you think that there will not be second- and third-order consequences to the reversal of a river’s flow.”

Place names revealed the Dzungar prehistory. Many were clearly not Russian words. Etymology exposed a history with no visible signs, not even cemeteries, from just a few centuries before.

Akademgorodok and the cybernetics fantasy

The Siberian science city, Akademgorodok (Academic City), is still there, still large and important, still producing science, with triumphs over the generations. Cybernetics is a big presence. But funding is down from the Soviet period, and remoteness from manufacturing centers strangled translation: the region around it is old dirty industry, coking coal steel plants, mid-20th-century style. “They had the science belt, but they did not have it connected very well to applications in an industrial economy that was forward-looking, so they fell into a little bit of a rut.”

Tyler: why were the Soviets obsessed with cybernetics and AI in the 1960s? Did they see where things were headed, or was it a big stupid mistake?

Kotkin: never rule out big stupid mistakes. But the serious answer: cybernetics promised more efficient ways of gathering and using information, and the planned economy was “at core, a fantasy, never a reality.” In practice the planned economy was central control over scarce commodities, to privilege certain factories in the supply chain for military-industrial output. The rest was black market, “including black market factory of factory.”

Cybernetics was supposed to make planning work by optimizing information from localities. “It was a fantasy in a different light, and it is the same one that the Chinese Communist Party has today, which is to say if your authoritarian politics and your productive economy do not mesh very well, turn to technology, turn to technological solutions to get beyond the fact that you refuse to do the structural reforms on the institutional side.” The eternal fantasy: science and tech let you keep central control and the Party monopoly without reform.

The scientists themselves were world-class mathematicians, physicists, and computer scientists. For them the work stood on its own terms, as it would for scientists anywhere.

Magnitogorsk: the closed city

Orange air, warm homes

In the 1980s Kotkin lived in Magnitogorsk in the Urals. The best thing about it: the people. The environment was harsh beyond description: “Pollution would not begin to describe the orange air in the town.” A one-company, one-industry town with a gigantic integrated steel plant. They installed pollution controls but did not use them, because controls lowered output, and pay and bonuses depended on quantitative output.

Outside: mismanagement, corruption, unbreathable air, undrinkable water, no goods because they were stolen. Inside people’s homes: warmth, hospitality, and conversation “about novels and plays and poetry and science and the meaning of life and philosophical terms,” before the drinking even started. The contrast is the point: Slavic hospitality inside, systemic wreckage outside.

Three architectures

The early plans used utopian urban design. They built a city on European modernism, Bauhaus from Germany, Werkstatte from Austria, spare and elegant, and discovered it was not livable. Families did not want one bathroom down the hall for everybody. They did not want children raised collectively. They wanted self-contained nuclear family units with kitchen, toilet, and bath inside their own space.

The regime changed its mind. Three phases resulted. The 1920s-30s brought spare modernist communal living. Then came Stalinist Baroque ornamented family apartments, very expensive to build and “infinitely more popular.” Then came Khrushchev’s five-story prefab cement blocks, poorly constructed. The modernist first phase is still there.

Kotkin lived in none of them. He stayed in the American colony: self-contained cottages built for US and foreign engineers hired to build and launch the plant, which derived from the Gary, Indiana plant. Isolated for security, under 24/7 surveillance.

Censorship is marination

The closed city’s questions for the American: everything. But the principle was curiosity after marination. “We have a wrong view of censorship, that it is only suppression. It is also promotion of certain kinds of information and certain ways of thinking.” People asked whether workers in the West ever got holidays or whether resorts were only for the bourgeoisie, because that was the information they were marinated in. Was Reagan really a dangerous guy who wanted war?

They also wanted children’s clothes, medicines in short supply, and someone to carry correspondence out past the censors. French films showed apartments, furniture, fruit arrayed on streets, “the most exotic things imaginable.” They asked: are shops really like that? Do people really live in seven- and eight-room apartments? Can you travel without an exit visa? To leave the country they needed an exit visa on top of the destination’s visa.

The cemetery walk

Kotkin’s method for winning trust: radical empathy without validation. On the observation platform, the mayor’s party denied the prison complex on the other side. He joked that of course only New York had prisons, since Magnitogorsk had no criminals. That broke the ice.

Then the cemetery. After the solemn commemoration, he walked them through and narrated the lives on the headstones from the archives. They did not know their own history to that degree. “He put it this way: no validation of Stalinism, the enslavement of the peasantry, or the Gulag. All of that is beyond validation, and he condemned it. But alongside the verdict, understanding: a radical empathy that seeks how they could do it, who did it, and why.” After that walk, access to documentation kept growing.

Never-confuse pair: validation versus understanding. Validation says the thing was right. Understanding says you see how people could do it. Kotkin does the second and condemns the first.

Stalin: culture, collectivization, and power

Did Stalin understand genius?

Tyler: Pasternak, Shostakovich, Bulgakov, Eisenstein. Did Stalin understand they were geniuses? He treated them badly but did not crush them fully.

Kotkin: for Stalin, high culture was a mark of advancement. Bildung, kulturnost, edification. His generation acquired literacy broadly: opera, painting, novels, poetry, Russian and pan-European canons. He was predominantly self-educated. Seminary, not gymnasium, so university was closed to him.

Trotsky performed greater education: exile in Europe, multiple languages read fluently. Stalin had “a working bazaar- or market-level knowledge of Persian, Armenian,” the Caucasus marketplace.

On judgment: yes, Stalin could judge Bulgakov the superior writer and Eisenstein the superior filmmaker. Few Communists shared those tastes for bourgeois or pre-revolutionary writers. He had blind spots too. He loved Chekhov most, because Chekhov’s villains were credible: “believable villains… full-bodied villains in addition to the heroes.” As the despot arbiter of what culture got distributed, his tastes were critical.

Why Prokofiev returned

Tyler: why did Prokofiev go back in 1936? Kotkin: exile is not emigration. Emigrants go voluntarily to assimilate. Exiles are kicked out or barely escape, and nurture hopes of return. For the supremely talented, appreciation at home differs from appreciation abroad. He cites the film Mephisto: the actor under the Nazis says not everybody can leave. For some, the German language and the theater are life. Prokofiev went back to do music for appreciative audiences in a musical culture where he was a master. In 1936 he could not fully see the coming Great Terror of 1936-38. But who did at the time?

The 1 percent

Tyler raises James Hughes’s hypothesis: Stalin’s 1928 Siberia trip showed him the countryside’s problem and taught him to crush the kulaks. Kotkin: no. He went to Barnaul, where the museum keeps the wooden sled that carried Stalin from the railhead to the barn where he gave the speech. The decision was already reached. The trip was staged to break the party’s liaison with the richer peasants. Provincial officials had grown rich with kulaks, married their daughters to them. Stalin delivered a searing, nationally publicized speech: the collective moves at full speed forward, and kulaks are the enemy.

The doctrine: for Marxist-Leninists, the base determines the superstructure. A Communist Party in power plus de facto market relations in the countryside, with richer peasants (more than two cows) as a quasi-bourgeois class called kulaks, meant either the base would triumph or the Party would have to remove the base.

Nobody imagined it could be done across all of Soviet Eurasia. Then the number: “Voluntary collectivization as of 1928… is 1 percent of arable land. One percent, meaning that the people who could not farm themselves, individually or as a household, as a family, they went into collectives voluntarily because they were incapable of managing on their own.”

The hand-worked arithmetic: to go from 1 percent to 99 percent collective, you must take property from 99 out of every 100 units of farmland and force the holders in. The property was de facto, not de jure, “but de facto property is still property.” Stalin did it. That shocked everyone. It caused famine and endangered the party’s own rule, and he kept going “because he had the courage of his convictions.” Critics got a mental note and revenge a few years later. He galvanized half-educated youth as shock troops against the kulaks.

Then Kotkin’s definition of totalitarianism: “he galvanized people’s agency, and those people, using their agency, destroyed their own agency. They disempowered themselves by taking up his call.”

Power, not DNA

Tyler: did Georgian blood-feud culture shape Stalin? Kotkin’s rebuttal is personal. He got into schoolyard fights too. People beat him up as a half Catholic, half Jew at a Catholic school in Englewood, New Jersey. His father disciplined him with the belt. “I did not go on to collectivize agriculture. I am not responsible for the deaths of 18 to 20 million people.” Childhood tropes cannot explain the phenomenon.

What explains Stalin: “the experience of getting into power and then exercising power. It is building and running the dictatorship.” Geography, capabilities relative to other great powers, inherited institutions. “This mix of Stalin building a dictatorship inside the dictatorship and exercising that power creates the kind of person that we know as Stalin.”

His source discipline: refuse retrospective memoirs, where survivors look back and find the cat in the microwave at age 11. Use only what people said about him in real time, prospectively. That method yields the six resignations: six times in the 1920s Stalin resigned as general secretary. Three are documented in writing, three in solid multi-source oral testimony. Every time, his closest colleagues refused to let him go, because he carried the regime on his shoulders. He was the only one in the group capable of it, dedicated like nobody else. They did not see the later Stalin.

Then the turn: “those people in the room with him those six times, he murdered almost all of them, in less than a decade in some cases, and in less than two decades in other cases.” They did not perceive the danger, or they would remove him. The personality transformed “in symbiosis” with building and running the dictatorship. Youth and church schools inform him, liturgical repetition and all, but they do not explain “one of the three bloodiest dictators in the history of our planet: Hitler, Stalin, and Mao.”

Foucault: micropower

Lunch with Foucault

Kotkin went to Berkeley for a PhD in 1981, finished in 1988, first job Princeton 1989. He started in French history, switched to Habsburg, then to Russian Soviet, learning the Russian alphabet in his third year, the year of his PhD exams.

Foucault came to Berkeley in the 80s, like Derrida, Habermas, and Levi-Strauss. At lunch Foucault suggested somebody apply his theories to Stalinism. Kotkin was 23. His image: meeting Nietzsche in a Swiss mountain cafe.

Kotkin had read Madness and Civilization in seminar without understanding it. He asked Foucault naive questions, and Foucault patiently explained to the self-described moron what he meant. Kotkin became class coordinator for Foucault’s lectures on the truth-teller in ancient Greece. Lunches at Little Joe’s in Little Italy followed. Foucault’s asides became dissertations: “The idea of civil society is the opiate of the intellectual class.” Class in France came from cholera in Paris neighborhoods, not factory status.

The toolkit

What Foucault showed him: how power works not through bureaucracy or the secret police but through daily life, “the micro versions of power… little people doing this.” Denunciation culture: neighbors denouncing neighbors for heresies, loosening all social trust, producing dependence on the state. “You are a gung-ho activist using your agency, and the next thing you know, you have no power whatsoever.”

Michel de Certeau gave him the phrase “the little tactics of the habitat,” a core idea of the dissertation and book Magnetic Mountain. People internalize the despot: “What would Stalin do in this situation?” asked by people who never met Stalin, until it becomes second nature.

Kotkin’s boundary: he never shared Foucault’s view that Western daily life was its own form of imprisonment. He took the analytical toolkit and applied it to actual totalitarianism.

Tyler connects it to Vasily Grossman’s Life and Fate: passivity, denunciations, decentralized control. Kotkin read Grossman later. His training was the Annales school: Bloch, Febvre, Braudel, Chaunu. Total history from judicial archives: economy, society, culture, politics in one. He became, he says, the first archival case study of a Soviet place, Annales-style, spliced with Foucault and de Certeau on micropower.

The Japanese detour

As an assistant professor up for tenure, Kotkin spent his fourth year, the year before the file was due, at a Japanese language boot camp in Tokyo. The senior faculty asked if he was out of his mind. Eighteen months of boot camp with students from Mongolia, Korea, Hong Kong. Two non-Asians in the class. Kotkin and the Austrian embassy’s cultural attache. The Hong Kong students could read the characters the teacher wrote. The sigh of recognition, “Ohh, that is what it means,” taught him how the writing systems diverged.

He worked with Wada Haruki at Tokyo University and joined Hamashta’s seminar on the Chinese tributary system. He met his wife, a South Korean PhD student at Columbia writing on Korean ceramics’ influence on Japanese ceramics, in Kanazawa. Two and a half years in Japan total, plus a year at the Slavic Research Center in Hokkaido. He chose Japanese over Chinese after a 1987 trip through both: two months in China from Harbin down to Hong Kong, Xi’an and the terracotta warriors, then 80s Japan, “this modernity that worked.” Tenure came anyway. Thirty-three years at Princeton.

Volume 3: the four partitions

Why it is late

The final Stalin volume is years away. Three separate, unrelated cancers cost about 18 months of medical care, all caught early. The bigger cause: each volume is harder than the last. World War II is half of volume 3, and “so much of what we think to be true… did not have solid evidence behind it in many cases.” He is now in the Chinese Revolution and the 1945-53 Cold War.

His discovery: the Cold War literature obsesses over nonstrategic questions like the fate of Poland. For Poles that is existential. For the global order, the bigger story is what he calls the four partitions: China, Korea, Japan, and Indochina.

The ledger: China, where partition was on the table but the Communists won outright. Korea, where full Soviet takeover was possible but Truman asked Stalin to stop at the 38th parallel, though Truman had no soldiers on the ground to enforce it. Japan, where Stalin was ready to invade the home islands and had already given the order, so partition looked certain, but the Communists never came near power. Indochina, now Vietnam, which was partitioned.

Two partitions happened, Korea and Indochina, and both led to war. The East Asian Cold War “still reverberates today and, I would argue, is not over.” Marshall’s mission failed in China as badly as the Marshall Plan succeeded in Europe.

Totalitarian Superpower

Volume 3 is called Totalitarian Superpower. The thesis: the Soviet Union was a superior totalitarianism to Nazism but an inferior superpower to the United States, “which is why it was successful in one case and unsuccessful in the other case.” The resonance with China today is deliberate but not identical.

Kotkin on the cost of the work: nearly two decades in Joseph Stalin’s company. The documents carry dried blood. The blood on that page is not Stalin’s. It belongs to somebody else, from their interrogation, which he read and signed off on. Stalin is “the gold standard of power”: accumulate, operationalize, consequences. “From the point of view of morality, freedom, the stuff that I cherish… it is just devastating. There is no limit. The moral squalor is just bottomless.”

He writes this from a plush Stanford office with Silicon Valley outside and a $3 million house on sale for $10 million down the street. Then he closes the laptop and gets sushi. The places he wrote about, Kramatorsk and the rest, are in the news again: “I never thought I would live through war in those places again.”

Questions and answers

Why might Siberia's water matter more than its oil?

Almost every important Asian river starts in the Himalayas. As those glaciers shrink, Siberian rivers become the strategic reserve. China sits south of the mountains with enormous water-starved populations. Oil, gas, and gold are priced and traded. Water is existential and hard to move. Desalination could change this, but only at continental scale: Israel-sized output is a neighborhood in a Chinese urban agglomeration.

Follow-up: What is the general move? Find the resource everyone ignores because it is not traded. Then ask who cannot live without it.
Walk me through the collectivization arithmetic. Why does 1 percent matter?

In 1928, voluntary collectivization covered 1 percent of arable land, the land of people who could not farm alone. To reach 99 percent, the regime had to take de facto property from 99 of every 100 units and force the holders in. The ratio of forced to voluntary was 99 to 1. That ratio is the whole story: the policy was not persuasion scaled up. It was confiscation.

Follow-up: What did the trip to Barnaul prove? Nothing about discovery. The wooden sled in the museum's back room marks a decision already made. The trip broke the party's kulak liaison and announced the confiscation.
What is Kotkin's definition of totalitarianism, in one mechanism?

The regime galvanizes people’s agency, and those people, using their agency, destroy their own agency. Neighbors denounce neighbors. Activists enforce the terror. Each act of participation loosens social trust and deepens dependence on the state. You start gung-ho and end powerless.

Follow-up: Where did he get the lens? Foucault's micropower: power enforced through daily life, not just bureaucracy. Plus de Certeau's "little tactics of the habitat." He kept the toolkit and dropped Foucault's claim that the West was imprisoned too.
Why does Kotkin reject the Georgian blood-feud explanation of Stalin?

Three reasons. First, the personal test: Kotkin had schoolyard fights and the belt in Englewood, New Jersey, and did not collectivize agriculture or kill 18 to 20 million people. Second, the source test: retrospective memoirs find the monster at age 11 because they know the ending. Prospective sources show six resignations in the 1920s, refused by colleagues who saw an indispensable workhorse. Third, the category test: plenty of Georgians of that generation were gentle, including Party members.

Follow-up: What does explain Stalin, then? Building and running the dictatorship. The personality formed in symbiosis with power. That is why the colleagues who refused his resignations were murdered within two decades: they could not see the transformation while inside it.
What is the difference between censorship as suppression and censorship as marination?

Suppression removes information. Marination promotes certain information and ways of thinking until they feel like common sense. The Magnitogorsk residents did not just lack facts about the West. They had a full alternative picture: workers never on holiday, resorts for the bourgeoisie, Reagan the warmonger. Propaganda is a diet, not just a locked door.

Follow-up: How do you detect marination in yourself? Ask what you believe that you never chose to check. Then find the French film: the casual image, the fruit on the street, that contradicts the diet without arguing.
Explain the four partitions and why Kotkin thinks Poland is overrated as Cold War history.

The four: China, Korea, Japan, Indochina. Two were partitioned (Korea at the 38th parallel on Truman’s ask, Indochina by settlement), and both led to war. Two were not (China because the Communists won, Japan because they never came near power). Poland’s fate is existential for Poles but not central to the global order. East Asia is where the Cold War still reverberates and is not over.

Follow-up: What is the method? Reweight by present consequences, not by past attention. Marshall failed in China as badly as he succeeded in Europe. The history books remember the success.
What is "radical empathy," and how is it different from validation?

Radical empathy tries to appreciate how people could do what they did, who did it, and why. Validation says it was right. Kotkin walked the Magnitogorsk cemetery narrating headstones from the archives, earning trust through knowledge. He condemns Stalinism, the enslavement of the peasantry, and the Gulag as beyond validation. Understanding is the tool. Condemnation is the verdict. They are not in tension.

Follow-up: When does empathy become excuse? When it skips the verdict. Kotkin states the condemnation first, then does the understanding. Order matters.
What is the cybernetics lesson for today?

The planned economy was a fantasy, never a reality. Cybernetics was a fantasy in a different light: better information would make planning work, so the regime would not have to reform. Kotkin says the Chinese Communist Party runs the same play today: technology instead of structural reform, to keep central control and the monopoly. The test for any tech fix: does it remove the need to give up power? If yes, suspect the fantasy.

Follow-up: What is the honest version? The Soviet scientists were world-class and the problems were genuinely fascinating. A fantasy can be intellectually serious and politically self-serving at once.

How to imbibe this

  • This week: practice the prospective rule. Pick one person you judge harshly with hindsight. Find one thing said about them in real time, before the outcome was known. Write down how the judgment changes.
  • Catch yourself: the next time you explain a monster by childhood, run Kotkin’s test. Did you share the supposed cause? Did gentle people share it too? If yes to either, drop the cause and look at power.
  • Catch yourself: the next time you call something censored, ask the marination question. What was promoted into the gap? Name the diet, not just the locked door.

  • Palimpsest: pick one place you know well. Find three layers in it: a name, a building, a vanished use. Ask what etymology or archives would reveal. Write the layers down.

Think it yourself

Guided drills with answers shown. Do them with your own brain before touching AI.

  1. Hand-worked: the 99 to 1. Voluntary collectivization is 1 percent of arable land. A district has 10,000 hectares. How many hectares were voluntarily collectivized? (Answer: 100.) How many had to be taken by force to reach 99 percent? (Answer: 9,800.) What does the ratio tell you about the verb “persuade” in this story?
  2. Fermi: Baikal holds roughly one-fifth of the world’s unfrozen surface fresh water. [uncertain: the episode calls it the largest freshwater lake but gives no fraction. The one-fifth figure is general knowledge, not from the episode.] Suppose a water-starved region of 100 million people needs 100 liters per person per day. How many days of that demand does one percent of Baikal’s volume cover? Set up the arithmetic and name every assumption. What breaks first in your model?
  3. Source audit: take one historical claim you believe about a famous person. Classify every source you can name as retrospective or prospective. How many survive the Kotkin filter? What changes?
  4. Journal: “My marination.” List five things you believe that you never chose to check. For each, name the promoter: who benefits from you believing it? Find one French film, one casual image, that contradicts each.
  5. Reweight: pick one historical event you were taught as central. Ask Kotkin’s Poland question: central to whom? Name the East Asia of your subject, the part with present consequences that got less weight. Sketch the reweighted story in five sentences.
  6. First principles: define totalitarianism three ways: Hannah Arendt’s, Kotkin’s (galvanized agency destroying itself), and your own from the micropower log. Where do the three agree? Where does Kotkin’s add something the others miss?

  • Apply the prospective-source filter to any historical or biographical claim.
