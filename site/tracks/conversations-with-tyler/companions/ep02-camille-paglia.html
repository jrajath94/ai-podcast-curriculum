Camille Paglia on Her Lifestyle of Observation and Lamb Vindaloo

Tyler Cowen calls her essays on Spenser, Alice in Wonderland, and de Sade the best ever written. What follows is a whirlwind: the death of the large image, Brazil's baroque exuberance, the prequels as high art, the Stones against the Beatles, Harold Bloom's summons, twelve rejections, the pay-gap arithmetic, the Eloi, Amelia Earhart at fourteen, and a forty-year vindaloo rut. Through it all runs one method: total observation, twenty-four hours a day.

The Last Tech Guru Was McLuhan

Source fact Tyler's introduction is brief and grand: Paglia wrote the very best essays ever on Edmund Spenser, Alice in Wonderland, and the Marquis de Sade, understands Bob Dylan and Susan Sontag, and pursued a career of great integrity. A reader asks how she feels about Silicon Valley dominating economy and culture, and whether any tech guru interests her. Her answer: her last big tech guru was Marshall McLuhan, with his prophetic insight into what would come. He is the patron saint of her web writing, back to the first issue of Salon in 1995, when established journalists treated the web as a joke. A major political reporter at the Boston Globe even pressured her not to write for it: no one takes the web seriously. The web then sucked in a whole generation, which is all they know now, and she thinks we are on the downside of it.

Nugget 1 · Foresight

Old prophets for new mediums

What happened. Asked to name a current tech guru, Paglia named a dead Canadian media theorist. McLuhan gave her the frame for the web before the web mattered, while working journalists dismissed it.

Why it matters. The best guide to a new medium is rarely its boosters. It is the thinker who described the pattern before the instance existed.

The principle. When a new medium arrives, read the old prophets first. The pattern is older than the platform.

~00:01

Stare at One Image

Source fact Tyler asks about Glittering Images, her book on the iconic and the spectacular, against a present where people stare at tiny smartphones. Is the splendid visual culture dead, and if it was so splendid, why did people abandon it so fast? Paglia's answer starts with the book's motive: young people drown in an avalanche of fragmented, disconnected, tacky visual impressions, and she believes it reshapes young brains, eroding the standard forms of logic and sequential reasoning. She wrote a book that asks the reader to stare at one image for a long time. Web design, she says, is in the pits. The miniaturization of the image is terrible. She grew up in the 1950s, when Hollywood fought television with the gigantic: in The Ten Commandments a colossal Pharaoh crosses the whole screen. Lawrence of Arabia gives you the dunes with that music. Young people now have no sense of the expansive, the big gesture.

Source fact Tyler presses: if the large fell so fast, what was wrong with the old culture? Paglia answers with cycles. Cultures alternate between the colossal and the small: the Bernini Baroque gives way to the Rococo, the evaporation of big Baroque swirls into tiny Valentine-card things. She feels lucky to have an epic imagination, raised on The Ten Commandments and Ben-Hur, which she could watch 300 times. The music of those films directly inspired Sexual Personae. She is directly inspired by music, and for women, she says, it is good to have something that makes you assert and trample and conquer. Asked whether that makes her a cultural conservative, she says no: a conservative wants to return to a lost past. She believes the momentum runs into the future. She is a libertarian, a Democrat who offends both sides, because a true intellectual stays beyond partisanship and critiques the premises of her own allies.

Nugget 2 · Attention

Stare at one image

What happened. Against the avalanche of fragmented impressions, Paglia wrote a book with one demand: look at a single image for a long time. Duration is the method.

Why it matters. Fragmentation trains the eye to skim. Skimming feels like seeing. The book's discipline restores the difference.

The principle. Depth is a practice, not a talent. Give one thing more time than the medium wants you to.

Nugget 3 · History

Culture moves in cycles

What happened. The colossal Baroque evaporated into the tiny Rococo. The gigantic 1950s screen shrank into the smartphone. Paglia reads both as the same oscillation, not a fall.

Why it matters. If the pattern is cyclical, the present is a phase, not an end. The small will exhaust itself and the large will return in some new form.

The principle. Do not mistake the phase for the fate. Ask where you are in the cycle before you mourn or celebrate.

~00:05

Brazil Understands Artifice

Source fact Paglia visited Brazil nine or ten times. What draws her: Brazilians understood her work from the first moment, because Carnival taught them artifice, costuming, masquerade, baroque exuberance, and the syncretism of Christianity with Yoruba cults in Salvador de Bahia. They grasped her vision of beauty as a serious human principle, at the exact moment her fellow feminists attacked beauty. They also understand nature's grandeur and power. Tyler names Iguaçu. Paglia contrasts that scale with what she calls silly little arguments that climate change will end the world: anyone who talks that way does not understand nature's power. To imagine humans can change it is absurd.

Source fact Tyler asks for her theory of modernity: why is Brazil on a different part of the curve? Her answer is structural. Brazil skipped the world wars, built no huge militaristic superstructure, and holds no messianic view of itself. Its politics are chaos and drama, like grand opera. It is another planet.

Nugget 4 · Culture

Brazil's interpretive key

What happened. Carnival gave Brazilians a native language for artifice and mask, so Paglia's work on beauty and spectacle needed no translation. Iguaçu gave them a native scale for nature's power.

Why it matters. Ideas land where the culture already has the receptor. The same book reads as revelation in one country and provocation in another, depending on the local metaphors.

The principle. Before you export an idea, find the local receptor. The culture that already performs your thesis will understand it first.

~00:07

Lucas, the Prequels, and the Volcano Planet

Source fact In Glittering Images Paglia called George Lucas perhaps, maybe definitely, the greatest artist of our time. Tyler notes The Force Awakens has since arrived, and it is Disney, not Lucas. Paglia has not seen it and will not: she refuses to sit in a theater and be tortured by the contamination of her ideals. On the prequels, which most people despise, she is specific: the volcano-planet climax of Revenge of the Sith is one of the greatest sequences in all of modern art. Most people who dismiss her judgment never actually watched the films. They gave up on the prequels long before. Anyone who rejects her reading of the scene's sublime quality, vision, execution, emotion, and passion has not exposed themselves to it.

Nugget 5 · Judgment

Judge the work, not the consensus

What happened. Paglia defends the prequels' best sequence as high art while admitting most of her critics never watched it. The consensus formed without the evidence.

Why it matters. Cultural verdicts are often laundered: people repeat the dismissal without the viewing. The confident opinion is frequently the unexamined one.

The principle. Before you join a consensus, check whether its members did the reading. If they skipped the work, their verdict is hearsay.

~00:09

Blues Against Music Hall

Source fact Tyler counts the Stones' longevity against them: they kept going, but he never hears anything new in the music anymore. Paglia has not followed them for years. For her, the Stones were a revolution against the upbeat Beatles: surly, sneering, spitting men animated by the blues tradition, against a Beatles sound rooted in British music hall and Tin Pan Alley. Tremendous songsmiths, but nothing dark. Paul McCartney is a wonderful bass player, but you do not get Bill Wyman's big roaring bass. Since Wyman left, she says, it has not been her Stones, and arena shows with light spectacles are social experiences, not musical ones. Asked what classic rock still reveals more on every listen, Tyler names Brian Wilson and Jimi Hendrix. Paglia agrees on Hendrix, one of the great geniuses on any instrument in a hundred years, and adds Crosby, Stills and Nash's Wooden Ships and Led Zeppelin's When the Levee Breaks, much of it built in the studio, not just live.

Source fact Who today stands up to the giants? Paglia had hopes for Rihanna, but the new album is an atrocity and she no longer works with top producers. The industry is formulaic. Young musicians cannot develop their chops the way her generation drew on the folk tradition. Kanye West? She dismisses him as bloat. Then the larger point: there are magic moments of fertility when influences converge, and luck matters enormously. Shakespeare would not be Shakespeare today. He left Stratford for London at the exact decades when theater flourished. Be the right person at the right time, in the right genre, or be forgotten.

Nugget 6 · Art

Tradition shapes the sound

What happened. The Stones' menace came from the blues. The Beatles' brightness came from music hall. Same era, same industry, different root systems, different music.

Why it matters. Surface style is downstream of the tradition the artist drank from. To understand the work, trace the well, not the bucket.

The principle. Ask what tradition feeds any strong voice. The root explains the fruit.

Nugget 7 · Timing

Magic moments need confluence

What happened. Shakespeare hit London during the few decades theater flourished. The 1960s bands drew on a living folk tradition. Remove the confluence and the genius has nothing to stand on.

Why it matters. Talent is necessary but not sufficient. The moment supplies the materials, the audience, and the rivals. Greatness is talent meeting a fertile window.

The principle. Study the window, not just the person. If you want to matter, find where the influences converge now.

~00:14

Send Them Abroad

Source fact Tyler describes Minerva, the new model school: four years, each in a different foreign country (Buenos Aires, Istanbul, Bangalore), small classes held online, no library, no campus. Paglia loves half of it. Sending young people abroad is a proper use of the money now going down the tubes at major universities. American primary education fails at world history and geography, and she sees the wreckage after 40 years of teaching. After 40 years she has a clear sense of what primary schools produce, and she calls it shocking, a recipe for disaster. The online half she rejects completely: teaching needs a live person who can talk extemporaneously and respond to the moment, not someone reading the same old lecture. And the Ivy seminar model she despises: small seminars where students learn to talk in a slightly pretentious way, impress each other, and get packaged as polished, witless clones who know nothing.

Trap card. The Ivy seminar and bourgeois-clone passages are Paglia's polemical characterizations of Ivy League teaching, not documented findings. The episode presents her verdict. Treat it as her view of the culture, not as a measured outcome.

Nugget 8 · Education

Send them abroad

What happened. Paglia endorsed Minerva's travel and rejected its screens. The world cures the ignorance she sees in every classroom. The live teacher cures the deadness of the recorded lecture.

Why it matters. Two different scarcities need two different fixes. Geography is cured by travel. Presence is cured by a person in the room. No single reform supplies both.

The principle. Match the cure to the scarcity. Do not let a good idea about place smuggle in a bad idea about presence.

Bloom and the Twelve Rejections

Source fact Asked for a Harold Bloom story she can tell: she never took his course at Yale. She knew him only to say hello until he heard she could not find a dissertation director for a study of androgyny in literature and art. At the time, nobody at the Yale Graduate School wrote dissertations on sex. He summoned her to his office and claimed the dissertation outright: he was the only one who could direct it. She said OK.

My dear, I am the only one who can direct that dissertation.

Harold Bloom to Camille Paglia · Episode ~00:16

Source fact He understood everything she wanted to do, and he sustained her through the decades when Sexual Personae could not get published: rejected by seven publishers and five agents. When it finally appeared, she was 43. She offers herself as a role model for soldiering through adversity and rejection and continuing to develop the craft until the work sees print. Bloom always said she gave him great naches, like a father to a daughter, and they agreed on Freud.

Nugget 9 · Mentorship

Find the one reader who gets it

What happened. No director at Yale would touch a dissertation on androgyny. Bloom summoned her and claimed it outright: he was the only one who could direct it, and he was right.

Why it matters. Unusual work does not need many supporters. It needs one person with the standing to protect it and the comprehension to guide it.

The principle. Do not shop a strange idea to the crowd. Find the single mind that already lives where you are headed.

Nugget 10 · Persistence

Rejection infuriates, then outlast it

What happened. Twelve rejections across publishers and agents. Twenty years of work. Publication at 43. Paglia's response to rejection was fury, then revenge through persistence: I will be around, and you will be dead.

Why it matters. Most brilliant minds she knew at Bennington quit after rejection. The difference between them and her was not talent but resilience against obstacles.

The principle. Let rejection infuriate you, then convert the fury into duration. Outlast the gatekeepers.

~00:18

Overrated or Underrated

Source fact The rapid round. Economics as a field: probably underrated. Economists are figures of fun in cartoons, which is her whole evidence, and she admits it. William Faulkner: gone. He was once a baseline figure everyone read. Then Kate Millett and the early-1970s feminist critics swept away the major male figures, Hemingway, D. H. Lawrence, Faulkner with them. Outside Mississippi, nobody mentions him. He should be on the reading list. Yoko Ono: one of her least favorite people in the universe. She blames her for the Beatles breakup. The screechy yodeling is a horror. But she gave Ono her due in Glittering Images as an important, innovative figure in 1960s conceptual art, however dreary and humorless the person. Alice Munro: Paglia does not read contemporary fiction at all. The last contemporary fiction she cared about was Auntie Mame. Fiction writers live in another world, their English stale and cloistered. She refused to write on Harry Potter for the Wall Street Journal. They asked Bloom instead, and he got famous for it because of her. Parenthood: first she thinks he means the show. Then, seriously: parenting is in crisis. Bourgeois pampered cocoons produce the whiny, oversensitive campus girls who shriek at offense. Parents have not prepared them for real life. The most underrated Shakespeare play: she teaches only the major plays, but Antony and Cleopatra recedes from the curriculum, perhaps because it is about imperialism.

Nugget 11 · Canon

Canons are political

What happened. Faulkner went from baseline to ghost in one critical generation. The mechanism was not rereading but sweeping: a school of critics decided the major male figures had to go.

Why it matters. What survives in the curriculum is not a pure merit ranking. It is merit filtered through the politics of each critical generation.

The principle. When a major figure vanishes, ask who swept and why. The absence is data about the sweepers, not just the swept.

~00:24

The Sexes and the Eloi

Source fact Which country has the healthiest relations between the sexes? Brazil, for sexuality itself: magnificent, stylish, assertive women, though professional men and women there still misfire. Brazilian men, she observes, sag and plump while the women rule. Woman is the cock of the walk. On gay culture: AIDS was like a Holocaust for gay male culture, wiping out a generation of fascinating men, and the culture is still recovering. Assimilation brought domesticity but dissolved the distinct gay aesthetic. Oscar Wilde remains a major influence on her thinking, and she teaches a whole course on him. Asked for today's ideal of masculinity: the masculine has no honor in current culture. University gender norms declare gender a pure construct with nothing in the body. Working-class culture retains an idea of the masculine, but the campus identifies anything masculine as toxic, part of rape culture, and imagines a utopia of genderless mannequins. Her image for the Ivy League graduate: the Eloi from The Time Machine, bland, idea-free, getting along because they are nothing, eating fruit handed down by the Morlocks of the industrial class.

The pay-gap arithmetic, per Paglia

Her claim: the famous 72 to 75 cent figure is an overall average across different jobs, not same-job pay.

Paglia's on-air argument, ~01:08. She attributes the gap to job choice and flexibility, calling same-job disparity rare. Presented as her case, not an adjudicated finding.

Trap card. The 72/75-cent rebuttal is Paglia's on-air argument on Equal Pay Day, not a settled empirical finding. She claims women doing the same job earn about the same, with the average gap coming from job choice and flexibility. The episode presents her case. Treat the arithmetic as her position.

Nugget 12 · Statistics

The wrong denominator

What happened. Paglia argues the famous pay figure divides all women's earnings by all men's earnings, mixing different jobs, then presents the result as if it described the same job. The same-job comparison, she claims, is near parity.

Why it matters. Averages across different choices do not measure discrimination within the same choice. The denominator decides what the number means.

The principle. Before you act on a statistic, ask what it divides by what. The wrong denominator manufactures the wrong scandal.

Nugget 13 · Culture

Honor lives outside the campus

What happened. Paglia grants the campus its verdict on masculinity and looks elsewhere: working-class culture, NASCAR crowds, places the Ivy League never visits, where the masculine still has honor.

Why it matters. Elite institutions mistake their norms for the culture's norms. The thing they declare dead is often alive one county over.

The principle. When an elite consensus declares something extinct, check outside the elite's habitat before you believe it.

Nugget 14 · Warning

The Eloi warning

What happened. Paglia's image for the processed Ivy graduate: the Eloi, bland and idea-free, agreeable because they are nothing, fed by an industrial class they never see.

Why it matters. The danger of elite processing is not cruelty but smoothness: every edge that could snag an idea gets polished away.

The principle. Beware the education that makes you agreeable. If you get along with everyone, you may have nothing left to disagree with.

~00:32

Nothingness, Then Everything

Source fact Tyler asks about her early, middle, and late periods, citing her essay on artistic revival. Paglia rejects the frame: her early period was total failure and flop, then suddenly she burst out like a jack-in-the-box, and it was blabber ever since. No phases. Nothingness, then everything, like a carnival. The late period, still unwritten, is the work no one in Manhattan has the slightest interest in: eight years on Native American cultures at the end of the ice age, as the glacier withdrew. She finds tiny artifacts, reads, and wants to show the religious and metaphysical vision of those cultures against what she calls the politicization of ethnic studies, with its eternal projection of genocide and disaster. Tyler offers a cyclical reading and the relevance of nature. She agrees completely. It is almost early animism, the same perception she loves in Bahia's Yoruba cults: the forces of nature as spirit entities that bring energy or vision.

Nugget 15 · Careers

Nothingness, then everything

What happened. No early, middle, and late periods. Just failure, then eruption. The career does not arc. It detonates.

Why it matters. The phase model flatters the successful and misleads the struggling. Some careers are not symphonies in movements but long silence followed by noise.

The principle. Do not force your life into someone else's arc. If your pattern is nothingness-then-everything, protect the nothingness. The silence is productive.

~00:38

The Earhart Method

Source fact In high school Paglia started a book on Amelia Earhart. At fourteen, after a 1961 Syracuse Herald Journal article, she spent three years researching in the bowels of the Syracuse Library, before microfilm, with the 1930s newspapers still on the shelves. That is how she became a feminist before feminism revived: she discovered the career women of the 1920s and 1930s, Earhart, Dorothy Parker, Dorothy Thompson, Clare Boothe Luce, Margaret Bourke-White, flourishing just after suffrage. When second-wave feminism arrived with Betty Friedan's cofounding of NOW in 1967, she was out of sync: they complained about men, and she would not bad-mouth men. Those earlier women admired men and demanded equal opportunity to achieve at the same level. The clashes were immediate and physical: at a 1971 Yale Law School feminist conference, Rita Mae Brown told her the difference between them was that Paglia wanted to save the universities and Brown wanted to burn them down. Paglia defended the Rolling Stones' Under My Thumb as a work of art against women chanting that nothing demeaning women can be art, which she calls a Stalinist view of art. At Bennington in 1972, a dinner with the new women's studies department collapsed before dessert over hormones: they denied hormones have the slightest impact on human life, even denied they exist.

Source fact At sixteen she wrote a letter to Newsweek, their number-one letter, protesting that Valentina Tereshkova's spaceflight was celebrated on an anniversary of Earhart's ocean flight while Earhart's fight for equal opportunity remained unwon. Her verdict on Gloria Steinem, delivered with glee: a fraud. When Paglia wrote that letter, Steinem ran around New York in a plastic skirt.

Nugget 16 · Research

The Earhart method

What happened. At fourteen, Paglia spent three years in a library basement with unmicrofilmed 1930s newspapers, building a book on Earhart. The research made her a feminist before the movement existed to recruit her.

Why it matters. Primary sources beat manifestos. She arrived at feminism through archives, which is why no movement could later tell her what it meant.

The principle. Do the unfashionable archival work young. It gives you convictions no faction can repossess.

Nugget 17 · Tone

Admire, do not bad-mouth

What happened. The 1920s career women Paglia idolized admired men's achievements and demanded equal opportunity to match them. The second wave's tone, as she heard it, was complaint about men.

Why it matters. Tone is strategy. Admiration recruits allies and sets the bar at excellence. Resentment recruits enemies and sets the bar at grievance.

The principle. If you want a seat at the table, praise the table. Demanding entry while despising the hosts is a losing opening.

~00:43

A Lifestyle of Observation

My substitute for LSD was Indian food

Camille Paglia · Episode ~00:43

Source fact How does she absorb so much culture? Her answer is the episode's title phrase: a lifestyle of observation, twenty-four hours a day. She never goes to conferences, which overlay the same insular ideology. She listens to conversations at the shopping mall. She adores radio, especially sports talk, the only place to hear actual working-class voices call in. Her prose voice comes from that spoken English, high-impact, not from novelists' recherche lingo. Then Tyler quotes her famous line about Indian food as her substitute for LSD, meaning lamb vindaloo. She stands by it. It is a forty-year rut: every Indian restaurant, she vows to try something new, and every time she returns to the vindaloo. It is like ecstasy. She attains nirvana.

Source fact She never took LSD, thank God, and never took drugs: wine and beer have thousands of years behind them. But she remains oriented toward the LSD vision, which she got through music instead: Jefferson Airplane's Bathing at Baxter's, the Byrds' Eight Miles High. She calls what she does psychedelic criticism: metaphysical, visionary, bigger than society.

Source fact That is her charge against Marxism. Arnold Hauser's Social History of Art influenced her enormously, and her work always attends to social context. But Marxism cannot see nature, cannot see the universe. What kind of vision sees only society?

Nugget 18 · Method

A lifestyle of observation

What happened. No conferences, no campus bubble. The mall, the radio, sports talk. Observation runs around the clock, and the writing voice comes from the spoken vernacular, not the seminar room.

Why it matters. Insular inputs produce insular thought. The shopping mall contains more of the culture than the faculty lounge.

The principle. Put your sensors where the people are, not where the prestige is. The method is the address book.

Nugget 19 · Attention

Boredom is a verdict on the observer

What happened. Paglia tells students that no scene is dull, and that boredom indicts the observer. The canceled plane, the fury, then the question: what can I absorb here?

Why it matters. Boredom is usually a verdict on the observer, not the scene. Every experience contains material for the developing system, once the fury passes.

The principle. When boredom strikes, interrogate the observer. The scene is innocent until proven dull.

~00:51

Keep Nefertiti in Berlin

Source fact The cover of Sexual Personae is the Nefertiti bust in Berlin's Neues Museum. Someone scanned it, and soon anyone with a 3-D printer can print a copy. Paglia loves it: archaeology is one of her master tropes. The bust, discovered in 1912, is a century old in the public eye and became a symbol of art itself. But on repatriation her view hardened. Greece and Egypt want their masterpieces back from the world's museums. With ISIS demolishing Palmyra and worse, she says: keep Nefertiti in Berlin, please. Do not send it back to Cairo.

Nugget 20 · Stewardship

Keep Nefertiti in Berlin

What happened. The repatriation argument assumed the home country was the safest keeper. Palmyra's demolition broke the assumption. Paglia reversed her position on the evidence.

Why it matters. Principles about ownership mean little if the object does not survive. Stewardship is a separate question from title, and survival outranks both.

The principle. Update the principle when the facts change. A rule that endangers the treasure is not justice but sentiment.

~00:52

Regrets, Deneuve, and Foucault

Source fact Any aesthetic judgment she fundamentally regrets? She cannot think of one. The early work had decades of second and hundredth thoughts built in. Her unrealized dream: to meet Catherine Deneuve. She did once run into her, smack, on Fifth Avenue in front of Saks, pursued her into the glove department, and forced her to sign a Fillmore East ticket envelope. What she wants now is a civilized conversation. On dating Suzanne Pleshette or Tippi Hedren: she does not date, she is a mad nun. But Hedren invited her to lunch on Rodeo Drive after her book on The Birds, the most elegant and wonderful woman, with a stack of twelve copies to sign. Pleshette she calls underutilized by Hollywood, knife-sharp, and recently compared to Lena Dunham in a Salon column: Pleshette versus Dunham, she says, is the visible measure of the cultural decline.

Source fact Her BFI book on The Birds (1998) was universally panned by film journals for doing nothing: no poststructuralism, no male gaze, just a scene-by-scene attention to the film itself. Slowly it made its way. Even Routledge, the theory house, comes around. Her mission statement for criticism: paying minute attention to the actual work. She is hopelessly old-fashioned. Then the broadside: Foucault is nothing, a windbag, a collection of influences pretending to be a mastermind. His biggest influence, she says, was Goffman of Philadelphia, the sociologist behind The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life. Every gender studies department in America is impregnated with Foucault, and that is why graduates know nothing.

Trap card. The Foucault broadside is Paglia's polemic, not a scholarly consensus. The claim that Foucault merely repackaged Goffman is her argument on air. The episode presents it as her verdict, and this companion reports it as such.

~01:03

Get a Job

Source fact Her life, she says, is completely mundane. She is a schoolmarm. Raised Catholic, she changed nothing when fame arrived at 43: no move to New York, no speakers' bureau, no book parties. The work depends on living close to ordinary life. She contrasts Susan Sontag, who did a little teaching, then floated above the continents as a dirigible luminary, holding court at parties, her work growing less meaningful while people worshipped at the shrine. Try to quote a great Sontag sentence, Paglia says. You cannot. The one quotable line was the one Sontag retracted: "The white race is the cancer of history," withdrawn when she got cancer herself. Against the luminary model, Paglia's: deal with the janitors, the chairs, the curtains. Have a job where frustrations are constant, because that is reality as everybody lives it. Withdrawing from the world to be a writer is a terrible mistake. Herman Melville hunted whales. Wallace Stevens went to the insurance office every day.

Get a job. Have a job.

Camille Paglia, advice to a life in ideas · Episode ~01:03

Source fact The rest of the advice: constantly observe and jot everything down, ideas on tomato-sauced paper, transferred to cards. Follow instinct and keep things on the back burner. Do not read the current theory books. They are incomplete and dull. Believe in the library: Sterling Library at Yale was her shrine. She learned more from old commentators like Frazer's Golden Bough than from anything current. The proof came while researching Glittering Images: 29 chapters, each checked against the full critical record chronologically, and the scholarship visibly collapses from the 1980s on. Solid through the 1960s, holding in the 1970s, then pygmies at the elite schools. The big two-semester art surveys lie dismantled. Graduate students can no longer teach the big picture because all narratives are now called imperialistic fictions.

Nugget 21 · The writer's life

Get a job

What happened. Fame at 43 changed nothing: same schoolmarm life, same janitors and chairs. Sontag's luminary path, parties and lecture circuits, produced work Paglia calls unquotable.

Why it matters. Ordinary friction is material. The writer who withdraws from the world loses the thing worth writing about.

The principle. Keep the real job. Frustration is fieldwork.

~01:08

The Audience Fights Back

Source fact A questioner recalls Smith College in 1993: booing and hissing, a papered walkway of hostile signs. Paglia remembers the treatment of a woman in her forties who had spent twenty years on a book, published by Yale Press, at one of the Seven Sisters. Feminism, she says, moves in phases. She belongs to its dissident wing, suppressed for 25 years, until the pro-sex 1990s broke the Steinem politburo's control, with help from Madonna, who was not even a feminist. Now Steinem is back, like a bad penny, and Paglia feels back to square one. On Bowie: he apparently asked for her phone number in the early 1990s through her publisher, and she laughed it off as a fan's prank. Only later, researching the V&A exhibition essay, did she learn he fired his whole staff and dealt with the world through friends. He wanted to use a Sexual Personae excerpt in a lyric. She regrets the miss but keeps the principle: respectful distance from great artists. Deneuve the performer disappointed up close. Bowie the creator would not have.

Source fact On the pay gap, asked on Equal Pay Day: the Democratic rubric pretends equal pay has not been law since JFK. Real same-job cases are rare. The 72 or 75 cent figure is an overall average: women choose flexible jobs over around-the-clock commission sales, prefer to stay near children, and the gap follows biology. She describes the world of the moms from the inside, as an adoptive mother: the woman becomes the generalissimo of the household, the master strategist, and the husband, who was once her equal, shrinks to one counter in her plan. Asking men to do more, she says, asks what they are not biologically prepared to do. On language: young people's respect for language degenerates. The old newspaper space limit forced crispness, and now the ear goes. On feminism's goals: she is an equal-opportunity feminist, remove the obstacles. But huge areas of life are not political, and legislation is helpless there. Capitalism made women's emancipation possible, whatever the curricula say. And the modern mismatch is structural: for tens of thousands of years mating happened early, Juliet at thirteen going on fourteen. Now women wait through an unnaturally long prelude before they know themselves as women. That is not the patriarchy. It is the machine age.

The episode in seven lines

  1. The large image died by miniaturization, but culture moves in cycles: the small will exhaust itself too.
  2. Brazil understood Paglia instantly, because Carnival and Iguaçu gave it the receptors for artifice and grandeur.
  3. Judge the work, not the consensus: the prequels' volcano climax is high art whether the crowd watched it or not.
  4. Twelve rejections and decades of refusal: the difference between the brilliant and the published is resilience.
  5. The 72-cent figure divides by the wrong denominator. Same-job parity is the honest comparison.
  6. Observation is a lifestyle, not a technique: the mall and the radio beat the conference and the seminar.
  7. Get a job, jot everything, trust the library: ordinary friction is the writer's fieldwork.
