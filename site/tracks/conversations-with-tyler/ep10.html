Episode 10: Niall Ferguson on Doom, History, and Counterfactuals

2021-07-28 · Instructor: Niall Ferguson · Conversations with Tyler

Video not loading? Watch on YouTube

Tyler interviews Niall Ferguson on his book Doom and the philosophy of history.

Why this episode matters

This is the WATCHING GREAT MINDS chapter on thinking in counterfactuals. Ferguson’s new book is Doom: The Politics of Catastrophe, and Tyler uses it to extract a whole philosophy of history: Scottish pessimism as method, Collingwood’s reconstitution of past thought, Taylor’s contrarian music, and the heretical cost-benefit analysis of Empire. You learn why the most likely future is the mundane middle between extinction and the singularity, and why libraries sort honestly while Google sells ads.

Scottish pessimism

English pessimism, in Scottish eyes, does not exist. The English expect to win the World Cup, a hubristic optimism the Scots periodically puncture. “We are doomed” only works in a Scottish accent. The roots: Calvinists versus Anglicans, Glasgow’s West of Scotland upbringing, pessimism drummed in and alleviated only by gallows humor, plus the weather. Hearing “The Sunny Side of the Street” as a boy, Ferguson wondered where that was. Americans see things differently, partly because it is sunnier. At Oxford at 17, the English struck him as far less steeped in doom.

Wales is Scotland lite

Welsh pessimism: “Scotland lite.” The diet version. After two decades in America, the Celtic peripheries look like small differences, “narcissism of small differences,” really one country called Not England. South Wales is the West of Scotland: coal, dead steelworks, same pubs, same talk, none of England’s embarrassment and class. The Welsh do not take their nationalism seriously. The Scots, unfortunately, might get their People’s Republic.

Marx, the pessimist

The most convincing case for British pessimism ever made: Marx. Das Kapital is a prophetic work about England, inspired by the Industrial Revolution in the British Isles. The English obsession with class makes the theory legible: class as the key to history, class conflict as capitalism’s death knell. Hatched in England, more influential than Blake or anyone.

Bureaucracy is not the English pathology. It is a German idea, Prussian, Weber’s. The English-speaking world imported it along with the PhD and the bad parts of academic life. In Britain it arrived late and became instant mockery. The funniest writing on bureaucracy is Yes, Minister. Now it is a general Western democratic pathology of the last 50 years.

Bond, brains, and cyber war

Bond was played by a Scot, Sean Connery, and conceived with a streak of Scottishness. Ferguson’s story: taken unannounced to Connery’s modest bungalow at Lyford Cay, introduced mid-lunch while Connery wore only a sarong. Ferguson mumbled that he learned everything about being a man from Connery’s films in 1970s Glasgow. Connery raised an eyebrow and asked whether Ferguson knew he was a homosexual. Ferguson asked whether the skirt explained it. The ice broke.

Brains over brawn

The point of Bond: Britain has no brawn left, second fiddle to America as in World War II, but it might have brains. Intelligence, spies, precision violence instead of world war. Doctor Who is the same idea: the only superhero who uses his brain, not his muscles, against America’s bodybuilders. Compensate for dwindling economic muscle with superior brains. The embarrassment: the KGB penetrated Britain’s networks better than America’s. Blame Cambridge.

Fleming descends from John Buchan’s Richard Hannay books, The Thirty-Nine Steps onward, “shockers,” ripping yarns not to be taken seriously, with something camp signaled throughout. But the villains matter now: not Soviet employees but semi-freelance criminals, Dr. No, Goldfinger. That is how cyber war works today. Semi-official criminal gangs working with the Kremlin, the Moldovan crew behind the Colonial Pipeline attack. Somewhere, Ferguson hopes, a 007 figure bumps off the ringleaders.

The epistemic crisis

Tyler asks for the fundamental epistemic crisis of modernity. Ferguson: we are haunted by doomsday scenarios, seared into the subconscious by religion even as we call ourselves secular. The end is nigh, in 12 years, no, 10. And we suspect, with Bostrom, that our technologies raised the extinction probability rather than lowering it. Against that, the singularity promise: technologies merging into superhuman beings, extended lifespans, AGI.

Price the middle

Ian Morris framed the choice: extinction-level events or the singularity? Ferguson’s historian instinct: each scenario has very low probability. We should think about the likelier ones between them. Tyler’s essay on the epistemic problem and consequentialism fed this: we need counterfactuals of the future, and we spend too much on the unlikely ends. The outcome will be more mundane. A relief if you feared the end, a disappointment if you awaited the singularity.

Whig or Tory, every morning

The Glorious Revolution counterfactual: everyone should ask each morning, Whig or Tory, Jacobite or not. One line each: Whigs were the parliamentary faction that backed the Protestant succession and the Glorious Revolution settlement. Tories were the faction that defended the crown and the established order. Jacobites were the supporters of the exiled Stuart line, named for James, Jacobus in Latin. The Dutch takeover was bizarre: a Dutch monarch imported at the behest of a faction driven by anti-Catholic prejudice. Ferguson would stand Whig on religious grounds, ardent Protestant Lowlander, drawn to Jacobite romanticism but repelled by the practice.

Scotland was Afghanistan

Read Walter Scott, he says. He avoided Scott as boring until the pandemic, then the Waverley novels laid out the dilemmas of the Glorious Revolution, the Civil War, and the 1745 rising. The key fact: Scotland in the 17th century was Afghanistan. Warring northern clans, a theocracy of extreme Calvinist zealots in the Lowlands, dysfunction, violence, homicide far above England’s. Then, beginning in the late 17th century, Scotland became the most dynamic tiger economy in the world and the cradle of the Enlightenment, the best ideas of Western civilization from a tiny number of people in Glasgow and Edinburgh in a short time. No book properly explains it. But the Whig conclusion stands: English institutions, including the Dutch imports of the Glorious Revolution, helped Scotland out of its Afghan predicament.

The split personality

Scott’s own secret: ambivalence. A one-man Johnson and Boswell, attracted to Jacobite and revolutionary romance, aware you are better off with the sober bourgeois life of Glasgow and Edinburgh. That tension is the culture. Jekyll and Hyde comes from it. Stevenson, Scott’s heir, keeps writing split personalities and fraternal feuds, The Master of Ballantrae. The romantic Tory Catholic versus the rationalist Whig Protestant. The hard-nosed Protestants are unattractive, but entrust the country to romanticism and it reverts to Afghanistan.

Jonathan Clark’s essay in Virtual History, Ferguson’s favorite there, runs the counterfactuals: the contingencies of the 17th and 18th centuries, including avoiding the American Revolution. Clark, a great historian of the generation, was driven into academic exile for being a conservative.

Collingwood and Taylor

Collingwood

The most profound philosopher of history: R. G. Collingwood. The historical act is the reconstitution of past thought from such relics of thought as survive, juxtaposed with the thought of your own time. Not studied for its own sake. Studied for the light it sheds on your predicament. Put best in his 1939 autobiography. Ferguson found it after crossing the Atlantic to Harvard: sudden illumination. Against it stands the modern ethos: condescend to the past with our values and call it insight that past people were racists and sexists.

A. J. P. Taylor: “Men only learn from history how to make new mistakes.” Schoolboy hero. Ferguson applied to Magdalen College thinking Taylor was a fellow. He had left years before, pre-internet. Taylor’s prose belongs with Orwell’s. The paradox-lover, the contrarian. The Origins of the Second World War as polemical masterpiece, The Struggle for Mastery in Europe as tour de force. History as closer to music than science, a line Ferguson loves: historical sensibility like musical sensibility.

Taylor’s miss

Where Taylor’s war book went wrong: the contrarian method plus strict adherence to diplomatic documents produced Hitler the traditional German statesman, the war as accident and misunderstanding. The 1938 to 1939 account is largely right, but it underplays the ideological, the diabolical, the catastrophic impulses that were primary. The antidote: Michael Burleigh’s The Third Reich: A New History, which gets the messianic, political-religious Hitler, the redeemer leading Germans into the abyss a second time. Kershaw’s Hitler reads like a negligent colleague at a provincial university. Only Burleigh captures the terrifying star quality.

Taylor on Kissinger’s A World Restored: fantastically scathing, by chance discovery. The flourishes are not anchored in the documents. The Rankean in Taylor revolted. Rankean means loyal to Leopold von Ranke: history built from primary documents, with no invention.

Quentin Skinner taught Ferguson how to be patronized: at Cambridge high table, intellectual history at the top of the great chain of being, economic history the bottom feeders. But Skinner’s contextualizing was admirable: do not read texts as stone tablets. Understand Machiavelli inside his Florence.

Keynes: awe and the parting

Tocqueville

The strongest defense of liberalism: Tocqueville, not Mill, not Hayek. An Oxford story: L’Ancien Regime in French, first term, hard work with weak French, conversations with tutor Angus Macintyre and near-contemporary Andrew Sullivan. The insight: liberty needs protection by nonobvious means. Things a liberal might not approve of. Democracy in America then showed the project: why France failed to be the United States, the institutional supports of American liberty. Ferguson is a philosophical ignoramus by his own account. He needs a story, and Tocqueville’s story of 18th-century France clicked.

Keynes inspires awe. The General Theory was the hardest thing Ferguson ever read: cover to cover, three times, with notes. The parting: Bloomsbury’s iconoclasm. Keynes and Strachey despised the Victorians, and Keynes subtly dismantled the Victorian achievement, the gold standard, free trade, posterity. Not that Keynes was a socialist. But the demolition cleared the path for a failed socialist experiment, Labour dominance, postwar stagnation. Ferguson is the worse aesthete with his own Bloomsbury network, so aesthetics is not the split. The split is the Victorians.

Two Keynes episodes

Two Keynes episodes matter. Paper and Iron caught the young Keynes advising Weimar on reparations, badly, then distancing himself after the 1923 catastrophe. And the redeeming heroism: Skidelsky’s third volume on keeping Britain in the game in 1945 against Roosevelt’s advisers, who were strong enough to dismantle the Empire on the spot. By the end, Skidelsky shows, Keynes was not a Keynesian and regarded self-described Keynesians with skepticism.

The heretical Empire

The heresy, held 20 years: the benefits of the British Empire outweighed the costs. Rigorously done, it was remarkably benign compared with the available alternatives, and the counterfactual is the whole argument. The left’s comparison to the Third Reich is a category error without a realistic counterfactual. Empire could not be published today. Bruce Gilley’s unpublished article makes the book’s argument without citing it.

The economic case

The economic case: the 19th-century empire became an empire of liberalism. Free trade, free migration, free capital movement, and abolition before the others. Against indigenous empires, the actual alternative, it was a positive force, more so in the 20th century.

The India challenge

Tyler’s India challenge: post-independence growth beat imperial growth, and public goods got little. Ferguson: Tirthankar Roy wrote the best book on this. Large-scale infrastructure investment, railroads, telegraphs, docks, vastly more than Qing China. Oriental empires invested far less. The real defect: nothing for primary education, only elite training to staff the empire. That shortfall is part of why India stayed poor. And the counterfactual cannot be the 1990s reforms in the 1890s. Compare comparable geographies in the 1890s.

Coda: Snow Crash, Mexico, and succession

Snow Crash and succession

Most plausible sci-fi dystopia: Neal Stephenson’s Snow Crash. We spend half our lives online, our avatars have more fun than we do, California breaks down, migrant flotillas arrive. Brilliant.

Mexico City: he went to see his daughter, unseen for 18 months. He learned anyway: the left’s populism will be rewarded more than the right’s in Latin America. Mexico recovers on America’s coattails despite bad excess mortality. Brazil suffers on, and Bolsonaro takes the blame. Mexico City seemed more okay than expected.

The future: no more books after the second Kissinger volume. Seventeen is enough, and nobody reads 400-page volumes all the way through anymore. The Kissinger book is worth it. Central Committee transcripts nobody touched before, a fresh US-China illumination from the 1970s. After that, institution building and intellectual succession. Academic life went off the rails since the 1980s. New institutions are urgently needed. “That should strike terror in my enemies’ hearts.”

Smaller verified pleasures: Boswell over Johnson, because Ferguson is Kissinger’s Boswell, trotting along writing down the aphorisms. Johnson has the better lines. Boswell deserves the credit for them. Alasdair Gray over Irvine Welsh. Lanark is a great book. Lennon over McCartney. He cried when Lennon died, punk rock liberated him from an unmusical family, Pink Floyd sent him into a coma, and there is no best American punk band. The Kelvin Bridge near Glasgow Academy, walked in all weathers. The Mitchell Library’s Thirty Years’ War shelf, hundreds of books, the moment history gripped him: libraries sort honestly, Google sorts to sell ads.

Questions and answers

What is Collingwood's method, step by step?

Gather the relics of past thought. Reconstitute the thought of the dead. Juxtapose it with the thought of your own time. Ask what light it sheds on your predicament. Never study the past for its own sake, and never condescend to it with modern values. The past is an instrument for seeing the present.

Follow-up: Take one decision you face. Reconstitute what someone in 1920 thought about an analogous choice. Juxtapose. What does their thought reveal about yours?
Why does Ferguson call the singularity and extinction both unlikely?

Because the historian’s instinct prices the middle. Doomsday scenarios are seared in by religion and haunt even the secular. Bostrom’s risks are real but the extreme ends of the distribution are not where the mass is. The mundane middle, relief or disappointment, is the base rate.

Follow-up: Take your own biggest fear and biggest hope. Assign each a probability. Now price the middle. Where did your attention go wrong?
What went wrong in Taylor's Hitler account?

Method met contrarianism. Strict diplomatic documents plus the urge to flip the table produced Hitler the traditional statesman and war by misunderstanding. The documents could not show the messianic, political-religious drive. Burleigh’s antidote: the redeemer with terrifying star quality, leading Germany into the abyss twice.

Follow-up: What do your sources systematically hide? Name the drive your documents cannot see. Who is your Burleigh?
What is the Empire argument, in one paragraph?

Cost-benefit against realistic counterfactuals, not against utopia or the Third Reich. The 19th-century empire ran free trade, free migration, free capital, and abolition, and built more infrastructure than the indigenous alternatives. It failed primary education badly. Net, against what was available, benign. The method is the point: no counterfactual, no verdict.

Follow-up: Take your most unfashionable belief. Run the counterfactual audit. What is the realistic alternative, and what does it cost?
Why Whig, in the Glorious Revolution?

Because Scotland was Afghanistan and became the Enlightenment. Warring clans and a Calvinist theocracy gave way to a tiger economy and the best ideas of the West in one short burst. English and Dutch institutions did that. Romantic Jacobitism is attractive and would revert the country. The sober bourgeois bet paid.

Follow-up: What is your Afghanistan-to-Enlightenment case? What institutions did the work? Who wants the romance back?
What is the Bond theory of British decline?

No brawn left, so brains instead. Second fiddle to America. Superior at intelligence and precision violence. Doctor Who is the same theory in science fiction. The theory flatters. The KGB penetration embarrasses. Decline managed by cleverness is still decline, just better dressed.

Follow-up: Where are you compensating for lost brawn with brains? Is the compensation real or costume?
Why libraries over Google?

Sorting. Libraries sort to connect the book in your hands with its relatives. Google sorts to sell ads. The Thirty Years’ War shelf taught Ferguson history’s scale: hundreds of books on one topic, honestly arranged. Cataloging is civilization’s underrated infrastructure.

Follow-up: Audit your information diet by sorting motive. What percentage is sorted to sell you something? Rebuild one shelf honestly.

How to imbibe this

  • This week: run Collingwood on one live problem. Find one relic of past thought about it, a letter, a memo, a diary. Reconstitute the thinking. Juxtapose with yours. Write what it illuminates.
  • Catch yourself: the next time you compare something to the worst case, stop. Name the realistic counterfactual. No counterfactual, no verdict.
  • Catch yourself: the next time you price the future, check whether you priced the middle. Extremes are vivid. The middle is likely.
  • Behavior change per major idea:
  • Counterfactuals: for every historical claim you make this month, state the alternative explicitly. If you cannot, drop the claim.
  • Reading: read one Waverley novel or one Taylor polemic. Note the ambivalence, the music.
  • Libraries: rebuild one information source on honest sorting. Remove the ad motive.
  • Succession: spend one hour on institution building for every four on output.
  • Monthly: ask Whig or Tory about one live political question. Then ask what the Dutch institutions would do.

Think it yourself

Unaided first-principles reasoning. No answers shown.

  1. Price three futures for your industry: extinction, singularity, mundane middle. Assign probabilities. Defend the middle against someone who only sees the ends.
  2. Run Collingwood on a decision you made last year. What did the people of 1920 think about the analogous choice? What does the juxtaposition reveal?
  3. Take your most unfashionable belief. Build the counterfactual audit: realistic alternative, costs, benefits. Where is it weakest?
  4. Identify your Afghanistan-to-Enlightenment case: a system that transformed fast without a good explanation. What institutions did the work?
  5. Audit your sources like Taylor’s documents: what drive are they systematically blind to? Who writes your Burleigh?
  6. Design the institution you would build with Ferguson’s remaining decades. What does it correct that universities no longer do?

Skills you can now use

  • Run Collingwood’s reconstitution on any problem.
  • Price futures across the full distribution, not the ends.
  • Demand realistic counterfactuals before verdicts.
  • Separate pessimism as method from doom as marketing.
  • Read sources for what they systematically hide.
  • Sort information honestly, like a catalog.
  • Ask Whig or Tory every morning.
  • Build institutions, not just output.
