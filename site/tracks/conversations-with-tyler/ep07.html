Episode 7: Steven Pinker on Language, Reason, and the Enlightenment

2016-11-02 · Instructor: Steven Pinker · Conversations with Tyler

Tyler interviews Steven Pinker on linguistics, rationality, and progress.

Why this episode matters

This is the WATCHING GREAT MINDS chapter on reason itself. Tyler thinks Pinker believes in reason more than he does, and the episode is a friendly duel about it. Pinker answers with his best line: “What are we doing here if you do not believe in reason? Why do we not have an arm wrestle or a beauty contest?” You learn the two-machine theory of language, the Hayekian economics of irregular verbs, the $10,000 Hiroshima test of optimism, and why Pinker calls group selection a big blunder.

Two machines: memory and rules

Pinker chose irregular verbs because they reveal cognitive organization. The two mechanisms behind language:

One, rote memory: arbitrary pairings between a sound and a meaning, stored by brute force. The word duck does not look, walk, or quack like a duck. Everyone memorized the pairing.

Two, the rule system: combine pieces with algorithms that compute meaning from parts and arrangement. Add -ed to the end, and walk becomes walked.

Irregular verbs are the war zone where the two compete. Stride, strode, come, came, sing, sang, go, went: about 165 quirky exceptions in English, each memorized. Walk, walked: the algorithm.

Method Rote, arbitrary pairing Algorithm, computed

The Hayekian erosion

The erosion machine

Tyler spots the economics immediately. Irregular verbs tend to be common verbs. Common means heard constantly, which means memorized easily. Rare verbs fall below the memory threshold, and children default to the all-purpose add -ed rule. Dreamt becomes dreamed. The stock of irregulars erodes as it filters through children’s minds.

Beauty as brake

Beauty slows the erosion. Irregulars are more euphonious: they fit the phonological template of a normal word, a nickname, a common word. Regulars can be kludgy, an -ed suffix as detritus on the end. Sixths and edited are near-unpronounceable because the suffix wrecks the word’s contour. Poets and lyricists prefer the irregular when there is a choice: strode over strided, strove over strived.

No one planned any of this. That is the Hayekian part. Millions of speakers make tiny adjustments. Kids learn. Tradeoffs balance. The language spontaneously evolves its optimum without a designer.

Too many or too few?

Tyler’s social question: in Orwell’s 1984, the Party bans all irregular verbs as excess regulation. Too many or too few? Pinker: he likes them and wants more. Losing them is sad. Occasionally a new one catches on, snuck, by parasitizing the memory strength of phonological neighbors. Tyler’s own phobia: abide, abode, abidden, abidance, all avoided by circumlocution. Pinker notes abode has not been in common usage for a few centuries. Chid, chidden, holp as the past of help, all gone, surviving only in Appalachia and remote British dialects. The three-way sink, sank, sunk gives way to shrunk, stunk. Honey, I Shrunk the Kids works. Honey, I Shrank the Kids never would.

Chomsky as a moving target

Tyler, the outsider economist: Chomsky’s universal grammar promised a definite structure, then collapsed into recursion, which feels almost tautological. Was the hypothesis simply wrong?

Pinker: the hypothesis is hard to pin down because Chomsky revises his theory every decade, “on a principle of Mao’s Continuous Revolution.” It was never specified precisely nor field-tested against language variation, “which I think is unfortunate in terms of ordinary scientific practice.”

The salvageable core

The salvageable core: children are biased to analyze speech in particular ways. They do not record sentences verbatim. The algorithmic half pulls combinatorial rules from the stream. Children are keyed to look for certain rules and elements. That set of abilities is what Pinker would call universal grammar.

The commonalities across languages come from generation: every generation of children constructs language anew from parental data. The mind makes the pattern, not the world.

Modules, faculties, organs

Haidt’s modules, Fodor’s metaphor, Gardner’s multiple intelligences, the Swiss Army knife, now a smartphone with apps: Tyler asks how unified cognition is.

Organs, not apps

Pinker: modules never quite fit. Modules are snap-in components with narrow channels. Too strong. The mind has structure and specialization without being independent parts. His preferred metaphor: organs and tissues. Blood was taught as an organ. It suffuses every tissue, no dotted line around it, not rump roast versus flank steak. The mind has specializations that interpenetrate.

He agrees with Haidt’s weaker claim: different mindsets, different faculties, analyze the same events differently. Much political disagreement is a fight over which frame to use. Acknowledge the multiplicity without the strict module metaphor.

Consciousness: the wall

Tyler wants the hard problem: why does it feel like something to be neurons? Could there be zombies that act identically with nobody home?

Pinker separates two questions: self-concept, self-knowledge, versus subjective experience, qualia. The robot that monitors itself but feels nothing is conceivable for the first. The second is the puzzle.

Mysterianism

His position: mysterianism, a misleading name. Colin McGinn is its modern champion. Nagel’s “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?” captures it. There may be facts that are true and unsatisfying to intuition forever. The mind reflecting on itself will naturally be puzzled by some aspects of itself. “Here I am inside me and almost by definition some things about the view of me inside me will resist it that the me doing the view cannot articulate.”

The guardrails: no ESP, no life after death, no action at a distance. All information processing and neurons. Dennett’s denial of the question’s coherence is one exit. Smuggling the soul back in is not, because the mind shows no nonmaterial powers. Other walls: the nature of time, what was before the Big Bang, finite versus infinite universe. No reason to expect every aspect of reality to be intuitive.

Determinism without despair

Tyler presses: everything in the mind has a physical correlate, so the mind is fully determined?

Determined but unpredictable

Pinker: in theory, perhaps. But processes are chaotic, nonlinear. Ions zig instead of zag from Brownian motion or quantum phenomena. The system is physically determined but practically random, like weather to several higher degrees. Laplace’s demon could in principle predict us, but some things true in theory are “so mind-bogglingly complicated that they may as well not be true in theory.” No miracle in the brain when we decide. In that sense determined. For all intents and purposes, not.

The underlying unity

Tyler offers his synthesis of Pinker’s whole corpus: the midway position. Reject the blank slaters who see no structure. Reject the over-structurers, early Chomsky included. Stake out room for reason within the laws of nature. A voluntarist, pro-reason, pro-science Enlightenment for the 21st century.

Down a level, not halfway

Pinker: close, but not Goldilocks. The truth is not always the arithmetic mean of extremes. The move is to go down a level to finer-grain causal mechanisms, stating something more precise than either extreme. Language is not innate or learned. There is an innate structure that does the learning. Learning does not happen by magic. What is innate is an ability to learn. Once you characterize the learning mechanism’s computational architecture, the nature-nurture question dissolves.

The Enlightenment core: the world is intelligible, progress in understanding and rational action is possible, pointedly including ourselves. Human nature is real and studiable. Studying it lets us discount our own illusions and learn what gives fulfillment.

The reason duel

Tyler’s counter: he is more Hayekian, more skeptical. If we cannot understand truly fundamental things, the confusion bleeds into everything. Reason is fairly weak. Passions rule.

Pinker’s counter-counter. First: “What are we doing here if you do not believe in reason? Why do we not have an arm wrestle or a beauty contest?” Asking the question commits you to reason. Second: Hume’s reason-as-slave-to-passions is a conceptual point, not a psychological one. It says you cannot deduce goals from beliefs, a category distinction. It does not say people are permanently irrational. People are corrigible: science, debate, confrontation with reality. Third: the record. No more human sacrifice. No virgins into volcanoes. Few hereditary monarchies, and the remaining ones keep the pageantry without the law-making. The frontal cortex is grafted onto the brain. We count to 10, save for a rainy day, hold our horses. Not uniformly, not reliably, but enough to celebrate and encourage.

Underrated, overrated

Tyler runs the lightning round.

Lightning round: rap, exercise, behavioral economics

Rap music: Pinker never got it, but he will not call it overrated. He defended black English against William F. Buckley on Firing Line. The grammars of vernaculars are equally rule-governed. No objective ranking exists. But a standard is good for communication. History could have made black English the standard.

Aerobic exercise: underrated. He jogs and cycles.

Behavioral economics: for it, but too quick to dismiss classical economics. Another false dichotomy. Individual quirks do not kill collective rationality: a system running by rules can be rational though no single mind is. And real money cures the lab fallacies. “You really cannot turn a person into a money pump.”

Lightning round: the passive, Shatner, Sontag

The passive voice: underrated. No construction survives 1,500 years without serving a purpose. The rule: start with the given, the topic, end with the new, the focus. The mime in the park gets pelted with zucchini: the mime is the topic, so the passive opens. Orwell and Strunk and White both use the passive in the very sentence that bans it.

William Shatner: underrated, fellow Montreal Jew. Not his singing.

Sontag’s photography passage: overrated. Photography is many things. First, a simulacrum of perceptual experience: vision makes hypotheses from a two-dimensional array of light, and the photograph duplicates that array with pigment or LEDs. The art is the tension between the thing out there and the two-dimensional patchwork as an aesthetic object.

The $10,000 Hiroshima question

Tyler’s stress test. Suppose anyone could blow up a significant part of a major city, Hiroshima-scale, for $10,000 and a few months. Seven billion people. How long before someone does it?

Two weak premises

Pinker’s answer is honest: he does not know. Then he attacks Tyler’s chain at two premises.

Premise one: destruction always gets cheaper exponentially. Not always. Plane travel leveled off after 1957, even got slightly slower. A $10,000 nuke still needs thousands of centrifuges. Premise two: desire. “The rate-limiting step on terrorist destruction is how many people think that it is a good idea to cause a lot of damage for no particular reason.” Violence is not limited by technology costs. It is limited by the number of people who want pointless destruction. Nukes have gone 70-plus years unused in war because a radioactive crater is not a coherent war goal. They deter existential threats. They do not win battles.

Fermi and the parochial primate

On the Fermi paradox, same logic. No natural arc toward civilizational self-destruction. War is stupid: you blow things up, kill people, and gain nothing you could not get another way. Advanced civilizations can build the functional equivalents of world government, norms, the UN, expectations. Calling primate anger the only path to intelligence is parochial.

Biggest existential worries: climate change and nuclear war. Climate change would be miserable and could make civilization unrecognizable, but it would not extinguish the species. People can move, adjust, build.

The euphemism treadmill

The treadmill

From audience Q&A. Politically motivated language change works on open-class words, nouns and verbs, but not on closed-class items like pronouns, articles, tense markers. Gender-neutral pronoun proposals date to the 1970s. None caught on. Grammar is learned early, high-frequency, distributed across millions of speakers. It resists engineering.

The euphemism treadmill: negro was respectful through the 1970s, King used it, the United Negro College Fund bore it. Then black, then African American, driven by Jesse Jackson in the 1990s. The mechanism: attitudes do not follow labels. Change the label and the new label absorbs the emotional aura of the concept. Asian replaced Oriental and stayed put, perhaps because less prejudice needed refreshing. African American has held 20-plus years without a replacement, which may mean prejudice is weaker now.

On Trump-era speech: Pinker doubts attitudes changed. Google searches for racist joke terms, Seth Stephens-Davidowitz’s barometer, show no U-turn. The shift is permission, not belief: people who hid attitudes feel allowed to voice them. Taboos broke. He hopes for a benign hypocrisy: some things you just do not say in public, because saying them legitimates them. Whether the taboos reassert themselves is too early to tell.

Blunders, fashion, and critical periods

Blunders and fashion

Group selection: “a big blunder.” Search “false allure of group selection.” No Darwinian process of differential survival applies to groups the way it applies to genes. On violence declining genetically: Germany went from the most militaristic to the most pacifist culture in a generation. The US homicide rate halved in eight years. Too fast for natural selection. No genetic change needed.

Fashion: Quentin Bell’s theory via Veblen. Elites once differentiated through sumptuosity, unfakeable fine fabrics. Cheap manufacturing killed that. Now the game is frequency-dependent selection: when everyone has long hair, show up with a crew cut. Fat lapels versus skinny lapels. The look trickles down to Target, the elite jump again, churning forever. What fashion communicates: difference from everyone else, plus the impunity to pull it off.

Critical periods

Critical periods: real, plural. Phonology and accent are most sensitive, Chinese tones included. Vocabulary has no critical period. We learn words all our lives. Syntax sits between. Grammar mastery starts declining around age 15. The brain change behind it is unknown.

Dreams are not prophetic: “a kind of screen saver.” Physics rules it out.

Language extinction is real: 90 percent of languages may die, leaving about 600. But Spanish, Hindi, Russian, Chinese are safe, and translation software plus national pride prevent one global tongue.

CRISPR babies: idle speculation. Traits sit on thousands of genes, each shifting the trait a smidgen, with mixed positive and negative effects. No gene for musical ability. A denser neural network can be stupider. Risk aversion alone stops it. People fear modified tomatoes. They will not gamble with children.

The next book: The New Enlightenment: A Manifesto for Science, Reason, Humanism, and Progress. The Enlightenment philosophers got much right, their dreams hold true, and well-being measured properly rises.

Questions and answers

Explain the two-machine theory of language in one example.

Sing, sang is the memory machine, a stored arbitrary pairing. Walk, walked is the rule machine, the add -ed algorithm. The 165 irregulars are where memory wins. Everything else is where the rule wins. The boundary moves with frequency. Drop below the memory threshold and the rule takes over, dreamt to dreamed.

Follow-up: Find one irregular in your own speech you use less and less. Is it frequency or beauty keeping it alive? What would its regularization cost you?
What is Hayekian about irregular verbs?

No designer. Millions of speakers make small adjustments, children filter the stock, tradeoffs between memory cost and euphony balance out. The system finds its optimum spontaneously. Order without a planner, in grammar.

Follow-up: Name one system in your work that everyone assumes was designed. Trace three of its features to unplanned local adjustments. Who is the Hayek in that story?
What is the flaw in Tyler's $10,000 Hiroshima chain?

Two weak premises. Cost does not always fall exponentially. Planes leveled off, nukes still need centrifuges. And desire is the rate-limiting step. Violence is constrained by how many people want pointless destruction, not by what they can afford. The pessimist’s chain assumes physics decides. Pinker says psychology decides.

Follow-up: Take your own worst-case scenario. Separate the cost curve from the desire curve. Which one is actually doing the work in your fear?
Why is the middle position not the arithmetic mean?

Because averaging extremes teaches nothing. The move is downward: find finer-grain causal mechanisms beneath the dichotomy and state something more precise than either side. Language is not half innate, half learned. There is an innate structure that does the learning. The dichotomy dissolves instead of splitting.

Follow-up: Take a debate you are stuck in. Instead of compromising, go one level down. What mechanism would make both sides partially right? Write it in three sentences.
How does the euphemism treadmill work?

Attitudes stain labels, not the reverse. Negro was respectful. Prejudice stained it. Black replaced it. Stained again. African American replaced that. It holds, possibly because prejudice is weaker now. You cannot launder a concept by renaming it while the underlying attitude persists.

Follow-up: Find a relabeling in your organization. Did the attitude follow the name or stain it? What would prove the attitude actually moved?
Why is group selection a blunder, in one argument?

Speed. Germany flipped from militaristic to pacifist in a generation. The US homicide rate halved in eight years. Genes do not move that fast. Whatever changed, it was not the gene pool. Invoking selection for a change selection cannot pace is the blunder.

Follow-up: Take any claim of recent human evolution toward something. Check the timescale. If the change is faster than a generation, what is the real mechanism?
What is the practical upshot of the critical-period findings?

Start sound early or accept the accent. Phonology is the most age-sensitive system. Vocabulary never closes. Syntax sits between, with grammar mastery falling from about 15. The State Department’s 35-year-old beginners fight biology. The policy moral: expose children early, and do not expect adult drills to fix phonology.

Follow-up: What skill of yours has a critical period you missed? What is the accent you still carry in it? Compensate with the system that has no critical period.

How to imbibe this

  • This week: run the two-machine audit on one of your systems. Which parts are memorized exceptions, which are rules? Find one exception below the frequency threshold and regularize it deliberately. Note the cost.
  • Catch yourself: the next time you split a difference, stop. Go one level down instead. Name the mechanism that makes both sides partially right. If you cannot find it, admit the split was empty.
  • Catch yourself: the next time you relabel something to fix it, check the treadmill. Is the attitude moving or just the name? Demand the attitude’s evidence.
  • Behavior change per major idea:
  • Reason: before a hard decision, ask Pinker’s question. If you think it through, you stand committed to reason. Finish the job properly.
  • Erosion: protect your beautiful irregulars. The distinctive practices that survive are the euphonious ones. Make your best habits the prettiest ones.
  • Desire over cost: in every risk assessment, separate the cost curve from the desire curve. Fear the desire side more.
  • Tribalism: build one rule this month that makes a wrong belief embarrassing to hold, in your team or your own notes.
  • Monthly: reread one Pinker chapter and one critic. Practice the downward move: what mechanism dissolves the fight?

Think it yourself

Fermi and journaling with less scaffolding. No answers shown.

  1. Fermi: 165 irregulars, children filtering. Estimate how many irregulars English will have in 2100. What is the half-life of an irregular verb? Show the arithmetic.
  2. Journal: write your midway position on one live debate. Then delete the compromise and go one level down. What mechanism did you find?
  3. Take Tyler’s $10,000 question and replace Hiroshima with your industry’s nightmare. What are the two weak premises? Which one is weaker?
  4. Run the treadmill test on three corporate relabelings you know firsthand. Which ones moved attitudes? What is the evidence?
  5. Design the embarrassment rule: what norm would make false beliefs costly in your circle? Who enforces it? What breaks first?
  6. Audit your modules: which faculty runs your political judgments? Which runs your technical ones? When did the wrong one take over?

Skills you can now use

  • Split language, and any system, into memory versus rule machines.
  • Price irregularity by frequency. Predict erosion.
  • Dissolve a dichotomy by going one level down to mechanism.
  • Separate cost curves from desire curves in risk assessment.
  • Detect the euphemism treadmill in organizational relabeling.
  • Reject group selection by checking timescales.
  • Defend reason by pointing at the act of reasoning itself.
  • Use the passive correctly: topic first, focus last.

Reason as a pure faculty or a lost cause. Blank slate versus over-structured mind, prophecy versus despair. The frontal cortex, the embarrassment rule, the count to 10. Reason as a graft: weak, corrigible, enough. Mechanisms under dichotomies, desire over cost, attitudes over labels.

Tradeoff: the graft never becomes the tree, which is why it needs rules and why it works. Footer: Pinker’s production function is the downward move to mechanism, repeated across language, mind, and history.
