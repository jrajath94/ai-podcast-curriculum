Episode 2: Cal Newport's Planning System (In Detail)

2022-09-02 · Instructor: Cal Newport · Deep Questions with Cal Newport

The root document, the three categories (core documents, productivity, discipline), and why he returns to this system every fall.

Why this episode matters

No other episode in this track gives you the machinery that makes deep work possible. Deep work is the engine. This episode is the chassis, the fuel system, and the maintenance schedule. Every later chapter tells you to protect focus and work slowly and think ahead. None of that survives without a system that turns values into a weekly plan, a weekly plan into a daily time block, and a daily time block into what you do right now. This chapter is that system.

The fall epiphany

Stress, lost sleep, and the summer laxness

A few days before recording, Cal got stressed and anxious enough to lose a lot of sleep. His mind would not shut down at night. The trigger was simple. The fall semester approached.

Cal is a professor and a writer. In summer he has little to do, so he goes lax on his systems. He takes his foot off the gas of his organizational machinery. Over that summer he accreted new side systems, new disciplines, new ideas, new projects. Notes about all of it were spread across many digital media and several notebooks.

Then came the reckoning. He had to wrangle everything and lock his systems back in for the fall. It would not work. Some pieces were redundant. Some did not make sense. There were too many initiatives. The mess stressed him out to the point of insomnia.

The epiphany he has every fall

The next morning came the epiphany, and Cal puts quotation marks around the word, because it is the exact same epiphany he has every single fall. The planning system he perfected over a decade is what works for him. Every time he tries to reinvent the wheel or bolt new components onto it, he gets stressed.

So he did what he does every fall. Forget the new inventions. Go back to the standard planning system. Get every piece of it up and running. He felt much better.

That system, he says, underlies almost everything he has done professionally in the last decade. Most of his books. Most of his academic work. It all happened because of this planning system.

The root document

Cal keeps a document he calls the root document, or the core document. It is the single source from which everything he does comes. The principle behind it is his own phrase: rooted productivity. Everything is written down, and you know where to find it. It is not floating in your head.

He read the actual document on the show. The wording is imperfect, unpolished, written for himself. That is a feature. It is not an essay for publication. He knows what it means.

The document divides his system into three categories. Everything related to his core planning falls under one of them: core documents, productivity, discipline.

Category 1: core documents

Values, and two strategic plans

The first category holds three kinds of documents. First, a values document describing his roles and the values by which he tries to live. Second, a career strategic plan. Third, a personal strategic plan. Each plan lays out his current thinking, experimental systems, and plans for living true to his values in that part of life.

There is a fourth kind, used as needed. Extended plans. If a big project or initiative needs its own description, it gets its own document, linked from the relevant strategic plan.

How the core documents are maintained

Maintenance has three rhythms, and each is explicit.

First, once a week he looks at his values and produces what he calls a values plan. This is the week’s emphasis. Which values need attention. Where he slipped. Which habits would emphasize a particular value. His example: community connection matters, so this week he will call someone every day. The values plan also holds his best practices for mental health, the things that keep his mind sharp and away from anxiety.

Second, once a week he reviews the strategic plans, and he can tweak them at any time. But at minimum, at the beginning of each new semester, he overhauls them. The plans are written for a semester at a time.

Third, he keeps an idea notebook, digital and analog. He uses Obsidian (a note-taking app) and a Moleskine. Ideas live there, and at the very least when he does the semester updates he goes through the ideas and acts on the ones that matter.

Category 2: productivity

The weekly plan

Each week he builds a weekly plan. The inputs are fixed: the strategic plans, the calendar, the task list, and the values plan. The format is not. A complicated week in the middle of the semester might become an intricate jenga game of fitting everything together. A week in July might say nothing more than an exclamation point.

The weekly plan answers one question. Given everything I value and everything on my plate, how do I attack this week.

The daily plan and the cascade

Each day he reviews three things: the weekly plan, the values plan, and the calendar. On a weekday he then makes a time block plan. Every hour of the day gets an assignment. On weekends he does something looser, a quick sketch of what he works on and what to remember.

The crucial idea is the cascade. The strategic plan influences the weekly plan. The weekly plan feeds the daily plan. The daily plan determines what you do right now. So what you do right now is shaped by your big-picture strategic plans, but you never have to think about your big-picture plans right now. The thinking happens at the right level and flows downhill.

The shutdown ritual

Two more pieces complete the productivity category. First, clear work shutdowns, with a shutdown complete ritual. Work ends at a defined point. There is a clear separation between work and non-work. He makes a rough but intentional plan for the rest of the day when he shuts down.

Second, full capture, in the tradition of David Allen. Capture everything. The whole game is trust. If he writes something down, he trusts it will be seen, processed, and routed. A calendar appointment goes on the calendar. A task goes on the task list. A thought about the plan updates the weekly plan. Because the capture is complete, he does not have to carry anything in his mind. In the moment he worries only about what he does now. In the evening, only about the relaxing thing he chose.

Category 3: discipline

Hard boundaries, tracked with metrics

The third category is an evolving list of core disciplines. Hard boundaries he strictly follows to lay a foundation for a deep life. Exercise. A number of deep work hours per day. In sales it might be the number of calls per day. Whatever the domain, the form is the same: I do this, I do that, I do the other thing, and I always do those things.

He often tracks these with metrics. During an academic semester he keeps a metric code for each discipline in his time block planner: did I do this today, did I do this today. In summer or over breaks he may take a break from tracking.

The discipline category was the last thing added to the system over the decade. And the reason is personal. For him, if something is not written down, he does not fully trust it, and then he gets anxious. Other people may not need this. He does.

The system expands and contracts

The final property Cal emphasizes is flexibility. The system scales. In complicated periods the documents get big. Strategic plans grow. Task lists grow. Weekly plans become epic essays. Extended plans get linked in.

In hard periods the system contracts to the basics. Burnout, despair, difficult stretches. Then it is just the values, the core things that must get done, the minimum that pulls him out of the depths. One system, two sizes.

He is honest about the rough edges. Some pieces are redundant. The values plan probably should not be separate from the weekly planning. There are little legacy incongruities. It does not matter. The parts mingle, the rhythms hold, and a decade of output is the evidence.

How to imbibe this

Write your root document this week. One page. Three headings: core documents, productivity, discipline. Under each, write what you actually do today, even if it is thin. Imperfect wording is fine. It is for you. The document is the system. The system is the document.

Start the weekly rhythm. Pick one day, Friday afternoon or Sunday, and build a weekly plan from four inputs: your strategic direction, your calendar, your task list, and what matters this week. Do not aim for elegance. Aim for one honest page about how you will attack the week.

Time block tomorrow morning. Before the day starts, assign every hour. Mark which blocks are deep work. When the day drifts, redraw the blocks. The plan is a living document, not a cage.

Run a shutdown ritual tonight. Close the loops. Capture anything floating. Say a phrase, check a box, close the laptop. Then make a rough intentional plan for the evening. When you are shut down, you are shut down.

Track one discipline. Pick one hard boundary: exercise, deep work hours, pages written. Track it daily for this week only. Notice what the tracking does to your trust in yourself.

Catch the invention urge. The episode’s deepest warning is the summer pattern: stressed, so invent a new system, which creates more stress. When you feel the urge to redesign everything, that is your signal. Return to the standard system instead.

Think it yourself

Guided drills. Do these with your own brain before touching AI.

Drill 1, write your values paragraph. Describe your roles and the values by which you try to live, in your own imperfect words. Then write this week’s values plan: which one value needs emphasis, and what is one habit that would emphasize it. Include one mental health practice.

Drill 2, build one weekly plan. Gather the four inputs: your direction for the season, your calendar, your task list, your values plan. Write the attack plan for this week in one page. If it is a light week, an exclamation point is allowed.

Drill 3, trace the cascade. Pick what you do right now. Trace it upward: which daily block is it in, which weekly plan chose that block, which strategic plan shaped the week, which value sits at the top. If the chain breaks anywhere, write the missing link. That gap is where your system leaks.

Drill 4, audit your capture. For one day, write down every obligation that crosses your mind on a single sheet. At day’s end, count how many have a trusted home in a system and how many live only in your head. The second number is your anxiety inventory.

Skills you can now use

  • Write a root document that summarizes your entire planning system on one page.
  • Maintain core documents at three cadences: weekly values plan, weekly strategic review, semester overhaul.
  • Build a weekly plan from the four fixed inputs, in any format the week demands.
  • Run a daily cascade: weekly plan to time block plan to the current hour.
  • Execute a shutdown complete ritual that separates work from the rest of the day.
  • Implement full capture so the head holds only the present moment.
  • Choose hard disciplines, track them with metrics during intense periods, and let the system contract during hard ones.

Q&A

Explain this episode to me like I am new. What is the system?

One root document describes everything. Under it, three categories. Core documents: your values, your career and personal strategic plans, maintained weekly and overhauled each semester, plus an idea notebook. Productivity: a weekly plan built from the plans, the calendar, the task list, and the values plan. A daily time block plan on weekdays. A shutdown ritual. Full capture of every obligation. Discipline: an evolving list of hard boundaries, tracked with metrics when life is intense. The levels cascade: strategy shapes the week, the week shapes the day, the day shapes the hour. You never think about the big picture in the present moment, yet the present moment serves it.

Why does he call it “rooted productivity”?

Because everything grows out of one written root. The alternative he lived every summer was notes spread across many digital media and notebooks, redundant systems, overlapping lists. That is unrooted productivity, and it produced stress and lost sleep. The root document fixes the location of truth. Anything outside it sits outside the system.

What is a values plan, exactly?

Once a week he looks at his values and pulls out the emphasis for this week. Where has he fallen off a value. What habit would restore it. His example: community connection matters, so call someone every day this week. He also writes in his best practices for mental health. It is the weekly, operational form of the permanent values. Values say what matters. The values plan says what matters this week.

How do the strategic plans differ from the weekly plan?

Time horizon and stability. The strategic plans cover a semester and get overhauled at its start, with tweaks any time. The weekly plan covers seven days and is rebuilt every week from the strategic plans plus the calendar, the task list, and the values plan. Strategy is the direction. The weekly plan is the attack.

Why time block instead of just keeping a to-do list?

A to-do list says what. A time block plan says when, and it protects the when. Cal marks deep work blocks on the plan, which makes the deep-to-shallow ratio visible and measurable. More importantly, the plan is the daily form of the cascade: it translates the week’s intentions into hours. When reality intrudes, he redraws the blocks. The plan absorbs the day instead of the day destroying the plan.

What does “full capture” actually buy him?

Trust, which buys calm. Every obligation gets written down and routed to a system he believes in. Because he believes the system will surface it at the right time, he does not carry it in his head. The head is then free to hold only the present moment. That is the entire stress-management mechanism: not fewer obligations, but zero floating ones.

Why is the system’s ability to contract important?

Because life has hard periods. Burnout, despair, overwhelm. A system that only works at full intensity gets abandoned exactly when you need it most. His contracts to the basics: the values, the core things that must get done, the minimum that gets him out of the depths. The same system covers both the epic-essay weeks and the survival weeks.

Applied: fall arrives, summer systems are a mess, and you are stressed. What is the exact first move?

Do not invent a new system. That is the trap he fell into every summer. Return to the standard system. Open the root document. Check each of the three categories: are the core documents current, is there a weekly plan, are the disciplines defined. Get the standard pieces running again. The stress came from system-hopping, not from missing features.

Memory aids

Mnemonic for the three categories: CPD. Core documents, Productivity, Discipline. Say it as the three questions: what do I believe, how do I spend time, what do I never skip.

Mnemonic for the cascade: SWDN. Strategy shapes the Week, the Week shapes the Day, the Day shapes Now.

Never-confuse pairs: - Values vs. values plan. Values are permanent. The values plan is this week’s emphasis. - Weekly plan vs. time block plan. The weekly plan says how to attack the week. The time block plan assigns the hours. - Strategic plan vs. extended plan. The strategic plan covers a life area for a semester. The extended plan describes one big initiative, linked from it. - Discipline vs. habit. A discipline is a hard boundary, tracked and non-negotiable. A habit is softer. - Full capture vs. a to-do list. Capture is the trust that everything written will be routed and surfaced. A list is just a pile.

Trap cards: - Inventing a new system when stressed. The stress is the signal to return to the standard system, not to redesign. - Polishing the root document. It is for you. Imperfect wording is fine. A beautiful document you do not trust beats nothing, but an ugly one you trust beats both. - Tracking disciplines forever. Track during intense periods. Release during breaks. The system breathes.
