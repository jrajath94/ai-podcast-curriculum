Episode 9: Learn Any Hard Skill In 2024

2024-01-01 · Instructor: Cal Newport · Deep Questions with Cal Newport

A New Year deep dive on mastering hard things: the stairs model, deliberate practice, and a Q&A on notes, hobbies, study advice, and choosing what to learn next.

Why this episode matters

Episode 7 explained why learning needs strain. This episode explains how learning actually compounds. It is the episode that replaces the most common wrong belief about ability, that some brains can and some brains cannot, with a worked model, a ladder of small steps, and a way to budget the one resource that actually limits you, time. It then stress-tests the model against real questions: how to take notes, how to fit hobbies in a week, where to find trustworthy study advice, and how to choose what to learn next. Read it as the operations manual for episode 7’s brain science.

The setup: the new-year question

The episode is Cal’s New Year message, recorded January 1, 2024. The question it answers: how do you master something complicated in the year ahead. In work, that could be a difficult but valuable system or method that lets you control your career. In personal life, it could be a connoisseurship, a deep appreciation that gives lasting satisfaction, instead of the screens that currently fill the hours.

Cal promises two things. First, he will deconstruct the mistaken mental model most people hold about how complex things get learned. Second, he will replace it with a model that works, and give concrete advice for climbing it.

The deep dive: learning hard things

The wrong mental model

Cal draws the model most people carry. A hierarchy of things to learn, ordered from most complex to least. At the top, complicated mathematics. Below that, complex literature, a Proust novel. In the middle, deep appreciation of a kind of music. Below that, following a sport like baseball. At the bottom, knowing a lot about YouTube.

The common belief maps each level to a brain. One person can master music but not mathematics, because mathematics is beyond that person’s brain. The PhD in literature has a subtle understanding you cannot approach. That scholar is smarter than you. The person who understands music is smarter than the 22-year-old whose main interest is YouTubers.

The model says: the complexity of what you master is a direct reflection of how smart you are. Cal calls this model wrong.

The good news: most people can learn most things

The reality has two parts. The good news first. Most people are cognitively capable of learning things high on the imagined hierarchy. Complicated literature. Mathematical ideas. A deep appreciation of music or sport. Most people can learn most things.

Brain differences exist. Cal puts it in plain terms: by adulthood, people have different RPMs, different speeds of mental operation. But he argues most of that difference is nurture, not nature. A child who reads heavily trains a stronger brain, the same way a child made to do pushups and squats before every meal grows stronger by nineteen. He names Arnold Schwarzenegger, whose father made him exercise before he could eat, as the physical version of the same principle.

Where do the differences actually bite? At the very top of a field, and in the speed of progress. Almost anyone can learn calculus. Not everyone will stand at the absolute summit of a discipline. And some brains climb faster than others. But almost nobody cares about the summit, and nobody publishes the exact rate at which someone mastered something, so the speed differences rarely matter in practice.

The bad news: you cannot learn everything

The second part of the reality is the price. You can learn almost anything. You cannot learn everything. The reason is simple. Reaching mastery takes a long time, and time is finite. Only so many complicated things fit into one life.

This is the mental shift Cal wants. Stop thinking of the complexity of what you learned as a function of brain power. Think of it as a function of time invested. More time means more complexity mastered. Less time means less. Brain power is mostly orthogonal.

The stairs

The picture that replaces the hierarchy is a staircase. Put the goal at the top, say a deep understanding of jazz. The climb goes level by level. Each step is small, because a brain at a given level can only make a relatively small move upward.

Each step is crossed by deliberate practice. Definition: carefully designed exercises that push your understanding to the next level, out of what you are already comfortable with. Two properties make it work. The stretch: the activity must be uncomfortable, past your current reach. The design: the activity must be the right next level, tractable but not trivial. You can accomplish it, but it is not easy, because easy means no stretch.

Cal shows the stairs in his own life. People see his papers in theoretical computer science and cannot imagine sitting down to learn all of it. The climb started at sixteen. AP computer science in high school. College courses after that. Then MIT, where the courses were much harder, but seventeen steps already sat behind him. By the time he studied theory of computation with Mike Sipser, each step was small and guided. Encountering him at thirty-five and calling him smart hides a nineteen-year climb.

Two requirements follow. Patience: the ladder is long, and progress looks slow at any single step. Expert help: choosing the right next step, tractable but not trivial, is a skill in itself. Experts, courses, books, and carefully chosen goals all supply it.

Why it is worth it

Cal closes the deep dive with the stakes. The brain is what distinguishes humans from other animals. He borrows from Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics: the ability to contemplate deeply, to aim the mind at abstract ends, is perhaps the ultimate purpose of human experience. Dogs do not do it. Cats do not do it. Humans can. A life with hard things mastered is, in the Aristotelian sense, a more human life.

His advice for young people is concrete. Aim for one complicated, valuable skill in professional life, something you do better than anyone else you know at your company: advanced statistical modeling, 3D work for games, hard programming. And aim for one in personal life: a real connoisseurship, not a casual interest. Not “I read about movies” but a sommelier certificate, not “I go to the theater” but reviews contributed to publications. Many people carry the gap, people who do that stuff and I do not, and it curdles into feeling inferior or into resentment. Everyone should climb at least one ladder.

The last question is where the time comes from. Cal’s answer is blunt. Stop spending it on the phone. If nothing in your life feels like real expertise, your screen time numbers are probably bad, and the dopamine pull toward the funny or outrageous or distracting eats hours. His method: the phone foyer. The phone lives plugged in at the kitchen or the foyer. It is not with you by default. Not on the couch. Not at the dinner table. Not in the bathroom. The brain, given freedom and a hunger for something to do, takes the next stair step instead.

The Q&A: learning as a system

Mark: what is note-taking for

Mark finds note-taking mostly disposable and worries it becomes navel-gazing. Cal answers by defining the term first, then splitting it. Note-taking means recording information on a durable written medium so it lives outside your head and can be referenced later. Knowledge workers need three separate systems, and the common failure mixes them.

One, the working memory extender. A plain text file on the desktop, Cal’s workingmemory.txt. It temporarily expands how much information you can hold while working: the five next steps from a meeting, the inbox actions to schedule. It resets constantly, every ten minutes or so, as notes get processed into calendars and obligation systems.

Two, the obligation tracker. Every obligation on the plate, with all its information, in one categorized place. Cal uses Trello: one board per role, one column per obligation type, one card per obligation, all relevant information on the card. This is the system from episode 8’s six lists, now named as one of three.

Three, the idea capturer. Key ideas about work and life: interesting articles, brainstorms, concerns, plans. Cal uses a Remarkable 2 digital notebook with dozens of separate virtual notebooks for ideas, reflections, and concerns. This gets reviewed rarely, maybe once every few months when rethinking part of the business.

Different tools for each. Different refresh rates for each. Fancy connected-note systems like Zettelkasten are optional, a hobby for people who enjoy information management. The requirement is coverage, not sophistication: all three areas covered, nothing mixed. Do not keep tasks next to a twenty-year cabin vision next to a grocery list in one notebook.

Rishab: can the hobbies fit in the week

Rishab, a 26-year-old software developer, wants to learn guitar, drawing, and gardening alongside a demanding job and a deep life. Cal turns the question quantitative. Assume the professional life is organized, days time blocked, shutdown clear. Then autopilot the hobbies into the calendar: gardening on a weekday morning, alternating guitar and drawing sessions for an hour before dinner. Run the schedule and watch the feedback. Does it feel sustainable, or does every day feel like sprinting between commitments.

Undergraduates got the same exercise. They would block study time, meetings, and five extracurriculars, and the plan either fit or it did not. Sometimes it barely fit, and a week later they reported that every minute was scheduled and life felt crazy. The calendar gives objective feedback.

If it does not fit, or fits and feels crowded, pull back. Three moves. Make pursuits seasonal: garden in spring, guitar in winter, drawing in fall. Stack them one over another instead of running all at once. Or slow the ambition: not four hours of guitar a day to shred in six months, but an hour every other day of hard deliberate practice, reaching a good level in a few years. The goal is quality time in deeper pursuits. Speed of improvement does not matter. Quantity of pursuits does not matter.

Craig: the incentive structure of advice

Craig, a college student, notices most YouTube study advice is top-ten app lists and wonders whether skipping the apps makes him less effective. Cal’s first answer is ironic, given the listener may be on YouTube: do not use YouTube to get advice on studying.

The argument is about incentive structures. A pure study YouTuber optimizes for views. To get views, you serve the recommendation algorithm, and over months the content morphs toward whatever the algorithm rewards. The creator starts wanting to help students and ends making top-ten to-do list app videos, because that is what the numbers reward. The advice drifts from what works to what performs.

Books have the opposite incentive. A book like How to Become a Straight-A Student does not debut with a big promotional push. People find it on a table at the Harvard Coop, read it, and recommend it only if it works. Cal looked up the number before the show: approaching 250,000 copies sold, with no major promotion, no podcast tour, no social media footprint. All word of mouth. To sell that many copies quietly, the advice had to actually work. That is why his book contains nothing about note-taking apps: it gets to the mechanics of real academic tasks, and those mechanics make boring videos but high GPAs.

Podcasts sit with books. No algorithm grades the content. Growth comes from listeners telling others the show worked. That is why Cal exempts this very episode, even on YouTube: the YouTube version is a video of a podcast, and the podcast’s incentives are what shaped the content. The thumbnails play algorithm games, but the substance comes from the podcast. The rule: find advice where the incentive is that the advice works.

Emile: what to learn next

Emile sits at the top of his discipline and wants to learn something new, but not at random. Cal takes the question seriously. Most people choose randomly. Some get lucky and land on a valuable skill. Most do not. Thinking about the choice beats luck.

The method: deconstruct the field. Find people further ahead whose current position you admire. Work out how they got there. What were the key moves: particular knowledge, a new system, taking over a niche. Gather hard evidence about what matters, then make an educated bet and put energy behind it.

The image is a terrain of hills. Skills sit on hills of different heights. Most people wander randomly and some stumble uphill. Systematic thinking walks straight to the high ground. Do not be thrown by how hard the question is. Its difficulty is what makes it valuable.

The slow productivity corner

Gabrielle: slow down to learn faster

Gabrielle is fifteen, learning app development for two months to launch a business, coding two hours every morning and up to eight hours on non-school days, with exams and a paper competing. Cal is burnt out just hearing the schedule. His advice: slow down.

The deep dive’s model is the guide. Mastery is step, step, step, each step needing invested time and a refractory period of recharging. You cannot force it. During exams, do very little. After exams, do more. When school gets busy again, slow down again.

The short term feels worse. Over three months, the slow approach loses to the frenetic one. Over one to two years, the slow approach wins, because the frenetic approach burns out in months. The fast learner stalls with basic skills and retreats to easy progress like video games. The slow learner keeps stepping and ends up genuinely professional, with college and side-hustle options open.

Cal traces the false expectation to video games. Games are calibrated so that one to three months of regular play ends in mastery, with visible daily improvement. Young players absorb that cycle as the shape of all learning. But anything valuable enough to be worth learning takes longer than three months. If it took three months, everyone would do it and its value would collapse. The principle: take longer, vary the pace. Sustainability aggregates more skill than bursts that fizzle.

The Karan call: where slow productivity came from

A caller named Karan asks how Slow Productivity fits the pattern of Cal’s books, where each book answers a problem he was personally living through. Cal confirms the pattern and places the new book.

So Good They Can’t Ignore You came from his MIT postdoc years, around age thirty: how to build a working life you love, with the answer being rare and valuable skills that buy autonomy. Deep Work followed: concentration is the tier-one skill for getting good at rare and valuable things, and the knowledge-work apparatus of email and meetings destroyed it. A World Without Email was the deep investigation: the damage is real, the way of working is not fundamental, something different is possible. That trilogy covered his thirties and ended with tenure, seventy or eighty peer-reviewed papers, five thousand citations, three best-paper awards, and a writing career.

Slow Productivity answers the next question, the forties question. His children are now elementary age, all boys, and what they need most is time with their father. How does he keep producing work he is proud of while investing in his kids and his community. The answer is a reconfiguration of productivity around work that makes him proud, inside a deep and varied life, at a sustainable pace.

There is also a cultural case, made in the book’s introduction. The pandemic exposed that knowledge work has no real definition of productivity. The working heuristic is pseudo-productivity: more work is better than less. That heuristic makes people miserable and it is not sustainable. The book offers a sustainable definition for the whole sector.

Andrew’s case study: the affinity group

Andrew formed a Slow Productivity affinity group at Butler, his university, in January 2023. Thirty faculty and staff joined. He built a page on the learning management system with resources, articles, book recommendations, videos, writing-accountability groups, idea exchange, good places to work on campus, and a discussion forum. A small budget of 500 dollars bought lunches and about 250 dollars of books for a lending library. Three people formed a writing accountability group and all three wrote more that semester.

Participation split in two. Some people cared about the slow side: burnout, work-life balance, the frustration of never being able to slow down. Others cared about the productivity side: systems and output. Cal’s advice: let the venting come first. Expressing frustration about the more-is-better arrangement is an important first step, because that arrangement puts people in an impossible position, constantly arguing against themselves about work versus life. The frustration is worse for people with families, and often worse for women. Only after the frustration is voiced does the systematic work begin.

The December books

The episode closes with five books from December 2023. Home Economics by Wendell Berry, an essay collection found in a Little Free Library, containing his well-known two-economies essay. Berry left an NYU professorship to farm in Kentucky with horses, and Cal calls him the original voice of intentional living and minimalism, an influence on writers like Bill McKibben and Michael Pollan.

Pandora’s Box by Peter Biskind, a step-by-step history of the prestige TV era from HBO through FX and AMC to Netflix. Competent journalism rather than insider revelation, and useful for Cal’s developing theory that independent media will end up looking more like linear TV than like recommendation algorithms.

Where the Deer and the Antelope Play by Nick Offerman. Uneven: the Mark Twain-style storytelling is superb, especially the long section on buying an RV during the pandemic and road-tripping with his wife, the actress Megan Mullally. The political passages are simplistic Hollywood-bubble takes that yank the reader out of the stories. Read his first book, Paddle Your Own Canoe, instead.

Who Wrote the Bible by Richard Elliott Friedman, and its follow-up The Exodus. Textual biblical criticism, the secular German-born method that splits the Torah into five authors by word analysis and Hebrew dating, presented so accessibly it reads like a detective novel. The Exodus theory: a smaller group, probably the Levites led by Moses, left Egypt and their story became the national story.

How to imbibe this

Pick your two ladders this week. Write down one professional skill and one personal connoisseurship you will climb. For each, name the very next stair step, tractable but not trivial. Not “learn machine learning.” The next step: finish one specific tutorial chapter and build the toy example without the guide. The step must be small enough to finish and hard enough to fail at once.

Run the phone foyer experiment for seven days. Move the charger to the kitchen or the foyer tonight. The phone is not on the couch, not at the table, not in the bathroom. Log what the freed brain reaches for. By day seven, decide whether the ladder got any steps.

Split your notes into the three systems. Open three places today: a text file for working memory, one board or list for obligations, one notebook for ideas. Move every mixed note to its correct home. If a system is empty, that is fine. The point is separation, not volume.

Audit one advice source by incentive. Pick the YouTube channel or newsletter you trust most. Ask: what is this source paid in, views or word of mouth. If the answer is views, find the book or podcast on the same topic that grew quietly and compare the advice. Keep whichever survives the comparison.

Draw your skill terrain. List the skills around your current position. Mark the ones the people ahead of you used to climb. Circle the one educated bet you will make this year. Put it on the calendar.

Think it yourself

Companion capstone drills. Full unaided reasoning, your brain only, no AI. These stay at the track’s capstone difficulty: episode 7 and 8 trained the muscle, these two chapters put it to work on new terrain.

Drill 1, the next-step audit. Take a skill on your current list. Write down what you did for it in the last 30 days. For each activity, mark: stretch plus design, stretch without design, or comfort. If nothing qualifies as stretch plus design, you are not climbing stairs. You stand on one. Design the next real step now.

Drill 2, the 90-minute climb. Choose the next stair step from “How to imbibe this.” Phone in another room, 90 minutes, no AI. Work the hard step with your own brain. Log when the strain peaks and what you did at the peak. The peak is the step. Everything before it was approach.

Drill 3, the quantitative hobby test. List every pursuit competing for your non-work hours. Block each on next week’s calendar with honest durations. Total the hours. Compare against the hours you actually have. If the plan overflows, choose: seasonal, stacked, or slowed. Write the choice and the reason. No plan survives contact with the calendar unedited.

Drill 4, the advice-source audit. Take three sources you learn from. For each, write the incentive structure in one sentence and predict the direction of its content drift. Then find one piece of advice from each that the incentive would favor and one it would suppress. Which source would you bet your next year on.

Drill 5, argue both sides on the video-game cycle. Steelman the fast approach: when is a three-month burst the right way to learn something. Steelman the slow approach: when does varying the pace beat intensity. Then place five real skills you want on the two timelines and justify each placement with the stairs model.

Skills you can now use

  • Replace the brain-power hierarchy with the time-investment model when judging what you can learn.
  • Climb stairs: name the next step of a skill so it is tractable but not trivial, then design the stretch.
  • Distinguish stretch plus design from flailing and from entertainment.
  • Run three separate note systems: working memory extender, obligation tracker, idea capturer, each with its own tool and cadence.
  • Test any hobby load against the calendar: block it, run it, pull back to seasonal, stacked, or slower.
  • Read advice by incentive structure: views buy performance, word of mouth buys results.
  • Choose what to learn with the skill terrain: study the people ahead, bet on the high ground.
  • Apply the slow-productivity pace: take longer, vary the intensity, let the steps aggregate.

Q&A

Explain this episode to me like I am new. What is the big idea?

Most people think learning ability is about brain power: smart brains master hard things, ordinary brains cannot. The episode replaces that with time. Most people can learn most things, from mathematics to music, because mastery is a long staircase of small steps, and what separates the expert from the beginner is years of climbing, not a bigger brain. The price is that time is finite, so you cannot learn everything. Pick a few ladders, climb step by step with deliberate practice, get expert help choosing the next step, and be patient. That is the whole model.

If brain power barely matters, why is there not a Fields Medalist on every block?

Because the model has an upper end and a speed term. Near the absolute summit of a field, small differences in raw ability start to matter. And some brains climb faster. But almost nobody aims at the summit, and nobody cares exactly how fast someone climbed, so for every practical purpose the limit is time, not talent. The episode’s claim is about the range that matters to a life, not the last decimal of elite competition.

What does “tractable but not trivial” mean in practice?

It is the design rule for one stair step. Tractable means you can actually complete the exercise: the solo is short enough to finish, the chapter has the prerequisites you already hold. Not trivial means it stretches you: you will fail at the fast part, the proof will resist the first attempt. Stretch without tractability thrashes at something hopeless. Tractability without stretch is entertainment that feels like learning. Deliberate practice is the overlap.

Why three note systems instead of one perfect system?

Because the three jobs have different cadences and mixing them breaks all three. Working memory notes die every ten minutes. Obligations live for days and move through states. Ideas live for months and get reviewed at summits. One notebook holding all three becomes a junk drawer where urgent tasks hide behind grocery lists and cabin visions. Separation is the system. Fancy connected-note software is optional, a hobby, not a requirement.

Applied: I want to be good at guitar in six months. What does the episode say?

It says to run the numbers and then slow down. Block the practice on your calendar at honest durations and check whether the life still fits. Then accept the stairs: six months of daily force will likely end in burnout and basic skills, while an hour every other day of hard deliberate practice compounds over a few years into genuine ability. If six months is a hard deadline, shrink the goal to match the steps available, not the other way around.

Why is YouTube bad for study advice but this episode is fine on YouTube?

Incentives. A pure study YouTuber is paid in views, and the algorithm slowly rewrites the content toward whatever performs, usually app lists and spectacle. The drift is structural, not a character flaw. A book is paid in word of mouth, which only flows if the advice works, so the content drifts toward effectiveness. A podcast works like a book. This episode’s YouTube version is a video of a podcast, so its content was shaped by podcast incentives even though the thumbnails play the algorithm game.

How do I decide what to learn next without choosing at random?

Deconstruct your field. Find people further ahead whose position you admire. Identify the key moves that got them there: a system they mastered, a niche they took, knowledge they hold. That is hard evidence about what the terrain rewards. Then place an educated bet and commit. The episode treats the difficulty of the question as a feature: hard questions with real answers are exactly the ones worth asking.

Applied: I burn out on every skill I start. What is the fix?

You run the video-game cycle: intense bursts calibrated for one-to-three-month mastery, applied to ladders that take years. The fix is the slow-productivity pace. Take longer. Vary the intensity: heavy weeks and light weeks, seasons for different pursuits. Accept feeling behind at month three. The stairs do not care about your burst. They reward the climber who is still stepping at year two.

Memory aids

Mnemonic for the model: GNS. Good news: you can learn almost anything. Bad News: you cannot learn everything. Stairs: small steps, climbed with time. Say “good news, bad news, stairs.”

Mnemonic for deliberate practice: TNT. Tractable, Not Trivial. A step must be finishable and must stretch you. If it is only one of the two, it is not deliberate practice.

Mnemonic for the three note systems: WOB. Working memory, Obligations, Big ideas. Three jobs, three tools, three cadences. Never mix the WOB.

Never-confuse pairs: - The hierarchy vs. the stairs. The hierarchy ranks brains. The stairs rank invested time. The episode keeps the second. - Stretch vs. strain. Stretch is the designed discomfort of a good step. Strain without design thrashes. Both hurt. Only one climbs. - Hobbies vs. strategic skills. Hobbies can be seasonal, stacked, or slowed without guilt. Strategic skills get protected calendar time. The episode allows both, with different rules. - Views vs. word of mouth. Views reward performance for the algorithm. Word of mouth rewards advice that works. Check the currency before trusting the source. - Fast vs. slow learning. Fast wins at three months and loses at two years. Slow feels behind at three months and wins at two years. Pick the timeline first.

Trap cards: - “I will master this in three months.” That is the video-game cycle talking. Real ladders take years. Budget in years. - “My brain is not built for this.” The hierarchy model. The evidence says time, not talent, with the summit as the only exception. - “One perfect note app will fix my chaos.” No. Separation fixes chaos. One drawer for everything is the chaos. - “The algorithm will surface the best study advice.” The algorithm surfaces the best-performing advice. Those are different things. - “I need to go faster to catch up.” The burst burns out. The stairs reward the patient climber.
