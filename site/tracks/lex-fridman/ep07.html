Episode 7: Mark Zuckerberg’s First Interview in the Metaverse

This is episode 398 of the Lex Fridman Podcast, recorded on September 28, 2023, with guest Mark Zuckerberg. It is the first interview Lex ever conducted in the metaverse: both participants appear as codec avatars, photorealistic digital faces rendered in real time. At 3.0 million views it ranks seventh in this track. The conversation covers codec avatar technology, Quest 3, the photorealistic-versus-expressive identity question, talking to the dead, AI versions of living creators, Meta AI and AI Studio, Llama 2 open source, and metaverse ethics.

Reading this chapter equals watching the whole episode. Shared method (paraphrase, [uncertain] marking): see the track index. Product claims are his statements in September 2023, reported as such.

The setup: an interview as avatars

In the episode, Lex and Zuckerberg speak as codec avatars: scanned, photorealistic digital representations of their faces, animated in real time by their expressions. The point of doing the interview this way is the demonstration itself. The viewer watches two photorealistic faces converse, then learns the faces are rendered. In the episode, Zuckerberg says the remaining flaws (slight stiffness, imperfect mouths) actually aid immersion rather than breaking it, because the brain reads the intent to be present. This is a claim about the uncanny valley: near-perfect faces disturb, but slightly imperfect ones that clearly try to communicate do not.

Codec avatars: scan everything, then compress to a codec

In the episode, Zuckerberg explains codec avatars. The process: scan a person making many expressions from many angles, building a complete model of their face. Then compress that model into a codec, a compact representation that can be animated in real time from the headset’s sensors. The mechanism: the heavy work (learning your face) happens once, up front. The light work (animating it from eye and face tracking) happens live.

The key engineering win is bandwidth. In the episode, he says a codec avatar call uses far less bandwidth than video, because you transmit facial-movement parameters, not pixels. A video call streams millions of pixels per frame. An avatar call streams a small vector of expression coefficients. The honest price: the scan is the hard part. In 2023 it required specialized capture rigs. The goal he states: a phone scan of 2 to 3 minutes that produces your codec avatar.

Quest 3: the $500 mixed-reality headset

In the episode, recorded around Quest 3’s launch, Zuckerberg describes it: 500 dollars, mixed reality (color passthrough so you see the real room with virtual objects in it), and a chip stronger than the 1,500-dollar Quest Pro’s, thanks to Qualcomm’s newer silicon. The headset is about 40 percent thinner than its predecessor. The mechanism of mixed reality: multiple cameras plus AI that maps the camera views to your eye’s perspective in real time. The honest price: passthrough is a reconstruction, not a window. It has latency and artifacts, and your brain notices at the edges.

The piano demo in the episode: Zuckerberg plays a virtual piano with hand tracking, no controllers, no perceptible latency. The mechanism: cameras track the hands, the system renders the keys, and spatial audio places the sound. The point: controllers were a crutch. Hands are the interface.

Holograms: screens as objects

In the episode, Zuckerberg says the long-term vision is holograms: as many virtual objects as physical ones. Screens, books, games become holographic objects you place in your room. The mechanism: once displays can put light where your eyes expect it (true holographic or light-field displays), a virtual screen is as usable as a physical one. The honest price: the display physics for true holograms is still years out. Quest 3’s passthrough is the bridge, not the destination.

Photorealistic versus expressive: the identity question returns

In the episode, Zuckerberg revisits the avatar spectrum from Episode 5 with new depth. Photorealistic avatars serve as a transition bridge: they make the metaverse feel legitimate to adults who find cartoons unserious. But he says the long-term preference may be expressive: people enjoy being slightly idealized, stylized versions of themselves. In the episode, he notes that people blend both fine, switching between photorealistic and expressive by context.

The deeper question he raises is temporal accuracy. In the episode, he asks: which version of you is the avatar? The summer beach-body version? The current version? Identity is not a fixed thing. It is a choice. The mechanism of the insight: avatars force explicit choices about self-presentation that physical life makes implicitly. The honest price: whoever controls the defaults controls a lot of identity. Avatar systems need user control over their own representation.

Talking to the dead: grief technology

In the episode, Lex asks about interacting with digital versions of deceased loved ones. Zuckerberg treats it as a real product and ethical question. The mechanism: a model trained on a person’s messages, voice, and videos can simulate conversation with them. He raises the norms problem: grief has protocols in every culture (funerals, mourning periods, memorials). Digital resurrection needs protocols too: who consents, who controls it, when does it end?

In the episode, he says messages from the dead should stay private: a person’s private messages should not train a public model. The decision rule he implies: consent must come from the person while alive, control must sit with the family, and the system must have an off switch. The honest price: no protocol will satisfy everyone, because grief itself has no consensus.

AI versions of the living: the creator problem

In the episode, Zuckerberg distinguishes AI versions of the dead from AI versions of living creators. Meta’s product: creators can make AI versions of themselves that talk to fans while they are alive and busy. The mechanism: the AI is trained on the creator’s content and style, handles routine fan interaction, and escalates what it cannot handle.

The hard problem he names is predictability bounds. In the episode, he says the AI must be predictable enough that the creator trusts it, but capable enough to be useful. Too predictable and it is a FAQ. Too capable and it says something the creator would never say. The decision rule: the creator sets the bounds, reviews the failures, and the bounds tighten over time. This is the data engine (Episode 6) applied to personality.

Meta AI and AI Studio: anyone can create AIs

In the episode, Zuckerberg describes Meta AI (the assistant) and AI Studio (the platform): anyone can create an AI character, the way anyone can post a video. User-generated AIs. The mechanism: creation tools plus distribution. YouTube made everyone a broadcaster. AI Studio aims to make everyone an AI creator. In the episode, he mentions a launch set of AIs with celebrity personas: a Snoop Dogg dungeon master for games, a Jane Austen for literature chat, a Marcus Aurelius for stoic advice. [Added: Meta’s launch set was reported as 28 personas outside the episode.] The mechanism of the celebrity: a familiar persona lowers the barrier to the first conversation.

The honest price: persona AIs inherit the predictability problem at scale. Twenty-eight personas times millions of users is a large surface for the AI to say something wrong in a famous person’s voice.

Llama 2 open source: value versus risk

In the episode, Zuckerberg defends open-sourcing Llama 2. His argument: the value of open AI (more builders, more scrutiny, wider distribution of power) exceeds the risks, and the models were red-teamed before release. In the episode, he says the Llama 3 open-sourcing decision was still ahead and would be debated on its merits. The mechanism of his position: open weights let the whole ecosystem build, which he believes produces safety through distribution rather than concentration. The honest price he concedes: open weights cannot be recalled. Once released, any safety property that depends on control is gone. This is the central tension of the open-source debate (continued in Episode 11).

Metaverse ethics: the harm principle

In the episode, Lex asks about ethics in virtual worlds. Zuckerberg’s principle: the harm principle. Shooting your friends in a game is fine. Everyone consented to the game. The mechanism: virtual actions need the same moral analysis as physical ones, adjusted for consent and context. He raises the anonymity problem: anonymity reduces the incentive to be a good citizen, because reputation does not follow you. The decision rule: design identity and reputation systems that carry consequences across contexts, or accept worse behavior.

Remote work and the nature of reality

In the episode, Zuckerberg says presence technology changes remote work: live anywhere, feel together. The mechanism: if presence is convincing, geography stops taxing collaboration. He closes the philosophical loop: reality is the physical plus the digital combined. Consciousness, in the episode’s framing, is what feels real. If a virtual interaction feels real to the conscious mind, it is real in the only sense that matters to experience. The honest price: this is a philosophical claim, not an empirical one. It justifies the whole metaverse bet, which is why he states it.

Q&A

Q1: How do codec avatars work, and why do they use less bandwidth than video? Scan once: capture your face making hundreds of expressions from many angles, and learn a complete model of it. Compress the model into a codec. At call time, the headset tracks your eyes and face and sends only the expression parameters (a small vector of numbers), and the other person’s device renders your face from them. Video sends millions of pixels per frame. Avatars send dozens of numbers per frame. Follow-up: what is the hard part? The scan. In 2023 it needed special rigs. The goal is a 2-to-3-minute phone scan. Follow-up: why do the flaws aid immersion? Because the brain reads the communicative intent. A slightly stiff face that tries to express is more present than a perfect face that feels dead. This is the uncanny valley crossed from the other side.

Q2: What is mixed reality on Quest 3, technically? Color cameras on the headset capture the room. AI maps the camera views to your eyes’ perspective in real time (perspective correction). The display shows the corrected real world with virtual objects composited in. Follow-up: why is it a reconstruction, not a window? The cameras are not where your eyes are, so the system must reproject. Reprojection has latency and artifacts. Follow-up: what do the numbers say? 500 dollars, 40 percent thinner, a chip stronger than the 1,500-dollar Quest Pro’s. The price-performance curve is the story.

Q3: Photorealistic or expressive: which wins, and why does it matter? Zuckerberg’s answer: photorealistic is the bridge (it makes the metaverse legitimate to skeptics), expressive is the destination (people prefer slightly idealized selves). It matters because identity defaults are power: whoever sets the default avatar shapes how billions see themselves. Follow-up: what is the temporal-accuracy problem? Your avatar freezes one version of you. Which version? The choice is now explicit and designed, not implicit and accidental. Follow-up: who should control it? The user, with full control over their own representation. Any less is identity rented from a platform.

Q4: What are the ethics of talking to the dead? The mechanism is straightforward: train on their messages, voice, and video. The ethics are not: consent (did they agree while alive?), control (who operates it?), privacy (do their private messages train it?), and ending (when does it stop?). Zuckerberg’s line: private messages stay private. Follow-up: design the protocol. Consent in life, family control after death, no training on private messages without explicit permission, a visible “this is a simulation” marker, and an off switch. Follow-up: what is the strongest argument against building it? That it freezes grief instead of processing it. Argue both sides from the episode’s framing.

Q5: What is the predictability-bounds problem for creator AIs? The AI must be useful (capable) without being dangerous (saying things the creator would never say). Too constrained and it is worthless. Too free and it is a liability. The solution in the episode: the creator sets bounds, reviews failures, and tightens over time. Follow-up: connect this to Episode 6’s data engine. Deploy the persona, mine the failures (things it said wrong), label corrections, retrain. Personality becomes a data-engine problem. Follow-up: what is the failure mode? A failure at scale: one wrong statement in a famous voice, seen by millions, before the bounds tighten.

Q6: Why open-source Llama 2? Give Zuckerberg’s full argument. Value: more builders, more scrutiny, wider distribution of AI power, an ecosystem that does not depend on one company. Risk management: red-teaming before release. The bet: safety through distribution beats safety through concentration. Follow-up: what is the irrevocable part? Open weights cannot be recalled. Any safety property that needs control is gone at release. Follow-up: how does this differ from OpenAI’s position in Episode 8? OpenAI open-sources some models but not the frontier ones. Meta open-sources near-frontier models. The disagreement is about where the line sits.

Q7: What is the harm principle for virtual worlds? Judge virtual actions the way you judge physical ones, adjusted for consent. Consensual game violence is fine. Non-consensual harm is not, even if virtual. Follow-up: what does anonymity break? Reputation. If actions do not follow you, the incentive to behave degrades. Follow-up: design the fix. Persistent identity with portable reputation, or accept the behavior. There is no third option in the episode’s framing.

Q8 (applied): You are building an avatar system for your company. What do you specify? Two-axis identity (realism × self-vs-fantasy) with user control of both. Authentication strength matched to likeness (photorealistic requires strong verification). A 2-to-3-minute enrollment scan as the usability bar. Bandwidth budget based on parameter streaming, not video. A written policy for deceased persons’ likenesses: consent, control, privacy, off switch. Follow-up: what do you measure? Enrollment completion rate, perceived presence scores, bandwidth per call, and impersonation incidents. Follow-up: what is the trap? Optimizing likeness while neglecting consent. The technology is ready before the norms are.

How to imbibe this

  1. This week: audit one default. Find a default someone else set for your identity or presentation (a profile photo, a bio, a status). Ask: did I choose this, or did I accept it? Change one default deliberately.
  2. Separate the bridge from the destination. Pick a goal. Name what is merely the bridge (what makes it legitimate now) and what is the destination (what you actually want). Do not confuse them.
  3. Write a consent rule. For one thing you share or build, write down who consented, who controls it, and how it ends. The grief-technology protocol applies to living data too.
  4. Test presence in your own communication. In your next video call, notice what breaks presence: the eyes, the audio, the latency. Fix the weakest channel.
  5. Set predictability bounds. If you delegate to anyone (or any AI), write the bounds: what they may decide, what they must escalate. Review one failure against the bounds.

Observable behavior change: by next week, you have one deliberately chosen default, one bridge-vs-destination map, and one written delegation bounds.

Think it yourself

  1. Guided Fermi. A video call at 1080p30 uses about 2 Mbps. If a codec avatar sends 100 expression parameters at 30 frames per second, with 4 bytes each, what is the bitrate? (Scaffolding: 100 × 30 × 4 = 12,000 bytes per second, about 96 kbps.) How many avatar calls fit in one video call’s bandwidth? (Answer: about 20.) Now you try: what does this imply for a billion users? (Your answer: avatar calls make global presence affordable where video never could.)
  2. Argue both sides. “AI versions of the dead should exist.” Three sentences for, three against. Your brain only.
  3. The identity toy. Place yourself on the two axes (realism × self-vs-fantasy) for three contexts: a job interview, a game with friends, a first date. Write one sentence justifying each point. Notice how contextual identity already is.
  4. Journaling. “Which version of me would my avatar be?” 200 words. No editing.
  5. The consent drill. Pick a photo of you that someone else posted. Trace: did you consent? Who controls it? How would you revoke it? Write the answers. This is the grief-protocol exercise applied to the living.

Skills you can now use

  • Bandwidth-first design. Compare parameter streaming to pixel streaming before choosing a media architecture.
  • Bridge-vs-destination analysis. Separate what makes an idea legitimate now from what it must become.
  • Consent-protocol design. Write consent, control, privacy, and termination rules for any system handling identity.
  • Predictability-bounds management. Delegate within explicit bounds and tighten them from failure review.
  • Harm-principle ethics. Judge virtual-world actions by consent and context, and design reputation to carry consequences.

Memory aids

  • Mnemonic for codec avatars: Scan once, stream parameters. The heavy learning is up front. The live call is a small vector.
  • Mnemonic for the identity axes: Realism × Identity. How real, and whose. RI: “Representation Is” a choice.
  • Never-confuse pair: Photorealistic (the bridge. Legitimacy. Needs strong authentication) versus expressive (the destination. Preference. Needs less). Do not build the bridge and mistake it for the destination.
  • Never-confuse pair: Presence (the feeling of being there. A systems property) versus graphics (how it looks. One subsystem). Presence needs all channels to agree.
  • Trap card: “Better likeness is the goal.” The trap: likeness without consent is impersonation infrastructure. Consent first, likeness second.
  • Trap card: “Open weights can be managed after release.” They cannot be recalled. Decide before release, because release is forever.
