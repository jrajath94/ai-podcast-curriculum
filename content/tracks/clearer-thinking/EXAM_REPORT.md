## Chapter 1 (ep01 — Andy Matuschak, learning and memory)

### Q1 [numerical]. Recompute the yearly maintenance cost of this whole track's keep-worthy ideas.

Answer: The chapter estimates a good book holds about 12 genuinely interesting
ideas, roughly 30 pieces worth keeping (ep01:137), and per-item maintenance
costs about 10 to 15 seconds of review per year after four to five exposures
(ep01:83). For the track: 10 chapters × 30 pieces = 300 pieces.
300 × 10 s = 3,000 s = 50 minutes; 300 × 15 s = 4,500 s = 75 minutes.
So the whole track costs about one hour of review per year to keep, matching
the chapter plate's own arithmetic (ep01:315).
Citations: ep01:83, ep01:137, ep01:315.

### Q2 [explain-like-new]. Explain the forgetting curve and the review schedule it implies, to someone new.

Answer: When you learn something, you forget it fast at first and then slower,
a power law of forgetting with rapid early loss and a long tail (ep01:49-53).
Each time you meet the idea again, the curve flattens, so you forget a little
more slowly next time (ep01:53-56). Combined with the spacing effect (spreading
the same hours across days beats one cram session, ep01:57-61), this implies an
exponential backoff schedule: review 1 after 1–3 days, review 2 after about a
week, review 3 after about a month, review 4 after about a quarter, then about
once a year — four to five exposures total keeps the item available for years
(ep01:63-72).
Citations: ep01:49-72.

### Q3 [applied]. You just finished a nonfiction book you want to remember. Design the first week of its memory system using only this chapter.

Answer: First, list the keep-worthy pieces before building any cards: about 12
interesting ideas per book, perhaps 30 pieces; you will naturally retain about
4, so build cards for the other 26 (ep01:137). Aim cards at behavior change and
salience, not trivia — e.g. a failure-mode card like Matuschak's ("What failure
mode did that person suggest I am at risk of when working with other people?",
ep01:101-109). Schedule each card on the backoff ladder: first review 1–3 days
after exposure, second about a week later (ep01:67-72). At day 2, review with
active recall — pull answers from memory, get corrected when wrong, because
rereading trains only recognition (ep01:89-93).
Citations: ep01:137, ep01:101-109, ep01:67-72, ep01:89-93.

## Chapter 2 (ep02 — Tyler Cowen, three laws)

### Q4 [numerical]. Recompute the real interest rate from the episode's worked example, and state what theory predicted.

Answer: Real rate = nominal rate minus inflation (ep02:57). Inflation was
6.2 percent; nominal yields were about 1 percent. 1 − 6.2 = −5.2, i.e. about
negative 5 percent (ep02:59, ep02:67-69). In real terms lenders lost money to
borrowers. Standard theory predicted the opposite: deficits should push real
rates up (crowding out investment), i.e. positive and rising (ep02:59, ep02:70).
Citations: ep02:57, ep02:59, ep02:67-70.

### Q5 [explain-like-new]. Explain Cowen's three laws to someone new, including when to use each.

Answer: Law one, "something is wrong with everything": you do not understand a
research paper until you can articulate what is wrong with it — use it when
reading, on every paper and position including your own (ep02:37-45). Law two,
"there is a literature on everything": before assuming you are first to a
problem, check what exists — use it when starting research (ep02:37-38, 47-51).
Law three, "all propositions about real interest rates are wrong": some domains
resist understanding so stubbornly that the honest move is a law about your own
ignorance — use it as a template for naming the limits of understanding in any
stubborn domain (ep02:39, 73-75).
Citations: ep02:37-51, ep02:73-75.

### Q6 [applied]. A colleague posts an opinion online that offends you. Walk through Cowen's rule seven on it, step by step.

Answer: Step 1: notice the offense as data about your own attention allocation,
not about their worth (ep02:100-103). Step 2: triage the sting — is it a status
threat or a real moral violation? Do not blur the two (ep02:105-108). If it is
a status threat, treat it as a signal you have under-invested in understanding
that view. Step 3: get drawn to it like a magnet and master their best case —
articulate it as strongly as possible, the way Cowen's daily writing forces
steelmanning (ep02:103-105, 119-122). Step 4: do not devalue-and-dismiss — do
not lower their status over one flaw and stop learning (ep02:99-103).
Citations: ep02:99-108, ep02:119-122.

## Chapter 3 (ep03 — David McRaney, persuasion)

### Q7 [numerical]. Recompute the evidence behind the deep-canvassing protocol, in person-hours.

Answer: The canvassers ran 17,000 conversations (ep03:144), each about 20
minutes (ep03:258). 17,000 × 20 minutes = 340,000 minutes = 5,667 hours ≈
5,700 hours, which is roughly 2.8 person-years at 2,000 hours/year — "nearly
three person-years" as the drill states (ep03:258). The protocol is the product
of that A/B testing, not of armchair advice.
Citations: ep03:144, ep03:258.

### Q8 [explain-like-new]. Explain the elaboration likelihood model's two routes to someone new.

Answer: Elaboration is how much careful thought a person gives a message, and
it equals motivation times ability (ep03:53). High elaboration takes the
central route: the person weighs the actual arguments, strong arguments beat
weak ones, and the resulting attitude change lasts (ep03:55). Low elaboration
takes the peripheral route: the person does not process the arguments and
responds to cues instead — how many arguments there are, how credible the
source seems, familiarity — and the change is shallow and fades (ep03:57).
The three-against-nine study: with high elaboration, 3 strong arguments beat 9
weak ones; with low elaboration, 9 weak arguments beat 3 strong ones, because
the unengaged person counts arguments without inspecting them (ep03:78-88).
Citations: ep03:53-57, ep03:78-88.

### Q9 [applied]. You must change a family member's mind about a policy. Diagnose the situation and pick your protocol from this chapter.

Answer: First, label what you hold: a belief answers to evidence, an attitude
answers to feelings and identity — most failed persuasion brings facts to a
feeling (ep03:33-45). Second, diagnose elaboration: are they motivated and able
to think this through right now? If low, you are on the peripheral route, where
credibility and motivation matter more than logic (ep03:90-96). Third, choose
technique rebuttal, not topic rebuttal: in a family conversation where reactance
is likely, ask "how did you decide this was a good reason?" instead of arguing
facts (ep03:172-178). Fourth, run the deep-canvassing protocol: rapport,
explicit consent to explore their reasoning, their claim plus a 1–10 confidence
number, "why does that number feel right?", their story, then why-not-one-higher
and one-lower so they argue both sides themselves (ep03:144-160).
Citations: ep03:33-45, ep03:90-96, ep03:144-160, ep03:172-178.

## Chapter 4 (ep04 — Daniel Kahneman, noise)

### Q10 [numerical]. Use the error equation with numbers, and recompute the underwriter noise ratio.

Answer: Total error² = bias² + noise² (ep04:39). Example: bias of 2 units and
noise of 4 units give error² = 4 + 16 = 20, so total error ≈ 4.5 units —
noise dominates the total even though bias alone would give only 2. For the
underwriters: expected difference between two random underwriters pricing the
same risk was about 10 percent; the measured difference was 52 percent
(ep04:47-54). Ratio: 52 ÷ 10 ≈ 5.2, "about 5 to 1" (ep04:47, ep04:53-54).
Citations: ep04:39, ep04:47-54.

### Q11 [explain-like-new]. Explain bias, noise, and the three kinds of noise to someone new.

Answer: Bias is systematic error pushing judgments one way, like a scale that
always reads two kilos heavy. Noise is random scatter, like a scale that reads
differently every time you step on (ep04:31-39). Total error² = bias² + noise².
A noise audit splits noise into three kinds: level noise — judges differ in
average severity (one judge averages 8 years, another 5); pattern noise —
judges weigh case features differently (harsh on theft, lenient on assault,
and the reverse), usually the biggest component; occasion noise — the same
judge varies across occasions (mood, time since lunch) (ep04:61-67).
Citations: ep04:31-39, ep04:61-67.

### Q12 [applied]. You are hiring for your team. Design the full decision-hygiene protocol from this chapter.

Answer: Step 1, mediating assessments: break hiring into independent scored
parts — skill, culture fit, growth — each scored before any discussion of the
candidate as a whole, killing the halo effect (ep04:116-120). Step 2,
independent judgments first: every interviewer records their scores privately
before anyone speaks; discussion after independent judgment averages out noise,
discussion before it creates conformity (ep04:122-124). Step 3, delay intuition:
the gut whole-picture judgment comes last, constrained by the structured pieces
(ep04:124-126). Step 4, run the premortum: after your judgment, imagine the
hire fails, name the most likely explanation, adjust, then average the original
and adjusted estimates — a crowd within one head, about one third as good as a
second person (ep04:128-136). Step 5, for timeline/cost forecasts use the
outside view: anchor on the reference class of similar past hires, not the
plan's details (ep04:138-144).
Citations: ep04:116-136, ep04:138-144.

## Chapter 5 (ep05 — Barry Schwartz, practical wisdom)

### Q13 [numerical]. Recompute the "tell me when to think" arithmetic.

Answer: The drill assumes roughly 35,000 decisions a day (ep05:211). If 1
percent deserve deliberate System 2 thought, that is 350 decisions. At 10
minutes each: 350 × 10 = 3,500 minutes ≈ 58 hours (ep05:211). That is
impossible, which is the point: the meta-skill cannot be "think about the
important 1 percent" naively — the real job of "tell me when to think" is to
allocate the clock far more selectively, since most positions must play
themselves through pattern recognition (ep05:96-102).
Citations: ep05:211, ep05:96-102.

### Q14 [explain-like-new]. Explain to someone new why the same beef can be a different decision depending on the frame.

Answer: A frame is the description of a choice, and different descriptions of
the same choice produce different decisions (ep05:66-68). The beef's counter
price excludes externalities — environmental and health costs paid elsewhere.
Framed as "cheap beef versus expensive beef," you buy the cheap. Framed as
"beef with hidden costs versus beef without them," the decision shifts. The
beef never changed (ep05:68-72). Narrow frames are spotlights: everything
outside the beam, like closed schools and lost livelihoods in the COVID
case-count framing, disappears from the decision (ep05:73-77). Defense:
reframe every important decision twice before deciding (ep05:150-154).
Citations: ep05:66-77, ep05:150-154.

### Q15 [applied]. You trust your gut on a domain you work in. Audit that trust using Kahneman and Klein.

Answer: Apply the two conditions: (1) the environment must be regular enough
for patterns to exist, and (2) you must get fast, unambiguous feedback
(ep05:89). Firefighting passes both: fires behave lawfully, feedback is
immediate. Stock picking fails both: noise dominates, feedback is slow and
ambiguous (ep05:89-95, ep05:95). Score your domain on each condition. If it
fails either, you are mistaking confidence — seniority, fluency — for expertise
(memory aid, ep05:190-195). Then decide whether to trust the gut there, or
whether that domain is a "clock position" deserving deliberate System 2 instead
(ep05:96-102).
Citations: ep05:89-102.

## Chapter 6 (ep06 — Julia Galef, scout mindset)

### Q16 [numerical]. Recompute how far Bezos adjusted the startup base rate upward for his own case.

Answer: The base rate for internet startups was about 10 percent; Bezos's
personal estimate was 30 percent, adjusted upward for his specific advantages
(ep06:84). 30 ÷ 10 = 3, i.e. a 3× upward adjustment from the class base rate
(ep06:98). This is the chapter's trap test: expected value without the base
rate is optimism with arithmetic — Bezos started from 10 percent and adjusted
with reasons, then multiplied 0.30 by the enormous payoff (ep06:101-105).
Citations: ep06:84, ep06:98-105.

### Q17 [explain-like-new]. Explain the scout mindset to someone new, and why techniques fail without it.

Answer: The soldier mindset treats reasoning as combat: defend your side,
defeat the other; reasons are weapons, evidence for you gets amplified,
evidence against gets attacked (ep06:31). The scout mindset treats reasoning
as mapping: see the territory as it is; evidence for and against both update
the map (ep06:33). The soldier is the default because it serves social goals —
belonging, status, identity — that usually matter more than accuracy (ep06:37).
CFAR, co-founded in 2012, taught excellent techniques and students did not use
them: the failure was motive, not comprehension — a technique in a soldier's
hands becomes a better weapon (ep06:50-56).
Citations: ep06:31-37, ep06:50-56.

### Q18 [applied]. You face a low-confidence, high-payoff decision. Run the Bezos/Musk expected-value analysis on it.

Answer: Step 1, start from the base rate, not your hopes: Bezos used about 10
percent, the startup base rate, then adjusted upward to 30 percent for his
specific advantages (ep06:84, ep06:98-105). Step 2, multiply: 0.30 × enormous
payoff, minus the cost of quitting Wall Street — strongly positive, so he acted
(ep06:84-86). Musk went further: 10 percent each for Tesla and SpaceX, assumed
probable failure, acted anyway because 0.10 × enormous payoff beat any safe
career — motivation from expected value plus mission, not certainty (ep06:90-94).
Step 3, avoid the trap: expected value without base rates is optimism with
arithmetic (ep06:105). For your decision: write the class base rate, adjust
with checkable reasons, multiply by payoff, subtract cost, and act on the
product — even at 10 percent confidence (ep06:101-105).
Citations: ep06:84-94, ep06:98-105.

## Chapter 7 (ep07 — Uri Bram, great conversations)

### Q19 [numerical]. Recompute how many hours a year definition-mismatch arguments can cost.

Answer: About one in three disagreements turn out to involve no real
disagreement — just different meanings of the key words (ep07:80-82). At two
heated disagreements a week: 2 × 52 = 104 per year, of which ~35 are
definitional. At 30 minutes each: 35 × 0.5 h ≈ 17.3 hours a year, matching the
drill's "about 17 hours" (ep07:251). Tabooing the contested word in the
first five minutes — banning it and restating each claim without it (ep07:86) —
dissolves the ones that were never about the world (ep07:86-92).
Citations: ep07:80-92, ep07:251.

### Q20 [explain-like-new]. Explain light gassing to someone new, and why it is dangerous.

Answer: Gaslighting makes people doubt their correct perceptions. Light gassing,
Bram's coined opposite, reinforces their false ones: "yes, the other side is
all evil and stupid," said with team warmth (ep07:126). It feels like
camaraderie and functions as corruption: each round of warm mutual agreement
moves the group's beliefs further from reality, while the warmth hides the
drift (ep07:128-133). Defense: notice when agreement feels too good, and ask
what the group says about its own side's flaws — if the answer is nothing, the
tower is rising from crooked bricks (ep07:133-139).
Citations: ep07:126-139.

### Q21 [applied]. A 12-person team discussion keeps failing. Redesign it using this chapter.

Answer: The failure is structural, not moral: four people can have a great
discussion, but by seven it breaks down — the buzzer problem makes the speaking
threshold so high that most people go quiet and the two loudest broadcast
(ep07:144-150). Do not moralize the dominator; fix the game (ep07:204-210).
Redesign: split into groups of four or fewer, or impose an explicit format —
turn-taking, written contributions — and make the two invisible thresholds
explicit up front: the relevance threshold (how on-topic a bid must be) and the
interruption threshold (how hard it is to break in) (ep07:152-160). Have someone
play host: draw out the quiet, redirect the dominant, keep energy up, land the
ending (ep07:162-168).
Citations: ep07:144-168.

## Chapter 8 (ep08 — Robin Hanson, futarchy)

### Q22 [numerical]. Recompute the annual cost of running decision markets at scale, and the payback bar.

Answer: 50 decisions a year × $10,000 subsidy each = $500,000 a year
(ep08:241-245). The subsidy funds an automated market maker that loses money on
average to informed traders, drawing real information into the prices
(ep08:97-100). The payback bar: one avoided eight-figure mistake pays for
decades of the program (ep08:241-245). The follow-up question the chapter
poses: why do companies not do this — answered by the resistance section,
markets get killed when they contradict managers (ep08:133-139).
Citations: ep08:97-100, ep08:133-139, ep08:241-245.

### Q23 [explain-like-new]. Explain futarchy to someone new: what is split, and who does each part?

Answer: Every policy decision mixes values (what should we maximize?) and
beliefs (which policy maximizes it?). Democracy hands both to the same voter
simultaneously, and voters are decent at the first, terrible at the second,
with no incentive to get informed (ep08:31-39). Futarchy splits the jobs:
citizens vote on a welfare measure — the definition of the good — and
prediction markets choose the policies expected to maximize it (ep08:47-55).
Slogan: vote on values, bet on beliefs (ep08:53). Futarchy does not remove
politics; it confines politics to the values question, where it belongs, and
removes it from the beliefs question, where it corrupts (ep08:41-44).
Citations: ep08:31-55.

### Q24 [applied]. Your board must decide whether to fire the CEO. Set up the conditional decision market from the chapter.

Answer: Create two stock markets. Market A: trades called off if the CEO stays
— its price estimates company value if the CEO leaves. Market B: trades called
off if the CEO leaves — its price estimates value if the CEO stays (ep08:63).
Traders buy or sell against their own estimates, driving each price to the
conditional expected value (ep08:77). Decision rule: compare the two prices;
if A is higher, the company is worth more without the CEO — fire them
(ep08:65, ep08:67-75). Avoid decision selection bias: read the prices at
decision time, with everything the board knows reflected in the market — via
their own trading or someone they inform — so the prices reflect consequences,
not predictions of the politics (ep08:83-89).
Citations: ep08:63-89.

## Chapter 9 (ep09 — Marcus Davis, effective giving)

### Q25 [numerical]. Recompute the income comparison that reframes giving, and the demand it implies.

Answer: $3 a day × 365 days = $1,095 ≈ $1,100 a year for the global poor
(ep09:37-44, ep09:234). Against $100,000 after-tax income: 1,100 ÷ 100,000 =
1.1 percent — about one percent of that income equals one person's entire
annual income (ep09:234). A relatively poor American at $25,000–$30,000 after
taxes sits around the 80th to 95th percentile of the global income distribution
(ep09:37-44). This is the pond-to-river logic: the dollar buys far more where
prices are lower and needs are basic, which is why the demandingness debate
bites: the pond proves the duty, the river shows it has no stopping point
(ep09:56-64, ep09:74).
Citations: ep09:37-44, ep09:56-74, ep09:234.

### Q26 [explain-like-new]. Explain moral uncertainty and the two aggregation rules to someone new.

Answer: Even with perfect data on consequences, you face moral uncertainty:
utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics disagree about what counts as good,
and no option commands certainty — you hold credences across theories
(ep09:118-123). To act, aggregate. Weighted averaging: assign each theory your
credence and weight its recommendation accordingly (ep09:127). Budget splitting:
divide your resources by credence — utilitarianism's 50 percent credence gets
50 percent of the budget to spend as it wants (ep09:129). Averaging risks
actions no theory endorses; splitting risks funding contradictions — pick one
and let it be criticized (ep09:131-134). Set the credences via reflective
equilibrium: iterate theory arguments against your trusted judgments until
they cohere (ep09:119-123).
Citations: ep09:118-134.

### Q27 [applied]. You have $1,000 to give. Write the explicit-model decision from this chapter.

Answer: Step 1, write the model down: cost per outcome and uncertainty spread
for each candidate cause — an implicit model hides its errors; the explicit
one makes them attackable (ep09:103-112). Step 2, demand the evidence bar:
RCTs showing causality at a measured cost per outcome — bed nets against
malaria, dozens of trials, about a couple of thousand dollars per life saved —
over testimonials, however moving (ep09:50-57). Step 3, Monte Carlo the
uncertainty: report distributions, not points — e.g. the cost per life saved
"falls between $1,000 and $4,000 with 80 percent probability" (ep09:108-116).
Step 4, act on the best explicit model available: GiveWell, open to criticism
and updated with evidence — and do not split the $1,000 across ten vivid
stories, which buys ten feelings instead of one outcome (ep09:136-142,
ep09:191-197).
Citations: ep09:50-57, ep09:103-116, ep09:136-142.

## Chapter 10 (ep10 — Anna Riedl, rationality and cognitive science)

### Q28 [numerical]. Work the domino parity argument from scratch with the chapter's numbers.

Answer: A chessboard has 64 squares in alternating colors; 32 dominoes tile it,
each covering exactly one black and one white square (ep10:72-74). Remove the
two diagonally opposite corners — same color (ep10:74). Remaining: 62 squares,
32 of one color and 30 of the other (ep10:74-84). Thirty-one dominoes would need
31 black and 31 white squares (ep10:84). 30 < 31, so tiling is impossible — no
search needed, in one subtraction (ep10:74, ep10:156-161). General lesson:
representation beats search; the right representation (colors, not positions)
turns an exponential puzzle into arithmetic (ep10:86-90).
Citations: ep10:72-90, ep10:156-161.

### Q29 [explain-like-new]. Explain the great rationality debate and its synthesis to someone new.

Answer: Two tribes barely talk to each other. The axiomatic approach judges
thinking against formal axioms of rationality (von Neumann and Morgenstern):
violate the axioms and you are biased, an error to correct (ep10:37-41). The
ecological approach judges thinking against fit to the environment: a heuristic
that works in its world is rational regardless of axioms, and deviations may be
features, not bugs (ep10:43-47). Synthesis: resource-rational analysis —
thinking costs time and computation, so the right standard is accuracy per unit
of cost; many apparent biases become optimal speed-accuracy trade-offs under a
ticking clock (ep10:50-56). What it does not explain: slow errors like the sunk
cost fallacy, which survive unlimited deliberation — time pressure explains
fast mistakes, motives explain the slow ones (ep10:58-62).
Citations: ep10:37-62.

### Q30 [applied]. You are stuck on a hard problem. Run this chapter's insight protocol on it.

Answer: Step 1, check for the added constraint: like the nine-dot problem,
where the solver silently added "stay inside the square" and nobody said it,
list your assumed constraints and mark which ones the problem actually stated —
insight is often dropped assumption, not new information (ep10:64-70). Step 2,
upgrade the representation before pushing the content: draw the colors instead
of the positions, diagram the mechanism instead of describing it — a diagram is
worth 10,000 words because it matches the mind's machinery (ep10:86-90,
ep10:141-147). Step 3, run the meta-rational calculation: is more thinking
worth its cost — and keep the test itself cheap, or it eats the budget it
protects (ep10:54). Step 4, check relevance: most failure is misattention, not
miscalculation — write the three things actually worth attending to (ep10:97-115).
Citations: ep10:54, ep10:64-70, ep10:86-90, ep10:97-115, ep10:141-147.
