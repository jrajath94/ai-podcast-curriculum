## Chapter 1 (ep01): The Perks of Living Without Social Media

### Q1 [N]
You run the boredom-ledger drill for one day and count 9 phone-reach impulses. Using the drill's own numbers, how many minutes did you reclaim that day, and how many hours is that over a year?

**Answer.** Each reach would have cost 5 minutes, so 9 x 5 = 45 minutes reclaimed that day. Over a year: 45 x 365 = 16,425 minutes = 273.75 hours.
Citations: ep01:191 (the drill: "if each reach would have cost 5 minutes... Multiply by 365").

### Q2 [E]
Explain like I am new: what is solitude, and what exactly does the phone do to it?

**Answer.** Solitude means time alone with your own thoughts, with no input competing for attention. It is the workshop where experience becomes a framework, and it is in time alone with our minds that we evolve as humans. The phone becomes the default distraction at the slightest hint of boredom, so boredom never survives long enough to turn into solitude. The reflex is not the user's fault: tens of billions of dollars of attention engineering stand ready to serve the perfect moment of distraction.
Citations: ep01:51 (solitude definition), ep01:49 ("It is in time alone with our minds that we evolve as humans"), ep01:39 (tens of billions of dollars of attention engineering).

### Q3 [A]
Your job requires you to keep an Instagram presence. What are the chapter's exact rules for converting it into a controlled channel?

**Answer.** Convert it to a controlled channel: use it on the desktop only, for the stated purpose only, never the app on the phone, never the back-and-forth. Post deliberately, like Cal's videos, and leave. Public output is fine when it is deliberate. Ambient reaction is the problem.
Citations: ep01:183 (desktop only, stated purpose only, never the app, never the back-and-forth; deliberate output vs ambient reaction).

## Chapter 2 (ep02): Cal Newport's Planning System

### Q4 [N]
The root document divides the system into how many categories, and the weekly plan is built from how many inputs? Name both lists.

**Answer.** Three categories: core documents, productivity, discipline. Four fixed inputs to the weekly plan: the strategic plans, the calendar, the task list, and the values plan.
Citations: ep02:51 (three categories), ep02:97 (four inputs).

### Q5 [E]
Explain like I am new: what is the cascade, and why does it mean you never have to think about your big-picture plans right now?

**Answer.** The cascade is the chain: the strategic plan influences the weekly plan, the weekly plan feeds the daily plan, the daily plan determines what you do right now. Because the thinking happens at the right level and flows downhill, what you do right now is shaped by your big-picture strategic plans, but you never have to think about your big-picture plans right now. The present moment serves the strategy without carrying it.
Citations: ep02:105 (the cascade chain), ep02:216 (levels cascade; "You never think about the big picture in the present moment, yet the present moment serves it").

### Q6 [A]
You are in a burnout month and your elaborate tracking system is collapsing. What does the chapter prescribe?

**Answer.** Contract the system to the basics: the values, the core things that must get done, the minimum that pulls you out of the depths. Do not invent a new system when stressed. Return to the standard system, open the root document, and get the standard pieces running again. The stress came from system-hopping, not from missing features.
Citations: ep02:161 (contract to the basics: values, core things, minimum), ep02:244 (do not invent a new system; return to the standard system).

## Chapter 3 (ep03): Core Idea: Deep Work

### Q7 [N]
You write a strategy memo for 60 minutes, quick-checking email every 6 minutes. How many context switches did you make, and which bucket does the session fall in?

**Answer.** 60 / 6 = 10 switches. The session is pseudo-deep work: cognitively demanding but interrupted. It does not count as deep work, because deep work requires the switches to stay at zero.
Citations: ep03:63 (email check every 5 to 6 minutes, about 10 switches; pseudo-deep work row), ep03:45 (context shift definition; switches must stay at zero).

### Q8 [E]
Explain like I am new: what is the difference between shallow work and pseudo-deep work, and why is pseudo-deep the most dangerous bucket?

**Answer.** Shallow work is not cognitively demanding: 60 minutes formatting the PowerPoint chart with perfect focus is shallow, even though nothing switches. Pseudo-deep work is cognitively demanding but full of context shifts: the hard strategy memo with the email checks. Pseudo-deep is the most dangerous because it feels productive while operating at a fraction of capability. Ten hours of pseudo-deep is ten hours at a fraction of capability, not ten hours of deep work.
Citations: ep03:64 (shallow work row: formatting the chart, nothing cognitively demanding), ep03:63 (pseudo-deep: a fraction of capability, degraded by every switch), ep03:223 (trap card: "Ten hours of pseudo-deep is ten hours at a fraction of capability").

### Q9 [A]
A teammate brags: "I put in ten hard hours yesterday. Deep work done." Diagnose the claim using the chapter's buckets.

**Answer.** Hours are not depth. A hard task alone does not make a session deep. If those ten hours contained any context shifts, they were pseudo-deep: hard plus switching equals pseudo-deep, delivered at a fraction of capability. Only a hard task with zero switches counts as deep work.
Citations: ep03:223 (trap card: hours are not depth), ep03:63 (pseudo-deep row), ep03:45 (switches must stay at zero).

## Chapter 4 (ep04): Digital Minimalism

### Q10 [N]
Your backwards audit found 8 apps that serve no value. Using the drill's own numbers, what did they cost you this week and this month?

**Answer.** At 20 minutes each per day: 8 x 20 = 160 minutes a day. Per week: 160 x 7 = 1,120 minutes, about 18.7 hours. Per month: about 1,120 x 4 = 4,480 minutes, about 74.7 hours.
Citations: ep04:160 (the drill: "if each none-app took 20 minutes, what did the valueless apps cost you this week? This month?").

### Q11 [E]
Explain like I am new: what is digital minimalism, and why is it not "use less technology"?

**Answer.** Digital minimalism has nothing to do with minimization and everything to do with intention. Minimization says less is better, fewer technologies. Cal rejects that completely: he is a computer scientist and a nerd who likes technology. Digital minimalism means starting from a well-formed vision of the life you want, working backwards to the best use of technology for each value, and putting rules and fences around it. You seek out technology, on your terms.
Citations: ep04:78 (not minimization; everything to do with intention), ep04:76 (Cal is a nerd who likes technology; rejects the less-is-better reading), ep04:90 (fences follow from the why).

### Q12 [A]
You joined a local cycling club's Facebook group but now catch yourself scrolling the news feed. Apply the episode's method step by step.

**Answer.** Value first: social connection and exercise. Technology chosen backwards: the Facebook group for local rides. Fences from the why: install a browser plugin that strips the news feed off the screen, log in only on the computer and never the phone, go straight to the group, get exactly what you came for, and leave. Same person, same platform, but now the technology serves the value instead of the person serving the platform's engagement metrics.
Citations: ep04:90 (the fences: plugin strips the feed, desktop only, straight to the group, then leave), ep04:197 (all three steps in miniature; the technology serves the value).

## Chapter 5 (ep05): Core Idea: Slow Productivity

### Q13 [N]
You carry 6 projects. Each brings 1 weekly meeting (1 hour) plus 24 coordination emails (1 hour). Compute your weekly overhead and the hours left for execution in a 45-hour week.

**Answer.** Fixed overhead per project is 2 hours, so 6 x 2 = 12 hours of weekly overhead. Time left for execution: 45 - 12 = 33 hours.
Citations: ep05:100 (1 meeting plus 24 emails = 2 hours per project per week), ep05:102 (43 hours left for 1 project, negative 5 for 25; same arithmetic).

### Q14 [E]
Explain like I am new: why does Cal say treating yourself like a computer processor is the design error behind overload?

**Answer.** A computer processor wants its pipeline of instructions full so it never misses a tick. Modern work management applies that model to humans: keep the queue full, never idle. Cal says you are not wired for that. The paleolithic baseline shows the correct model: one or two skilled things a day, with a two-hour midday rest inside an all-day hunt, intensity going up and down. Treating people like processors produces the relentless pace that overload depends on.
Citations: ep05:113 (processor wants its pipeline full; "You are a human. You are not wired for that"), ep05:55 (all-day hunt with a two-hour midday rest; one or two things, not eighteen).

### Q15 [A]
You manage an engineer whose plate is full, and a new request just landed on her desk. What must you do, exactly?

**Answer.** Workloads must be transparent, with a shared understanding of what is reasonable. If her plate is full, the new request cannot just land on it. You must figure out where it goes until someone has room. The mechanism is the external pull system: obligations go into a shared system with their information, status, and priority visible, and individuals pull work out as slots open. Pull, not push.
Citations: ep05:115 (transparent workloads; a new request cannot just land on a full plate; the manager figures out where it goes), ep05:117 (external systems; pull, not push).

## Chapter 6 (ep06): The Slope of Terribleness

### Q16 [N]
The curation algorithms represent content as vectors across roughly how many descriptive categories, and how many posts per day force the need to curate?

**Answer.** Roughly a thousand descriptive categories. And 500 million people submitting posts a day, which is why curation is mandatory.
Citations: ep06:138 (embeddings across something like a thousand descriptive categories), ep06:158 (with 500 million people submitting posts a day, you must curate).

### Q17 [E]
Explain like I am new: why is dopamine a motivation chemical rather than a pleasure chemical?

**Answer.** Because of what the circuit does. It looks at available activities, predicts outcomes from past learning, and if the prediction is positive, dopamine floods the circuit and the activity feels irresistible before you act. The cookie example: the circuit learned the cookie felt good, so the sight of the next cookie triggers motivation to eat it. Pleasure happens after. Dopamine is the push, not the reward.
Citations: ep06:116 (prediction circuits; the cookie example), ep06:247 ("Pleasure happens after. Dopamine is the push, not the reward").

### Q18 [A]
Someone proposes fixing a platform with a purely chronological feed. Run the Pizza Hut test and give the verdict.

**Answer.** The test asks: does the fix remove conversation at scale, or engagement-optimized curation? A chronological feed removes engagement-optimized curation, so the slope dynamics would not follow. But that is the Pizza Hut verdict: with 500 million posts a day, curation is mandatory, and a curated-on-engagement feed is the service itself. Asking for Twitter without those properties is like asking Pizza Hut to stay in business without selling pizza. No implementation mistake causes the slope; the design guarantees it, so any fix that keeps the service keeps the slope.
Citations: ep06:259 (the test: does the fix remove either property; if not, the slope survives), ep06:158 (Pizza Hut analogy; 500 million posts a day; the design itself guarantees it).

## Chapter 7 (ep07): Make Learning As Addictive As Social Media

### Q19 [N]
Dylan's fixed study method does seven topics an hour. In a 3-hour study block, how many topics does he cover, and what is the exact per-topic protocol?

**Answer.** 3 x 7 = 21 topics. Per topic: five flashcards, one out-loud lecture, check it off, next topic. Then keep what works.
Citations: ep07:154 (seven topics an hour; "five flashcards, one out-loud lecture per topic, check it off, next topic, seven topics an hour").

### Q20 [E]
Explain like I am new: what is the difference between deliberate practice and flow, and why does learning need the first?

**Answer.** Flow is the loss of self in an activity you can already do: the downhill skier dissolved into the run, time disappearing, pleasurable. Deliberate practice is the opposite: the push past what you can comfortably do, full concentration, mistakes, misery. The guitarist concentrating so hard he forgot to breathe is deliberate practice. You never get lost practicing because the strain will not let you. Learning runs on the second, not the first: capability moves forward only in the strain.
Citations: ep07:263 (flow vs deliberate practice; "Learning runs on the second, not the first"), ep07:67 (Csikszentmihalyi flow; "You never get lost practicing, because the push is so hard it is miserable").

### Q21 [A]
A friend says Duolingo streaks and mascots will make Spanish as addictive as TikTok. Give Cal's two-part verdict and the real alternative.

**Answer.** Part one: the claim is doomed to failure. Gamifying learning fights TikTok on the dopamine system's home turf, and learning requires strain, which is unpleasant, and no amount of owls can engineer that away. TikTok's reward is pure; the learning app's reward always has strain subtracted. Part two: the lasting alternative is the episodic future thinking playbook. First, fill the hippocampus with resonant examples: biographies, profiles, talks, real people who converted a love of learning into a life you admire. Second, clarify your values: name what is at stake down to family, craft, or impact. Then the valued future feels better than the quick hit, and you endure the strain without the owl.
Citations: ep07:41 (verdict part one: "doomed to failure"), ep07:63 (learning requires strain; no amount of owls can engineer that away), ep07:115 (fill the hippocampus with resonant examples), ep07:117 (clarify your values).

## Chapter 8 (ep08): The 5-Step Productivity System

### Q22 [N]
Compute Felipe's overhead math: four 1-hour deep work sessions versus one 4-hour block, given a 30-minute spin-up per session.

**Answer.** Four 1-hour sessions: 30 minutes spin-up each leaves 30 minutes of full-cylinder work each, so 4 x 30 = 120 minutes, or 2 hours. One 4-hour block: 30 minutes spin-up, then 210 minutes at full speed, or 3.5 hours. Overhead is paid per start, so fewer starts mean more depth.
Citations: ep08:241-243 (spin-up 30 minutes; 30 vs 210 minutes per session; 2 hours vs 3.5 hours total), ep08:231 (30 minutes spin-up; four blocks give about two hours), ep08:233 (three and a half hours at full speed).

### Q23 [E]
Explain like I am new: what is the one-item rule, and why is it strict?

**Answer.** Every obligation gets exactly one item in the system. It can move between lists as its status changes. It never duplicates. One item, one status, one source of truth. It is strict because it prevents the classic failure: the same obligation tracked in two places, updated in neither, trusted in none. In database terms, the lists are views over a single status field.
Citations: ep08:139 (one item; moves between lists; cannot exist on multiple lists), ep08:352 (one item, one status, one source of truth; the two-places failure).

### Q24 [A]
You must drop a backburner commitment you agreed to help with. What is the exact move, and what is its quantified trade?

**Answer.** Triage the backburner and send the sorry message early, weeks ahead of the deadline. The trade: about seven seconds of annoyance for the recipient, about seven hours of freedom for the sender. Sent early, the withdrawal reads as competence, this person has their act together. Sent at the deadline, it reads as chaos. Early honesty builds trust. Late honesty is just a confession.
Citations: ep08:164 (triage the backburner; seven seconds of annoyance, seven hours of freedom; early reads as competence), ep08:360 (early honesty builds trust; late honesty is just a confession).
