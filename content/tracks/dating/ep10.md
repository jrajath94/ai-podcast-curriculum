# Episode 10: The Relationship Crisis

Guest: Esther Perel, relationship therapist with 40 years of practice, author of Mating in Captivity and The State of Affairs. This episode aired 2025-06-12 and ran 5302 seconds.

The episode makes one claim: the relationship crisis is not a sex crisis. It is a connection crisis. Social atrophy, the loss of the skills and places of connection, starves everything downstream: dating, desire, and trust. The repair is not a technique. It is details, presence, and the willingness to stay in the room.

## The episode in one paragraph

Perel's diagnosis is social atrophy: like an unused muscle going numb, our social skills decay from disuse, and we are a species wired for connection that now gets almost no practice. The dating app is a tool being used as a replacement: the swipe loop burns users out, teaches low-effort contact, and manufactures the misery of semi-anonymous treatment, ghosting, and the quiet closing of the shop. Her case study is the man who swiped 2,058,000 times over nearly six years for 2,000 matches, 1,200 chats, and one date. The sexual recession, 1 in 3 young men without partner sex, porn instead of partners, young men as the majority of erectile-dysfunction cases, follows from the same atrophy: no social map, no attunement. At home, the screen creates ambiguous loss: physically present, psychologically gone, the phone's still face across the couch. On infidelity, she argues that confessing to cleanse your own guilt can be cruelty dressed as honesty. On monogamy, women get bored sooner than men, and the fix is aliveness, not novelty. Couples fight for three things only: power and control, trust and closeness, respect and recognition. And the closing prescription is small: say hello, talk to strangers, sing together, learn to have conflict, and send the sweet nothing. Life is lived in the details.

## Social atrophy: the muscle nobody exercises

Atrophy is what happens to a muscle you stop using: it goes numb, then it goes. Perel applies the word to social skill. We are wired for connection, and the evidence she cites is longevity: we live longer because we are connected, not because we are master biohackers. Connection is not a lifestyle accessory. It is vital.

The practice fields keep closing. Her plane story: three hours talking with two strangers because there was no Wi-Fi, and all three agreed the conversation would never happen with connectivity. Every dead zone used to be a social gym. Now the gyms are empty.

Her anger at the apps is specific. The app was a tool for broadening your circle, and it became a replacement for every other way of meeting. The loop she describes: swipe, swipe, swipe, respond with minimum effort because you are already burned out, get no matches because minimum effort invites none, blame the app, quit for six months, return to the app instead of going outside. And the original design goal, she says, was to avoid rejection. But rejection is a major feature of relationships. Learning to live with people saying no is essential equipment, and the app trains you to never pick it up.

## The 2,058,000 swipes

The episode's hardest number belongs to one man. He swiped 2,058,000 times. That produced 2,000 matches, which produced 1,200 chats, which produced one date. He spent just over five years, almost six, on the apps. His lead photo held a massive fish.

Work the funnel. Matches per swipe: 2,000 divided by 2,058,000 equals 0.000972, about 0.097 percent, roughly one match per 1,029 swipes. Chats per match: 1,200 divided by 2,000 equals 0.60, a 60 percent chat rate. Dates per chat: 1 divided by 1,200 equals 0.000833, about 0.083 percent. Dates per swipe: 1 in 2,058,000. Over five and a half years, the swipe rate is 2,058,000 divided by 5.5, about 374,000 swipes a year, about 1,025 a day. One thousand swipes a day, every day, for five and a half years, for one date.

Perel's response is not about his photos, though she notes the fish. It is about rigidity: "more of it is not going to give you more of it." Five years is an enormous amount of a life. Her parking-space analogy: if you cannot get into the space, at some point you look for another space. The questions she would ask are all off-app: do you have friends, do your friends introduce you to people, have you gone where your interests live.

The deeper charge is commodification. The app began as circle-broadening and became emotional capitalism: people treat each other as inventory, ghost left and right, and never say "I am not interested," they just close the shop. Perel's line on the misery: the bitterness comes from the treatment the semi-anonymity enables, not from meeting no one. Nobody has to be polite anymore, and the bitterness that follows gets misread as a verdict on the self.

## The sexual recession and ambiguous loss

The statistics: 1 in 3 men under 30 reported no sex in the past year, triple the 2008 rate. Over 40 percent of young adults in Japan are virgins, many uninterested. Married couples in the UK and US report steady sexual decline since the early 2000s. Perel wrote about this twenty years ago in Mating in Captivity, so the trend is old. Her causal chain: social atrophy first, sex last. Adolescents now socialize in groups with little pair bonding, so romance arrives as an unknown continent with no map, no female friends, no rehearsal. Partnered sex is the casualty. Solo sex continues: "they have sex on porn." And the clinical tell: the majority of men coming to sex therapy for erectile dysfunction are now young men in their twenties, not older men with prostate issues. Years of solo porn use train the body to respond to the self only. Partnered erection is attunement, resonance, two systems grooving. The solo-trained body never learned the duet.

At home, the screen makes its own diagnosis: nine hours a day in front of it, then TV plus phone scrolling on the couch, the partner's "uh-huh" without a glance. Perel's term is ambiguous loss, from psychologist Pauline Boss [uncertain: transcript says "Pauline Bos"]: the person is physically present and psychologically gone, like Alzheimer's in reverse, or the deployed soldier, or the miscarriage, present inside you but not in the room. You never know whether to hold on or say goodbye. The phone's still face is the new ambiguous loss: with you, but in another world.

The repair is demarcation. Carve out clean time, a half hour that is actually clean, and protect it like gym time: the clothes, the place, the role, the boundary. Perel insists the clean half hour is not a gift to her. It pays him: drop the phone, tell people to call in an hour, and the oxytocin and energy beat any supplement. The collapsed-roles feeling, boss, podcaster, boyfriend all bleeding together, is indigestion. Delineation is digestion.

## Infidelity, boredom, and the moon

Three friends' questions get three answers. Can I be satisfied with one sexual partner? Perel's reframe: women get bored with monogamy sooner than men, which men misread as women not wanting sex. The correction: she is not interested in the sex on offer. For her to stay interested, it must be worth wanting: interesting, pleasurable, alive. The double standard kept women home historically. The boredom is not new, only newly speakable.

Should I confess past infidelity? Her answer is the episode's most quoted: telling someone just to clean your own conscience is not always kind. "Honesty sometimes is extremely caring and at other times can be very cruel. It cleanses you and it destroys another person." Deal with your guilt, take responsibility, and invest the energy into the relationship instead of demolishing its shared narrative. Transparency is not automatically the moral option.

How do I tell her I no longer find her attractive? First, check what the sentence means. Attraction is fluid: you are not attracted when angry, and you run to hug her when she is kind to a stranger. Three hours of Netflix plus two of scrolling does not manufacture desire, and the new shiny object only looks better because interaction is zero. Sometimes the body carries old hurt: the woman at her event talked everything out, but her body shut down, carrying anger the conversation never reached. Attraction shares a root with interaction. No interaction, no attraction.

John's letter, the 25-year case, gets the full prescription. His options: open the relationship, which she will refuse, or make the sex worth wanting. "Have you been doing the same old for god knows how many years?" Bring playfulness, curiosity, imagination, eroticism as life force, not as performance. Do not bring the leftovers home while the best of you goes to work. And accept the trade-off: wanting everything is the disease. His script for her: something died long ago, I miss you, I miss us, desire moves through eclipses like the moon, it can reappear, will you re-engage and do your part. The moon image is the whole theory: disappearance is not death.

Her summary line on dead bedrooms: "Is the sex getting less interesting? No. Their life with each other is less interesting." Sex is the consequence of seven things before it: they have not laughed, kissed, looked, touched, or said anything interesting. Engagement produces performance, at work and in bed. Low engagement, low output. Same mechanism.

## The self, the fight, and the details

The self-care culture gets a correction. The premise, fix your relationship with yourself and the rest follows, is, in her word, overboard: a distortion that feeds consumerism. What actually roots well-being is connection with others. Giving raises your oxytocin too. The gym image: people super engaged with themselves, lifting alone, interacting with no one, going home to no one. "Relating to yourself is not a goal in and of itself." It is a step toward relating to others.

On emasculation, she refuses the frame. Emasculation has no feminine equivalent. Masculinity is an identity that must constantly prove itself, or it would not need the proving. The gender-war discourse, useless men versus trash men, gets no participation from her. Fathers everywhere work day and night to give their families better lives. Her fishbowl retreats put men in the center talking about shame, loss, and doubt while everyone listens without judgment, and strangers weep. "Humanity is bigger than gender."

Confidence gets Terry Real's definition: seeing yourself as flawed and still holding yourself in high regard. It builds layer by layer, and accelerating it is called arrogance. The mystery of resilience, why the same events build one person and break another, has one known differentiator: somebody believed in them when they did not believe in themselves.

Meaning is made, not found. We are meaning-making machines, and the culture supplies the labels: anxiety today, unhappy ancestors elsewhere. The trauma discussion lands on the empathic witness: what makes an event traumatic is often experiencing it without anyone acknowledging it happened. The JFK airport image: three lines, tourist, citizen, resident alien, internal versus external identity, never quite matching.

Asked for three global steps against atrophy, she answers in details, not laws. Say hello like the town she visited where everyone did. Talk to strangers. It is art, not legislation. Make people laugh or sing together. Collective resonance is holy work. And teach people to have conflict, because most fights are about three things, from Howard Markman's work: power and control, who decides and whose priorities win. Trust, care, and closeness, do you have my back. Respect and recognition, do I matter. The workplace version, built with Culture Amp on 1.5 billion survey points, names four pillars: trust, belonging, recognition, and collective resilience. Trust itself: "an active engagement with the unknown," in Rachel Botsman's [uncertain: transcript says "Rachel Butsman"] phrase. Not certainty. Willingness.

The AI question gets the tool answer: use it to generate ideas with people, and hope it never reshapes us into a species we would not recognize. The last word is the smallest: send the sweet nothing. Tell her she is on your mind. "Life is lived in the details."

## How to imbibe this

This week, practice presence as a skill. First, create one clean half hour daily: phone off and out of the room, with one person or with yourself, and protect it like training. Second, talk to one stranger a day: the coffee-shop line, the dog walker, the plane seat. Log the rejections and the surprises. Third, audit your ambiguous loss: ask the person closest to you whether you are ever physically present but gone, and listen to the answer without defending. Fourth, send one sweet nothing daily: a text that says they are on your mind, with no request attached. Fifth, name your last three arguments and sort each into power, trust, or respect. Fight the real one next time.

5. Map the self-care industry onto the episode's critique: which parts survive as genuine steps toward relating, and which parts are the distortion? Draw the line with examples.
6. You are made president against atrophy with Perel's three details, hello, strangers, singing, plus conflict skills. Write the one-week implementation for a city of 100,000. Then name how you would know it worked.

## Memory aids

Mnemonic: **Atrophy, Loss, Moon, Three.** Social atrophy, ambiguous loss, the moon's eclipses, the three fight-fors.

Never-confuse pairs:
- **Atrophy vs choice.** Nobody chose to lose the skill. Disuse chose for them. The fix is practice, not willpower.
- **Honesty vs cruelty.** Confession that cleanses you and destroys them is cruelty in honesty's clothes. Ask who the telling serves.
- **Boredom vs low desire.** She is rarely bored with sex. She is bored with the sex on offer. Worth wanting is the variable.
- **Topic vs fight-for.** The dishes are the topic. Power, trust, or respect is the fight. Argue the second.

Trap cards:
- **The tool trap.** The app is a tool. Used as a replacement for every third place, it becomes the disease it promised to cure.
- **The confession trap.** Transparency as a moral absolute. Sometimes the kindest honesty is the letter never sent and the guilt carried into better behavior.
- **The novelty trap.** New partner, new shine, same zero interaction. The moon wanes everywhere. Eclipses pass.
- **The self trap.** Optimizing the self while the species runs on connection. Relating to yourself is a step, not the destination.

## Q&A

**Q1. Explain like I am new: what is social atrophy?**
It is the decay of social skill from disuse, named for what happens to an unused muscle. Humans are wired for connection. We even live longer when connected. Every place we used to practice, planes without Wi-Fi, shops, streets, gets replaced by screens, so the skill numbs. The dating crisis, the sex recession, and the loneliness numbers are all downstream of the same numbness. The cure is embarrassingly small: talk to strangers, daily, on purpose.

**Q2. What does the swipe-funnel arithmetic prove, and what does it not prove?**
It proves the strategy failed: 1,025 swipes a day for five and a half years yielding one date is not bad luck, it is a broken method. The 0.097 percent match rate and the 0.083 percent chat-to-date rate quantify the rigidity. It does not prove the man is undatable, the apps are useless for everyone, or better photos would fix it. Perel's point is structural: more of a failing input does not produce more output. Change the input.

**Q3. Why can telling the truth about infidelity be cruel?**
Because confession serves two masters: the relationship and the confessor's conscience. When the affair is over and the telling cannot repair anything, only unburden the guilty, the honesty cleanses one person and demolishes the other's entire narrative of the relationship. Perel's rule asks who the telling is for. If it is for you, carry the guilt into better behavior instead. Honesty is not automatically kind. Sometimes it is cruelty with good branding.

**Q4. Applied: a couple reports a dead bedroom. Run Perel's diagnostic.**
Do not start with the bedroom. Audit the seven things before it: have they laughed, kissed, looked at each other, touched, said anything interesting this week? Check screens: nine hours a day plus couch scrolling predicts ambiguous loss, presence without psychology. Check the story: is she uninterested in sex, or uninterested in the sex on offer? Prescribe aliveness before technique: clean time, curiosity, playfulness, the moon letter if something died. Measure the life together first. The sex is the last number, not the first.

**Q5. What is ambiguous loss, and why does the phone cause it?**
Ambiguous loss: being with someone whose presence is uncertain: physically there, psychologically gone. The term comes from Alzheimer's and deployment, where you never know whether to hold on or say goodbye. The phone creates the civilian version: the still face, the "uh-huh" without a glance, the person in another world while sitting beside you. The partner cannot grieve the absence because the body is right there. Clean, demarcated time is the treatment: presence with boundaries, or honest absence.

**Q6. Explain the three fight-fors with one fresh example.**
A couple fights about the dishwasher. Power and control: who decided the chore split, and whose standards rule? Trust, care, closeness: when I am sick, will you handle it without keeping score? Respect and recognition: do you notice that I do this daily? The dishwasher is never the fight. Ask "what are we fighting for" and the argument drops one level to the real one, where it can actually be resolved.

**Q7. Why does Perel refuse the gender-war framing, and what does she offer instead?**
Because it misdescribes the species. Emasculation has no feminine equivalent, masculinity must constantly prove itself, and the discourse of useless men versus trash women flattens fathers who work day and night for their families. Her alternative is the human layer beneath gender: fishbowl retreats where men speak shame aloud and strangers weep, because "humanity is bigger than gender." The offer is better conversations, not better arguments. Connection first, categories second.

## Skills you can now use

- **Atrophy audit.** Map where your social practice happens weekly, and add one third place.
- **Funnel math.** Convert any effort into rates: per-unit yields expose broken strategies faster than feelings do.
- **Presence demarcation.** Build clean time with phone-off boundaries and protect it like training.
- **Loss naming.** Spot ambiguous loss in yourself and name it to the person affected, without defense.
- **Confession ethics.** Test any hard truth with "who is this for" before speaking it.
- **Fight sorting.** Translate any argument into power, trust, or respect, and fight that one.
- **Detail practice.** Send the sweet nothing, say hello, sing along: run the small things that compound.
