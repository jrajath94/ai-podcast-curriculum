# Episode 6: Modern Dating Is Impossible - Here's The Playbook

Guest: Orion Taraban, psychologist and author of the book The Value of Others. This episode aired 2024-04-11 and ran 6484 seconds.

The episode makes one claim: dating is a market, and markets run on value, transactions, and negotiation. Men who understand the market can trade well. Men who moralize about it stay poor in it.

## The episode in one paragraph

Taraban opens with the General Social Survey: 1 in 3 men under 30 had no sex in the last 12 months, triple the rate of 10 years ago, leaving them functionally invisible in the sexual marketplace. His model: men trade resources, everything except sex, for sexual opportunity, while women trade sexual opportunity for resources. Online dating breaks the negotiation because the two sides live in opposite illusions: he sees an infinite sea of women and devalues sex, she sees an infinite sea of men and runs a seller's market. The fixes are structural, not moral: leave the apps or use them narrowly, unbundle marriage into gig relationships, hold commitment as long as it appreciates, and never try to negotiate desire directly. The episode closes with the deconstruction of love itself: strip away the named parts and what remains is the willingness to be used exhaustively for the other's good.

## The market and the invisible third

Start with the numbers. The General Social Survey finds that 1 in 3 men under 30 reported no sex in the past year. That rate tripled in 10 years. Work it: tripled means the rate a decade ago was about 1 in 9. Today it is 1 in 3. Taraban's term for these men is "functionally invisible in the sexual marketplace": present in the population, absent from the market.

Then the crossover. Age 30 is the point where the average man's sexual marketplace value exceeds the average woman's for the first time, and it never reverses. Her value peaks early and declines. His builds with status, resources, and competence. The strategic read: a man's twenties are for building value, not for locking in its price.

The transaction model underneath: men exchange resources for sexual opportunity, and resources means everything except sex, money, attention, validation, support, children, commitment, time. Women exchange sexual opportunity for resources. His Ken and Barbie analogy: men want the Barbie doll, which is why objectification talk angers women, and women want everything the Ken doll comes with, where the doll itself is a cipher. Female chastity sits in the model ambivalently: highly valued in a wife, frustrating obstruction in a lover. Same trait, opposite prices in different markets.

His line for women in their twenties: they spend the decade on hedonism and career, then find it harder to secure relationships on their terms after 30. And the rule for when women consider a long-term relationship at all: only if they perceive significant benefit. "If it is a wash, it is a loss."

## The online dating paradox: two illusions, one market

Figure 1 is the episode's centerpiece. Online dating puts the two sides in opposite subjective worlds. His experience: one man in an infinite sea of women. When supply looks infinite, the perceived cost of sex collapses, which manufactures the fuckboy: why commit when someone else is always one swipe away. Her experience: one woman in an infinite sea of men. When demand looks infinite, she runs a seller's market and transacts her sexual opportunity for maximum resources. Her "looking for a serious committed relationship" is the gender-swapped equivalent of his "just looking to fuck": each states the thing the other side rations. Note the price ranking the episode asserts: commitment is worth far more than sex in market value.

The gap between the illusions widens until negotiation becomes impossible. He will not pay her price because his world says sex is cheap. She will not accept his terms because her world says her scarcity deserves a premium. Both reason correctly from false premises.

Taraban's solutions are trade-offs, not fixes. Get off the apps, or use the apps for narrow purposes and not others. There is no pure solution because the paradox is structural: the interface manufactures both illusions at once.

## Gig relationships: unbundle the marriage

Marriage, Taraban argues, is hyper-conflated: one contract tries to be a legal agreement, a religious commitment, a cohabitation, a co-parenting deal, a passionate romance, a best friendship, a therapy relationship, and a division of domestic labor. A chain is only as strong as its weakest link, so the bundled marriage fails at its weakest role and takes the rest down with it.

The alternative is gig relationships, modeled on the gig economy: unbundle the roles and staff them separately, with clear trade-offs instead of one template. This is not presented as net positive or net negative. It is presented as honest: every arrangement has a price, so pick your prices consciously.

The affair numbers explain why men quietly unbundle already. Taraban says over 50 percent of the entrepreneurs he knows have had affairs, counting only the ones who admit it. His model of male success: men became successful to get access to more and better reproductive opportunities. The strategic advice follows: hold off long-term commitment as late as possible, because a man's opportunities grow with his status, wealth, and prestige.

Monogamish is the named middle: 90 to 95 percent sexually exclusive, rather than 100. The logic is pure trade-off math. At 100 percent, temptation plus opportunity eventually produces lying, cheating, or dissolution. At 90 to 95 percent, the arrangement survives contact with reality. His line: if your 95 percent is significantly better than the 100 percent she could get from other men, she will not cut off her nose to spite her face. He adds the cultural data: Asian and French cultures tolerate affairs with rules, do not embarrass me, do not bring a child home. His rule of thumb is that non-Judeo-Christian and non-Islamic cultures are more tolerant. The West's problem, in his framing, makes equality and egalitarianism the supreme value where Confucian cultures run on responsibilities and privileges.

## Cowardice, pride, and the enemies within

Taraban names each sex's greatest enemy. The man's greatest enemy is cowardice. Afraid to approach: the Me Too era, the fear of being recorded and canceled. Afraid to lead: raised on 50/50, the man who knows the way will not say so. His rule: the person who knows should lead.

The woman's greatest enemy is pride. Ideological feminism, in his telling, prescribes against what men most want from women, and it devalues domesticity, wifehood, and motherhood as valid choices. His line: you cannot have everything. The trade-off framing again: every choice prices something else.

The hypergamy section applies the market to successful women. Women pair up in status: older, taller, stronger, richer. A woman making 2 million dollars a year faces a tiny hypergamous pool. But the law of marginal utility mutes the demand: at 2 million, another 100,000 means little, so she starts pricing other traits. His data point from divorce lawyers: housewives are the most litigious in divorce, which complicates the story that female earning protects marriage.

## Why marriages fail: the four mechanisms

The episode lists four, and they stack into one theory of value decay.

One: marriage is too many things, the conflation problem above. Two: the partner's value changes as your goals evolve. What you needed at 25 is not what you need at 40, and the contract assumed a fixed person. Three: the plumber paradox. "No one continues to pay the plumber." Once the want is discharged, the value of the provider drops. The game does not run on gratitude. Four: value drops as wants get discharged generally, which is the same mechanism as three, stated at the level of the whole relationship.

## Negotiation: desire has no price tag

"You cannot negotiate desire." The episode's example: a contract for sex twice a week produces compliance without desire, the starfish. The negotiation failed because desire is not a term. It is the thing the terms are about.

So negotiate inexplicitly. The episode's line: the fundamental game of human relationships is negotiation, but no script works on everyone. Read: calibrate to the person, trade in the unsaid, and never mistake a signed agreement for attraction.

Three market micro-rules close the practical section. Adored versus adorer: everyone wants to be the adorer, because loving feels better than being loved, but it is better for the man to be the adored, because she needs the emotional dimension and he can hold from duty. The discount rule: the more women like you, the bigger the discount. The attracted woman removes obstacles and fits into your life, the less-attracted woman charges more, with commitment as compensation. And testing: women test men the way buyers test-drive cars. Nobody test-drives for fun. The buyer stresses the car to find defects before signing, then treats it gently after. The test is a buying signal, not an attack.

Bold versus boring is the posture rule. Short-term, err on too bold: be the peacock, not the wallpaper. Most men are wallpaper. Boldness gives her the tingles, and "if you can make a woman feel something you can move her toward a sexual relationship." Long-term, err on too boring: give her the average-Tuesday preview, Sweetgreen salads and all, and give her every reason to leave now. Filter early with the truth.

Instagram is the best dating app, in this episode's ranking, because it is a curated peephole into a lifestyle: a lifestyle proposal, not a profile. His proof case: the unemployed musician in his parents' basement who still has sex tonight through emotional responsiveness. The product is the feeling, not the resume.

## Love, deconstructed

The episode's hardest passage takes love apart. Subtract everything that has another name: passion is romance and lust, companionate love is kindness and loyalty. What remains, Taraban says, is "the humiliated self exalted": the willingness to sacrifice anything for the loved one's good, to be used exhaustively. His images: the Giving Tree, ground down to a stump, and Jesus. Then the cold line: "Love generally does not work in relationships," because the lover is rarely the loved one's best option.

"I love you" gets translated four ways, all from the episode: do not leave me, do not be mad at me, tell me you love me, forgive me. The phrase is a bid, not a report.

The book's thesis, The Value of Others, closes the system: relationships are the medium in which value is transacted, people enter them with their perceived best option, and "if you have more of what more people want, you will never be alone." Nobody deserves anything. Earn optionality by cultivating value.

The consultation numbers show the market for the advice itself: 500 dollars an hour, booked a month out, 100 inquiries a day. The clients sort by age and state: women in their thirties ask how to get married, young men want access, married men want the dynamic fixed, divorced older men are re-entering.

## How to imbibe this

This week, price yourself like a market. First, write your value inventory: what do you have that more people want, in the episode's currency of resources? Be specific and honest. Second, audit your app usage against the paradox: if you use apps, name the narrow purpose, or delete them for 30 days and log what changes. Third, catch one cowardice moment daily: the approach not made, the lead not taken, and write what it cost. Fourth, run the Tuesday preview on yourself: describe your average Tuesday honestly, then ask whether you show it early enough to filter well. Fifth, translate one "I love you," yours or hers, into the episode's four bids, and notice which one fits.

## Think it yourself

Brain first, no AI. The drills are now negotiations and pricings, the episode's home turf.

1. A man earns 400k, works 80-hour weeks, and cannot get dates. Price his problem with the transaction model: what is he rich in, what is he poor in, and what trade would you advise?
2. She wants a serious relationship. He wants something casual. Using the paradox, explain why direct negotiation fails here, then design the inexplicit negotiation: what does each one do instead of saying?
3. A couple signs a twice-a-week sex contract. Predict the outcome with the episode's logic, then design the alternative that might actually produce desire.
4. Unbundle a marriage into gig pieces for a hypothetical couple: list five roles from the episode's bundle and assign each a staffing solution. Then name what is lost in the unbundling.
5. Apply the discount rule: two women are interested in you, one clearly more attracted than the other. What changes in how you negotiate commitment with each? Write the difference in two sentences.
6. Taraban says love, deconstructed, is the willingness to be used exhaustively. Argue against him: give the best case that something named remains after his subtraction, in four sentences.

## Memory aids

Mnemonic: **Market, Paradox, Gig, Plumber.** The four stations: the transaction market, the online paradox, gig relationships, the plumber's decay.

Never-confuse pairs:
- **Price vs worth.** The market prices you. Your worth is a different ledger. Confusing them makes you bitter at buyers.
- **Adorer vs adored.** Everyone wants to be the adorer. The man should aim to be the adored. Wanting and getting want different seats.
- **Bold vs boring.** Bold for short-term, boring for long-term. Same man, opposite posture, different market.
- **Negotiate vs demand.** Desire cannot be negotiated directly. It can only be arranged for inexplicitly.

Trap cards:
- **The app trap.** Using the app as the whole strategy while the paradox eats your pricing. Narrow the purpose or leave.
- **The gratitude trap.** Expecting loyalty as payment for past value. The game does not run on gratitude.
- **The 100 percent trap.** Demanding total exclusivity and getting lying instead. Price the 90 to 95 honestly or pay the policing cost.
- **The love-word trap.** Hearing "I love you" as a report. It is a bid: do not leave me, do not be mad, say it back, forgive me.

## Q&A

**Q1. Explain like I am new: what is the sexual marketplace?**
It is the dating world treated as a market. Everyone brings something to trade and wants something in return. Men trade resources, money, attention, time, commitment, everything except sex. Women trade sexual opportunity. Prices move with supply and demand like any market. The model is not romantic. It is predictive: it tells you why the app behaves the way it does and why your twenties are for building value.

**Q2. Why does online dating make negotiation impossible, in the episode's logic?**
Because the interface feeds each side a false picture. He sees endless women, so sex looks cheap and commitment looks foolish. She sees endless men, so she looks scarce and prices herself at a premium. He will not pay her price. She will not accept his terms. Both are rational. Both are wrong about the world. No deal clears because the two sides are not in the same market.

**Q3. What is a gig relationship, and what problem does it solve?**
An unbundled relationship: instead of one marriage contract covering law, religion, housing, parenting, romance, friendship, and therapy, each role gets its own arrangement. It solves the weakest-link problem: in a bundled marriage, failure in one role, say the romance dies, detonates the rest, including the parenting and the housing. Unbundling contains the blast radius. The cost is the loss of the all-in-one story.

**Q4. Applied: a 33-year-old man with rising income asks when to commit. Advise with the crossover and the trade-off logic.**
Tell him the crossover math first: his market value is still appreciating at 33, and male value keeps building with status and resources. Committing now prices the asset early. The trade-off: waiting buys optionality and costs time, including her time if she is older. So the advice is conditional: if he wants children young, the market math bows to biology. If not, holding commitment while building value is the appreciating play. Either way, name the price out loud.

**Q5. Explain the plumber paradox and its dating application.**
Nobody keeps paying the plumber after the leak is fixed. Value lives in the unsatisfied want. Once you satisfy it fully, your price for satisfying it collapses, because gratitude does not sustain payment. In dating, the man who gives everything, total availability, total devotion, total access, discharges every want and becomes redundant. Ep09 will restate this as an axiom. The application: satisfy gradually, and keep future wants satisfiable.

**Q6. Why is it "better for the man to be the adored"?**
Because the two sides get different things from the two seats. Being the adorer feels intrinsically rewarding: loving someone is its own joy. Being adored is emotionally flat by comparison. She needs the emotional dimension more, so she should hold the adorer seat, and he can hold the adored seat on duty and steadiness. Flip it and you get her feeling unloved while he burns out performing devotion he does not feel.

**Q7. What does the test-drive analogy imply about how to handle her tests?**
Those tests signal buying intent. A buyer test-drives to stress the car and find defects before signing, not for fun, and treats it gently after the purchase. So when she tests you, she is a motivated buyer checking for defects. The correct response is to pass the test calmly, not to resent it, lecture her, or fail theatrically. Resenting the test-drive is how you lose the sale.

## Skills you can now use

- **Market pricing.** State any dating situation as a trade: who brings what, who wants what, and whether the prices can clear.
- **Paradox detection.** Spot when an interface feeds both sides false supply pictures, and refuse to negotiate inside the illusion.
- **Unbundling.** Break a relationship bundle into its roles and price each separately, instead of letting one weak role sink the rest.
- **Desire-safe negotiation.** Pursue outcomes inexplicitly: arrange conditions for desire instead of contracting for it.
- **Posture switching.** Run bold for short-term markets and boring for long-term filtering, deliberately, not by mood.
- **Value cultivation.** Inventory what you have that more people want, and invest where the market pays.
