# Episode 3: The Brutal Truth About Female Attraction

Guest: Macken Murphy, an evolutionary psychology researcher and writer. This episode aired 2025-12-11 and ran 9695 seconds.

The episode makes one claim: female attraction follows measurable patterns, but most men read the research wrong. They confuse ideals with requirements, chase the wrong signals, and miss the costs that come with winning.

## The episode in one paragraph

Murphy opens with the most cited study in the episode: women shown 3D models preferred about 6.3 inches in length for a long-term partner, against a population average of 5.16 inches, and 27 percent said size had partly ended a relationship. The lesson is not the number. It is the gap between an ideal preference and a minimum cutoff, which the study never measured. He then builds the formidability thesis: women prefer the man who looks like he would win a fight, with 13 to 14.1 percent body fat as the measured optimum, not the shredded gym look. Career matters to women far less than men think. Men pair assortatively with successful women because a relationship is, in his line, one long podcast, and you want 20,000 hours of good conversation. On body count, CDC medians sit at 4 for women and 6 for men, ideals peak at 1 to 3 past partners, and counts above 5 double the infidelity rate. High mate value has real costs: optionality distress, heavier mate guarding, and cheating that goes up in attractiveness. The episode ends with the paradoxes of modern pairing: hyperandry data, falling divorce rates, and speed-dating proof that leagues are mostly fiction.

## Ideals are not requirements: the 3D-model study

Start with the study because it teaches the master skill of the episode: reading research without fooling yourself. Pruss and colleagues [uncertain: transcript spells "Pruss". The published 3D-model study is likely Prause et al.] showed women 3D models and asked for preferences. For a long-term partner, the average preferred length was 6.3 inches and circumference 4.8 inches. For a short-term partner, 6.4 inches and 5.0 inches. A meta-analysis of 17 studies put the average man at 5.16 inches.

Now work the numbers. The gap between average and ideal is 6.3 minus 5.16, which equals 1.14 inches. Men overestimate their own size by about 20 to 21 percent. The episode's rule of thumb is that a man is about an inch smaller than he claims. So a man who claims 6.3 inches probably measures around 5.3 inches, still below the stated ideal. Murphy adds the percentile: 6.3 inches sits around the 95th percentile, about as common as a man being 6 feet 2 inches tall. Five percent of men clear the bar. Ninety-five percent do not, and most of them still find partners.

The 27 percent figure needs the same care. About 27 percent of women said they ended a relationship at least partly over size, and in most of those cases the complaint was too small, with a few saying too big. That means roughly one in four women has ever treated size as a factor in a breakup. It does not mean one in four relationships ends over size.

Then the hinge of the whole section. Murphy points out that the ideal the women picked was bigger than what most of them had ever seen. That is an ideal-preference artifact: people name a fantasy when you ask for an ideal. The study never asked for a minimum, a cutoff below which she would reject a man. Without the cutoff, the 6.3 number tells you what women dream about, not what they require. His 6-foot-2 analogy: ask women their ideal male height and many say 6 foot 2, then they happily date men who are 5 foot 10. Ideal and minimum are different numbers. Confusing them is the most expensive reading error in the episode.

## Formidability: look like you would win the fight

Murphy's thesis for the male body is one sentence: between two physiques, the one that looks like it would win a fight is usually the one women prefer. He calls it formidability. The cues: strong veiny hands, big arms, broad shoulders, height, and a body that is not too lean.

The measured optimum for body fat, from Zia and colleagues [uncertain: transcript spelling] using DXA scans, landed between 13 and 14.1 percent. That number embarrasses gym culture two ways. Gym men guess the ideal is around 10 percent, leaner than women actually prefer. And the transformation-poll example, Ollie M, split the sexes cleanly: women preferred the before photo, men preferred the after. Men build for male judges.

The dad-bod study sharpens the point. Researchers found that less-lean men got credited with more positive fatherly qualities. The mechanism is a tradeoff between mating effort and paternal investment. A hyper-lean shredded body signals a man oriented toward mating effort: chasing, competing, displaying. A softer strong body signals a man oriented toward paternal investment: staying, providing, parenting. Women read the signal and sort accordingly. The Sasha Baron Cohen example: his breakup body read as a signal of availability, which is a poor family-guy cue if she wants a family guy.

The intrasexual-competition argument explains why gym culture overshoots. Bodybuilding, Murphy says, is largely men competing with men for status inside the group, like women's fashion modeling passing the opposite sex's optimum. Difficulty and status inside the group set the target, not female preference. The group rewards the extreme. Women prefer the middle.

## What men think matters: career, and the 20,000-hour podcast

The 7.1-million-view tweet Murphy cites says men do not care about your career. His read of the research: career is a low-to-moderate priority in both stated and revealed preferences. But successful men still pair with successful women, through assortative mating: richer and more educated men marry richer and more educated women, and the richer the man, the smaller the age gap. Women, he notes, stayed largely immune to the marriage decline since the 1970s when they are richer.

His line for why: a relationship is essentially one long podcast, and you want the most generative conversation, because you will spend something like 20,000 hours together without wanting to get bored. Work the number: 20,000 hours divided by 24 equals about 833 days of continuous talk, or roughly 2.3 years of nonstop conversation. Spread over a 50-year relationship at a few hours a day, it is the bulk of a life. Career does not attract directly. It correlates with the conversation, the competence, and the options that do.

The Applebee's theory, from a friend's joke Murphy adopts: if you can imagine her working at Applebee's, you have a shot. It is a calibration tool against celebrity standards. The men who only swipe on women who could not plausibly work at Applebee's shop in a market where they are not competitive.

## Body count: medians, ideals, and the doubling rate

The numbers first. CDC data, excluding virgins: median body count 4 for women, 6 for men. Murphy's summary line: with over- and under-reporting, "it is both five." On ideals, Stewart-Williams and Thomas found male preference peaks at 1 to 3 past partners, and zero was rated as attractive as six. Both sexes discriminate against virgins, and women discriminate against male virgins more harshly than men do against female virgins.

Then the consequence number. An IFS analysis split people at a body count of 5. The infidelity rate in the above-5 group was double the rate in the below-5 group, with no sex difference, and in some cuts the effect was larger for men. Murphy pairs it with Buss's line from The Evolution of Desire: "the single biggest predictor of extramarital sex is premarital sex." He immediately adds the caveat the episode insists on: the data is correlational. Relationship dissatisfaction also predicts infidelity, but that is close to tautological: unhappy people stray more.

Thomas's recency nuance stops the lazy reading. Ten partners all in undergrad followed by one long relationship is a different personality signal than ten partners in the last month with you as number eleven. Age and context change the meaning: 7 is high for a 19-year-old and normal for a 35-year-old. Murphy's borrowed line, from behavioral geneticist Robert Plowman [uncertain: transcript spelling], is "many different routes up the same mountain": the same number can come from very different lives.

## The costs of winning: jealousy, guarding, and too much optionality

High mate value is mostly win-win: more mates, more desirable mates, more committed partners. Then the costs arrive. The jealousy asymmetry: men are upset more by sexual infidelity, women by emotional infidelity. Murphy's line for the male side: for male mammals, getting cuckolded is true disaster mode. Non-monogamy gives men the bigger benefits, sociosexuality is one of the most replicable sex differences in psychology, and the bigger costs, because the jealousy runs deeper.

Awareness of options taxes the winner. Mate-value discrepancy breeds cost-inflicting strategies: negging, excessive mate guarding, and in extremes domestic violence. Two concrete numbers from the episode: men who are not the primary breadwinner are 50 percent more likely to use erectile dysfunction medication, and people cheat up in attractiveness, which makes rarer partners protective against being cheated on. The DIY analysis on tall men and cheating found no association, against Murphy's own expectation, and he reports the miss honestly.

Speed dating delivers the episode's most hopeful number-free finding: people end up attracted to the best-looking person who reciprocates, not to someone in their "league." Leagues describe the market. Reciprocity clears it.

## Pairing paradoxes: hyperandry, divorce, and the long view

The IFS analysis of 1.4 million people found the hyperandry pattern: the bottom 40 percent of men marry up economically, and the top 20 percent of women marry down. European data adds that status-reversed marriages, where she outranks him, show worse outcomes including domestic violence. Cultures adapt slowly, strategically, and genetically. The Paliyan of South India, where women produce about 70 percent of the food, show a culture adapted to female provision.

Against the gloom, the divorce numbers surprise. Divorce rates are the lowest since 1970 among people who do marry, and Gen Z is on track for 1950s-like divorce rates. The crisis story about marriage is mostly a story about fewer people marrying, not about marriages failing more.

## How to imbibe this

This week, practice the master skill: separate ideals from cutoffs. First, write down three attraction numbers you believe, a height, a body-fat percent, a body count. For each, label it ideal or cutoff, and write where you learned it. If you cannot name the source, mark it [uncertain] and stop repeating it. Second, audit your gym training against the 13 to 14.1 percent optimum: are you training for female preference or for the approval of the men in your gym? Third, apply the 20,000-hour test to your current dating criteria: rank your top five partner criteria by how much they matter across 20,000 hours of conversation, and notice what drops out. Fourth, catch the virgin/ideal confusion in yourself: if you hold a body-count standard, check whether your own number clears it.

## Memory aids

Mnemonic: **I-C-F-B-J.** Ideal, Cutoff, Formidability, Body count, Jealousy. The five stations of the episode, in order.

Never-confuse pairs:
- **Ideal vs cutoff.** 6.3 inches is an ideal. The cutoff was never measured. Never pay a price for an ideal.
- **Mating effort vs paternal investment.** Shredded signals chasing. Strong-but-soft signals staying. Know which game your signals serve.
- **Correlation vs cause.** Body count above 5 doubles infidelity rate in the data. It does not prove the count caused the cheating. Buss's predictor line is still correlational.
- **League vs reciprocity.** Speed dating says she picks the best-looking man who likes her back. The league never enters the room.

Trap cards:
- **The 6.3 trap.** Quoting the ideal as a requirement. Five percent of men clear it. The species continues.
- **The gym-mirror trap.** Training to 10 percent body fat for an audience of men, then wondering why women preferred the before photo.
- **The tall-cheater trap.** Murphy expected tall men to cheat more. His own DIY analysis found nothing. Report the miss, not the theory.
- **The divorce trap.** Falling marriage rates are not rising divorce rates. Among those who marry, divorce is at a 50-year low.

## Q&A

**Q1. Explain like I am new: what is an ideal-preference artifact?**
When researchers ask "what is your ideal," people describe a fantasy, not a shopping list. In the 3D-model study, women picked a size bigger than most had ever seen. That is the artifact: the answer describes imagination, not experience. It becomes dangerous when men read the fantasy as a minimum requirement and despair over a bar nobody actually enforces. Always ask: did the study measure what people want, or what they would reject?

**Q2. Why does the episode say bodybuilding overshoots female preference?**
Because the judges are men. Bodybuilding is intrasexual competition: men competing for status inside a male group, where difficulty and extremity earn rank. The measured female optimum is 13 to 14.1 percent body fat, while gym culture guesses 10. The Ollie M poll is the clean demo: women picked the before photo, men picked the after. When the audience for your body is other men, say so honestly, and stop billing it as attraction strategy.

**Q3. A friend cites the "double infidelity rate above 5" as proof he should reject any woman with a count above 5. What are the three errors?**
First, correlation is not cause: the IFS split shows association, and dissatisfaction predicts too. Second, recency and context: Thomas's nuance says 10 in college plus years of loyalty differs from 10 last month. Third, symmetry: the doubling has no sex difference and sometimes runs larger in men, so the rule, applied honestly, would reject him too at many counts. The number informs. It does not decide.

**Q4. Applied: you are 5 foot 9, 17 percent body fat, all lifts for male approval. Rebuild the plan with this chapter.**
Stop cutting toward 10 percent. The measured optimum is 13 to 14.1, so you are closer than gym culture says. Shift training toward the formidability cues women actually score: shoulders, arms, hands, posture, plus the height you cannot change. Keep strength, lose the shred-or-die framing. Then run the 20,000-hour audit on your dating profile: lead with conversation-generating specifics, not gym photos, because the relationship is a podcast, not a weigh-in.

**Q5. What is the "problems of too rich" argument, in one paragraph?**
Winning the mating game creates new ways to lose. Optionality distress: knowing your options makes commitment feel like a cost. Mate-value discrepancy: when he outranks her clearly, some men switch to cost-inflicting strategies like negging and heavy guarding. Status reversal cuts the other way: men who are not the primary breadwinner are 50 percent more likely to use ED medication, and status-reversed marriages show worse outcomes in European data. High value raises the ceiling and the floor falls out faster.

**Q6. How does the episode explain women pairing with successful men if career is low priority?**
Through assortative mating, not direct attraction to the career. Richer and more educated men marry richer and more educated women because they meet in the same schools, jobs, and circles, and because the traits that build careers, like intelligence and drive, also build the 20,000-hour conversation. The career is a proxy and a meeting place. Nobody falls in love with a job title.

**Q7. What does speed dating prove about "leagues," and why does it matter?**
It proves the league is mostly a story men tell before the event. In speed dating, people become attracted to the best-looking person who reciprocates their interest, regardless of league math. Reciprocity is the market-clearing mechanism: mutual interest beats rank. It matters because league thinking keeps men from approaching at all, which guarantees the zero the league predicted.

## Skills you can now use

- **Research hygiene.** For any attraction claim, ask: ideal or cutoff, who was asked, what was actually measured. Refuse to act on ideals priced as requirements.
- **Body audit.** Compare your body-fat and training goals against the 13 to 14.1 percent optimum and the formidability cues, and name whose approval you are actually training for.
- **Number translation.** Convert study figures into plain frequencies: 95th percentile means 19 of 20 men miss it. Double the rate means 2R, not certain doom.
- **History reading.** Read a body count with Thomas's nuance: recency, age, and context before the number.
- **Cost accounting.** When your mate value rises, name the new costs out loud: guarding urges, optionality noise, status-friction, so they stay visible instead of acting through you.
