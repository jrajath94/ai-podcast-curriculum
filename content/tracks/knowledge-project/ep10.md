## Why this episode matters

Howard Marks co-founded Oaktree Capital Management and writes the memos Warren Buffett calls the first thing he opens. In the last 15 weeks of 2008, while the financial world appeared to end, Marks's firm invested an average of 650 million dollars a week, about 10 billion dollars total. This episode explains how he could act when almost everyone else froze, and builds his full framework: risk as the probability of bad outcomes, cycles as excess and correction, and second-level thinking as the price of outperformance. It closes the track because it is the final exam for every tool: models, principles, and procedures all face the market's ultimate test, acting under uncertainty.

## 2008: investing into the abyss

### Excesses and correction

Crises are complicated, but their shape is simple: excesses build, then get corrected, painfully. The financial crisis grew from a willing suspension of disbelief about mortgages and mortgage-backed securities, held too heavily and too riskily by essential institutions. Bear Stearns, Merrill Lynch, Wachovia, and Washington Mutual disappeared or were rescued. Lehman Brothers went bankrupt on September 15, 2008.

After Lehman, investor psychology completed its swing. Marks's line: in the real world things fluctuate between pretty good and not so hot, but investors swing from "perfect" to "no chance of survival." People spoke of the end of the financial world. It looked, he says, like the movie "The China Syndrome": a vicious circle drilling straight through the earth.

### The two-question analysis

His analysis was short, because the key question was unanalyzable. Question one: does the financial system melt down? This could not be proved or disproved, it was not subject to intellectualization. His stance: it is hard to predict the meltdown, anything you do to prepare for it would be a disaster under any other circumstances, and most of the time the financial world does not end.

Question two: do we invest or not? The logic was a forced move. If they invested and the world melted down, nothing they did mattered. If they did not invest and it did not melt down, they abdicated the responsibility clients hired them for. A crisis that does not end in meltdown is the best possible environment for investing. So they had no choice: invest.

For the last 15 weeks of 2008 they put in an average of 650 million dollars a week, roughly 10 billion total (650 × 15 = 9.75 billion). Sometimes they felt they moved too fast, sometimes too slow. The financial world did not melt down.

### Why he could act

Shane's real question: many saw the opportunity, almost nobody acted. Marks gives four reasons.

First, rehearsal. He and his partner Bruce Karsh, who runs the distressed-debt funds where most of this activity sits, lived through the severe downturns of 1991 and 2002. At the crisis he had 40 years of experience. Intellect, aided by dispassionate observation of experience, can eventually overcome emotion.

Second, temperament. They may simply be less emotional than most.

Third, the activity itself. Distressed-debt investing is inherently contrarian: buying companies that are bankrupt or heading there. After 20 years of it, succumbing to the normal view of distressed companies was not an option.

Fourth, the partnership. Thirty-one years together, and neither has ever said "you did a stupid thing" to the other. You cannot bat 1.000, baseball's greatest hit .330 to .400, and Marks hopes for 60 to 70 percent. An organization that second-guesses mistakes makes people mistake-averse. Theirs does not. That sets the tone: no finger-pointing, which is what lets people act when action looks insane.

On hiring for this: no psychological tests, just conversation, but with an acknowledged priority. The first step with a personnel problem: admit it matters. They select for unemotional, non-egotistical, low-testosterone people and keep the organization mellow.

### Emotions buy high and sell low

The mechanism they overcame: emotions conspire to make people do the wrong thing, probably rooted in fight-or-flight wiring. Good times bring good earnings, positive media, rising prices, enthusiasm, it becomes very hard not to buy. So people buy more the higher prices go, the opposite of every other market in life. Then the top, the turn, the cascade: losses, scare stories, depression. Near the bottom: "get me out, I am terrified." Emotion sells at the bottom just as it bought at the top.

His Feynman quote: physics would be much harder if electrons had feelings. And his ontological point: there is no such thing as "the market." There is only a group of people who implement their views by transacting. People have feelings, the building on Wall Street is just a signifier.

## Risk

### Not volatility

The academic definition, born at the University of Chicago in the early 1960s (Marks arrived in 1967, among the first classes taught it): risk is volatility of prices or returns. His objection: volatility was chosen because it is measurable. You can observe past volatility and extrapolate. There is no observable historical number for the probability of bad outcomes, so the formulas used what existed.

His definition: risk is the probability of bad outcomes. His 2015 memo "Risk Revisited Again" refined it further. For most investors the bad outcome is loss, but there are at least two risks worth naming: the probability of loss, and the probability of gains you miss. People wired as they are care more about the first, a professional investor gets fired for losing money, not for missing upside. But both are bad outcomes.

Peter Bernstein's formulation, which Marks quotes: risk exists because the future is a range of possibilities. Elroy Dimson's one-liner: "risk means more things can happen than will happen." Managing risk therefore requires a view of the probability distribution: what is most likely, least likely, and in between.

Two cautions. First, starting from the wrong distribution guarantees failure. Second, even the exactly right distribution does not tell you what will happen. His craps-table image: two dice, 36 possible outcomes, six ways to make 7, the most likely result. Roll a thousand times and 7 comes up most. Roll once and you have no idea. That gap between probability and outcome is where risk lives.

And expected value is not enough. The highest-expected-value action can include unsurvivable outcomes. His example: cliff diving might maximize expected elation, but he dislikes the bad outcomes, so he does not dive. Similarly a conservative investor avoids venture capital, where total loss sits inside the distribution. Decisions need the distribution plus your tolerances.

### Decision versus outcome

From Wharton, 55 years earlier, the first lesson that stuck: you cannot tell the quality of a decision from the outcome. Backgammon with his friend Bruce Newberg: you need some crazy number to win and you get it. That does not make you a good player, it makes you lucky. Improbable things happen all the time, probable things fail to happen all the time. If probable things always happened, there would be no risk.

The organizational consequence: do not criticize people every time something goes wrong. A good decision can look exactly like a bad decision in the short run. Judge the process, not the outcome.

Oaktree's motto encodes the priority: "If we avoid the losers, the winners take care of themselves." It fit high-yield bonds bought at par perfectly: the best outcome is interest plus principal back, with many worse outcomes, so securing against the worst was the whole game. In aspirational strategies like distressed debt (16 to 17 percent a year for 30 years, no debt), avoiding losers is not sufficient, you must find winners. But they kept the motto because it keeps risk control front of mind.

## Cycles

### Excess and correction

His summary of the economic cycle, sharpened since the book printed: cycles happen because people, not electrons, commit excesses to the upside out of enthusiasm, which then must be corrected, and the corrections overshoot to the downside.

The factory parable: coming out of recession, every company's management sees recovery demand ahead. All build factories, hire a thousand workers, and stock inventories simultaneously. That makes an above-average year. But collectively they built more capacity than the demand gain justifies, so next year nobody builds: a below-average year. Success sows the seeds of its own reversal.

On the then-current expansion: the tenth year of recovery, never exceeded in history, but also the slowest since World War II. Every development has two sides. The negative side of slow is slow. The positive side: no excesses accumulated, so no correction was due. His guess: the recovery would set the longevity record precisely because of the slowness.

Then the December tax bill: corporate rates cut from 35 to 21 (not to 25 as expected), highly stimulative for a healthy economy. His image: doctors do not give adrenaline to healthy patients, they give it to heart-attack victims. Over-stimulation would breed excesses requiring correction, probably via higher interest rates than otherwise needed, raising recession risk.

### The Fed and the Greenspan put

The Fed's number-one job for a century: control inflation, keep the economy growing without overheating. The later-added second mandate, supporting employment, opposes the first: growth brings jobs, too much growth brings inflation. Managing the opposition is inherently hard.

His criticism: the Fed, especially under Greenspan, was so accommodating that it created the "Greenspan put," the perception that whenever trouble appears, the Fed injects liquidity and cuts rates. If everyone believes problems will always be prevented, nobody plans for tough times, and planning biases entirely to the upside. When tough times arrive anyway, by definition nobody is ready.

### Governments redistribute, they do not make

Outside the Fed, government's economic role is redistribution: collect money, spend it. It makes nothing. Tax policy (how much from rich, poor, dividends, salaries) and spending policy (welfare, guaranteed jobs, basic income) are the entire game, and the main grist of politics.

On "fair share": he bridles. It is a religious or philosophical statement, not an economic one. There is no such thing as a fair share, the only question is who defines it, and in practice "fair share" always means "more than now." Meanwhile the upper income distribution already pays almost all the taxes.

His May 2016 memo "Economic Reality" versus August 2016's "Political Reality": economics is the science of choices under scarcity ($10 buys ten $1 hamburgers). Politics promises the hamburgers free. Examples of economic reality: the biggest New Jersey taxpayer moving away, Venezuela's toilet-paper price controls producing no toilet paper, France's 75 percent top rate under Hollande driving the wealthy out, the SALT deduction change pushing people toward Florida, Texas, and Nevada (he meets New Yorkers who live in Florida and visit 181 days a year, the maximum). Governments cannot undo by fiat what economic reality requires. Nations cheat on treaties the way people do, the cheaters get away with it for a while.

### Globalization's arithmetic

The sheep-and-shoes parable: his country raises sheep well, yours makes shoes well. Trading hides for finished shoes yields 100 pairs a year. A politician walls off trade, now each does both badly: 40 pairs each, 80 total. Specialization's gains are real, and blocking them shrinks the pie.

The trade-deficit fallacy: getting a haircut for $20 means you run a "trade deficit" with the barber. He gets $20, you get nothing he buys. Nobody calls that losing. An $800 billion deficit with China means Americans bought $800 billion more in Chinese goods than China bought in American goods, because Americans preferred the goods: some combination of better and cheaper. Tariffs do not change who pays: the Chinese do not pay them, American consumers do, in higher Walmart prices.

The honest accounting he demands: tariffs might bring back some of the 3 million jobs lost to China over 15 years. The price: 100 million Americans paying more for everyday goods. Good or bad? Washington decides, but it should decide with both columns visible. Steel tariffs even disadvantage American motorcycle makers using foreign steel against rivals using domestic steel, and against foreign manufacturers entirely. Tariffs are attempts to regulate by rule against economic reality, and reality charges interest.

His honest caveat: globalization maximizes the world product but creates individual losers. His stepmother thought everyone deserved a Las Vegas vacation and a color TV, his reply was the casino that nobody can lose at, which would close in an hour. Free enterprise requires losers. The political question is what you do for them.

## Luck, work, and machines

### Getting lucky

His January 2014 memo "Getting Lucky," the most-responded-to memo he ever wrote, lists about a dozen ways luck favored him. Against the Silicon Valley line that "success is never accidental, you make your own luck": luck is real, important, and inherently unfair. Your IQ: what did you do to deserve the brain you were born with? Nothing. That is luck, not merit.

His response to luck is gratitude, not guilt. Cicero: "the thankful heart is the greatest of all the virtues, and the parent of all the others." The lucky should acknowledge their luck, revel in it, and share its fruits. He admits the illogic cheerfully: past luck probably does not predict future luck, but feeling lucky keeps him positive, and he will take the trade.

Equal opportunity, not equal outcome, is his standard: work ethic and luck both matter, and birth advantages are real.

### Universal basic income

Work gives far more than income: self-satisfaction, the sense of producing your own subsistence, colleagues, team effort, accomplishment. Galbraith's rant: calling both the CEO's day and the road worker's day "work" stretches the word past sense, but both get these goods. UBI would feed physical needs without providing them. The Clinton-era welfare reduction, he believes, was right: bad incentives make bad habits.

The deeper problem is motivation as jobs vanish. His numbers: US manufacturing output roughly doubled in 40 years while employment fell from about 19 million to 12 million. Output per worker tripled. Banning productivity gains would be silly, someone else would take them. The arithmetic he offers: roughly 24 million jobs lost to productivity versus 3 million to China over 40 years. The problem is not China.

Self-driving cars extend it: not just drivers (the largest job category), but body shops, paint shops, steel, insurance adjusters. Cars sit unused most of the day, full utilization needs a fraction of the fleet. The factory-of-the-future joke: one man and one dog, the dog keeps the man from touching the machinery, the man feeds the dog. Amazon Go: ten bookstore workers become one greeter. New labor-intensive industries absorbed the farm workers once, he cannot picture the equivalent now. He calls himself an optimist but not a stupid one, and on this he is not optimistic. A solution would need bipartisan radicalism, Washington cannot agree on anything. He supports the No Labels bipartisanship effort, most call it quixotic.

## Second-level thinking

### Different and better

Investing is incredibly easy to be average at: buy the index fund, guarantee never falling short, accept never exceeding. Superior results require the opposite of average thinking. His chain: think the same as everyone → act the same → perform the same → average by definition. To beat the average you must diverge from the herd.

The complication is the efficient market hypothesis (Chicago, 1964): the consensus usually incorporates available information well, so most of the time the crowd is about as right as anyone can be. Idiosyncratic thinking is usually wrong. Therefore second-level thinking must be different and better, and by definition few can do it.

His "Most Important Thing" has 21 chapters, each naming a different "most important thing," because there is no single one, they are bricks in a wall. The first brick is second-level thinking. The textbook example: the first-level thinker says "this is a great company, buy the stock." The second-level thinker says "it is a great company, but less great than everyone thinks, the optimism is already in the price, when truth comes out the price falls, sell." Everything important in investing is counterintuitive, everything obvious is wrong.

### Dare to look wrong

His 2006 memo "Dare to Be Great," revised in 2014: everybody dares to be great, the questions are whether you dare to be different, dare to be wrong, and dare to look wrong. Even positions that will be right in the long run can look wrong in the short run. His own money was made in assets that were unpopular when he arrived: high-yield bonds in 1978. Telling the straight investing world "buy this because nobody else is" gets "are you crazy?" in reply, plus the career fear: deviating and erring makes you look stupid.

Variant perception is the working method. His son, training as a young investor, would pitch Ford on a great new Mustang. The pedagogical reply, always: "Who does not know that?" If everyone knows, the expectation is in the price, the event helps Ford but not Ford's investors. Outperformance comes from seeing what others do not, which usually means profiting from their mistakes: over-excitement pricing too high, despair pricing too low.

The information diet follows: the base layer is what will happen (the restructuring, the building's optimization). The second layer is what others think, why they think it, where they diverge from you, and why you are right and they are wrong. His own reading is deliberately non-purposeful now: he is not making investment decisions, he works to get smarter and keep his thinking challenged. His definition of risk changed between memos, refinement never stops.

## Parenting and the closing paragraph

### Money with children

Talk about money early, as responsibility rather than worship. Never insulate children from it. His rules: give them the feeling of finiteness (his daughter got a credit card with bills, unlike her friend who "does not get bills"), never let the richest kid set the family's standard ("in our family we do not spend that way" builds character), let children make non-lethal choices (his daughter picked her high school against their preference, she did fine, and the trust compounded), let them make small mistakes early, so the first mistake at 5 teaches instead of the first at 35 crushing.

The moral core: respect others, care for others, team effort over getting ahead, the Golden Rule. Erik Erikson's stages: late in life, how you are thought of and how you think of yourself is what remains, and by then it is too late to change it.

### Being wrong is the franchise

Shane reads his favorite paragraph, from the book's end, quoting Peter Bernstein: the future is not ours to know, but it helps to know that being wrong is inevitable and normal, not a tragedy, not a failing of reasoning, not even bad luck in most cases. Being wrong comes with the franchise of any activity whose outcome depends on an unknown future. No baseball player expects to bat 1.000, investors should not either. Do not get down on yourself for a mistake with a good process, and do not castigate staff or children for one.

## Questions and answers

**Q1: Explain this episode to me like I am new. What is Marks's message?**
Risk is the chance of bad outcomes, not the wiggle of prices. Economies and markets move in cycles because people get enthusiastic, overdo it, and then overcorrect. You cannot predict the cycle's turns, but you can read where you are in it by measuring the excess. Outperforming requires thinking differently from the crowd and being right, which means enduring looking wrong. And through it all, judge decisions by their process, because outcomes belong partly to luck.

**Q2: Work the 2008 numbers with me.**
Fifteen weeks, 650 million dollars a week: 650 × 15 = 9,750 million, about 10 billion. The logic was not a prediction that things would recover. It was a decision tree: meltdown makes all choices irrelevant, survival makes investing the duty. The number that matters is not the 10 billion. It is the willingness to act on the logic while everyone else's emotions said get out.

**Q3: What is the difference between risk and uncertainty here?**
Risk, in his working sense, is a probability distribution you can roughly sketch: most likely, least likely, in between. Uncertainty is deeper: sometimes you do not even know what is in the range. Volatility is neither, it is just the measurable shadow of past wiggles. The practical move: form the distribution as best you can, remember you may have the wrong distribution, and never take a bet whose bad tail you cannot survive.

**Q4: Applied: where are we in the cycle right now?**
His method, not a forecast: look for excesses. Are investors credulous? Is debt building? Is psychology at "perfect" or "no chance of survival"? Remember every development has two sides: a long slow expansion has the negative of slowness and the positive of no accumulated excess. Do not ask when it ends. Ask what would have to correct, and whether you are positioned for the correction overshooting.

**Q5: What is second-level thinking in one exercise?**
Take any consensus view you hold. Write the first-level version ("good company, buy"). Now write what is already in the price: what does everyone expect? Then write the variant perception: what do you see that they do not, and what mistake are they making? If you cannot write the third paragraph, you are a first-level thinker on this question. Do not bet as if you were second-level.

**Q6: How do the 24-million versus 3-million jobs change the automation debate?**
They relocate it. If 24 million jobs went to productivity and 3 million to China over 40 years, tariffs aim at the smaller cause. The question becomes: what do people whose asset is a strong back do when machines do it better? His honest answer is that he does not know, and that "we faced this before" is not a plan. Use the numbers to argue about the real cause, not the convenient one.

**Q7: I am afraid to look wrong. What would Marks say?**
That the fear is the price of admission, and most people will not pay it. His three dares: be different, be wrong, look wrong. Note the order: different is the strategy, wrong is the risk, looking wrong is the emotional cost. Build the partnership and the culture that never says "you did a stupid thing," because without it you will optimize for never looking wrong, which guarantees average.

**Q8: How does this episode close the track's arc?**
The arc ran: models → rationality → great minds → how minds work → debugging self-delusion → sustained focus. Marks is the final exam. Second-level thinking is rationality under social pressure. The cycle framework is systems thinking with human emotions inside. "Being wrong is the franchise" is ep04's Stoicism translated to markets. And the 2008 decision is the whole curriculum in one act: read the situation, separate what you can know from what you cannot, and act.

## Memory aids

Mnemonic: **R-C-S**: Risk (probability of bad outcomes), Cycles (excess then correction), Second-level thinking (different and better).

Never-confuse pairs:
- Risk versus volatility: probability of bad outcomes versus measurable price wiggles. One matters, the other is just available.
- Probability versus outcome: the distribution versus the single roll. Even the true distribution does not say what happens next.
- Economic reality versus political reality: scarcity and choices versus promises without cost. One governs, the other campaigns.
- First-level versus second-level: what is good versus what is already priced. Good news can be a sell signal.

Trap cards:
- Trap: "Good things happened, so we will make money." Reality: it depends on expectations. $1.75 on expected $2.00 sinks the stock.
- Trap: "The consensus is usually right, so follow it." Reality: the consensus is usually right, which is why following it guarantees average.
- Trap: "Tariffs punish the foreign producer." Reality: American consumers pay. Check both columns.
- Trap: "A bad outcome means a bad decision." Reality: backgammon. Judge the process, luck owns the rest.

## How to imbibe this

1. The expectations check. For your next piece of good news (yours or the market's), write what was expected first. Good news against high expectations is bad news. Observable change: fewer surprises.
2. The cycle position. Quarterly, score the excesses: credulity, debt, psychology (perfect to no-chance). Write where you think we are. Observable change: positioning becomes deliberate.
3. The variant perception. For each consensus view you act on, write the paragraph: what do I see that they miss? No paragraph, no bet. Observable change: fewer crowded trades.
4. The three dares. Name one decision where you will be different, possibly wrong, and look wrong short-term. Tell your partner. Observable change: courage becomes scheduled.
5. The process audit. After each outcome, good or bad, grade the decision process separately from the result. Observable change: luck stops corrupting your learning.
6. The finiteness lesson. Give one financial responsibility with real bills to someone you mentor. Observable change: they learn what no lecture teaches.

## Think it yourself

The track's final set. Comprehensive.

**Drill 1: the 2008 memo.** Write the two-question memo as if you were Marks in September 2008: the unanalyzable question, the forced move, the numbers. Then write the equivalent memo for today's biggest fear in your world. Find your forced move.

**Drill 2: cycle mapping.** Pick three cycles (economic, psychological, industry-specific). For each: name the current excess or its absence, the correction mechanism, and the overshoot risk. Date your map. Revisit in six months and grade yourself.

**Drill 3: risk distribution.** For your biggest financial decision, sketch the probability distribution: most likely, least likely, in between. Mark the unsurvivable tail. If the expected value is attractive but the tail is unsurvivable, write why you decline. This is the cliff-diving discipline.

**Drill 4: second-level audit.** List five positions or opinions you hold because "everyone knows." For each, write who does not know it (the Mustang test). Kill the ones where everyone knows. For survivors, write the variant perception.

**Drill 5: the jobs arithmetic.** Take the 24M vs 3M structure and apply it to a debate in your field: find the convenient cause and the real cause, with numbers. Write the one-paragraph correction you would publish.

**Drill 6: track synthesis.** Write one page: the single idea from each of the 10 episodes you will actually use, and the procedure that installs it. This is your personal curriculum. If an episode contributed nothing usable, write why honestly.

## Skills you can now use

- Define risk as probability of bad outcomes and sketch the distribution.
- Read cycle position by measuring excess rather than predicting turns.
- Think at the second level: price in expectations, find variant perception.
- Dare to be different, wrong, and look wrong on a schedule.
- Separate decision quality from outcome quality in review.
- Argue policy with both columns visible (tariffs, jobs, prices).
- Teach finiteness and decision-making to the next generation.
