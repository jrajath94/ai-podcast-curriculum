## ep01 — Naval Ravikant (Reading, Decisions, Habits, Happiness)

**Q1 [NUMERICAL]. Naval's investing funnel is 10,000 companies seen, 500 funded with a shot, 5 winners doubled down on. Compute the survival rate at each cut and the share of seen companies that become doubled-down winners.**

Answer: First cut: 500 / 10,000 = 5 percent survive. Second cut: 5 / 500 = 1 percent survive. Overall: 5 / 10,000 = 0.05 percent of seen companies become doubled-down winners. This is the system-over-goals design: the outcome is likely across many universes because the structure does the work, not one brilliant pick.
Citations: ep01:97 (10,000 seen, 500 funded, 5 doubled), ep01:104 (funnel table).

**Q2 [EXPLAIN, like new]. What is a memory-prediction machine, and why does Naval say principles beat specific past events for predicting the future?**

Answer: A memory-prediction machine takes memories of what worked in the past and projects them onto the future. The lousy version is "X happened before, so X will happen again" — it keys on specific circumstances, which do not transfer. The better version extracts principles, reusable mental models from evolution, game theory, and Charlie Munger, that transfer across circumstances. Models fire by pattern match, not by checklist.
Citations: ep01:75 (definition), ep01:77-80 (two versions of prediction).

**Q3 [APPLIED]. You are three months into a business book you hate. Per the chapter, what exactly do you do?**

Answer: Drop it today. Skim for the interesting part, take the idea, and stop — Naval estimates he skips two thirds of most books, because most nonfiction books carry one good idea wrapped in hundreds of pages of examples. The social rule "finish what you start" is a tax on curiosity and kills the habit. The only hard stop: if the author states something you know is false (his test: "if the author says thermodynamics is not true"), close the book, because you can no longer separate truth from fiction inside it.
Citations: ep01:45 (skims two thirds, thermodynamics test), ep01:59 (10 percent completion rate, habit over completion).

## ep02 — Chamath Palihapitiya (Truth, Freedom, First Principles)

**Q4 [NUMERICAL]. Using Chamath's freedom drill: monthly spending S = 5,000 dollars. Compute annual spending A and the rough freedom fund F with the 4 percent rule. Show the arithmetic.**

Answer: A = 5,000 x 12 = 60,000 dollars per year. F = 25 x A = 25 x 60,000 = 1,500,000 dollars. So someone spending 5,000 dollars a month has a rough freedom number of 1.5 million dollars. This makes the path to freedom concrete and copyable.
Citation: ep02:233 (freedom math drill, example arithmetic).

**Q5 [EXPLAIN, like new]. What are the two paths, and what goes wrong when you confuse them?**

Answer: The path to freedom is financial: money buys freedom, and the path is teachable and copyable (work, study, join a community, accumulate capital over 5 to 20 years). The path to happiness is internal and personal: no one can copy it, it is constant trial and error. Confuse them and you get destruction in both directions: racing for happiness with money brings debt, drugs, and losing everything, while trying to meditate your way to financial security never works. Work both paths simultaneously, with different tools.
Citations: ep02:77 (the two paths section), ep02:85-95 (freedom teachable, happiness personal, path confusion).

**Q6 [APPLIED]. You feel like an impostor at work. Give Chamath's protocol from the chapter.**

Answer: Four steps. (1) Normalize it: he feels impostor syndrome constantly, and it sharpens as he climbs. (2) Talk: to his partner, trusted friends, and two therapists he sees weekly — talking is dental floss for the mind, not 100 percent, but it prevents 90 to 95 percent of the buildup. (3) Separate feeling from facts: the feeling says you have not earned the seat, the work record says otherwise. (4) Keep "oh wow" moments in your life so the job does not become your identity.
Citations: ep02:45-53 (therapists, plaque analogy, 90 to 95 percent), ep02:185 (Q3 four-step protocol).

## ep03 — Bill Gurley (Systems Thinking, Bedrock, the Edge)

**Q7 [NUMERICAL]. Per the chapter's rails table, compute the cost of moving 10,000 dollars by (a) US same-day wire, (b) US card payment at 2.5 percent, (c) USDC stablecoin. Compare.**

Answer: (a) 25 dollars plus a page of forms. (b) 2.5 percent x 10,000 = 250 dollars. (c) Pennies, effectively about zero. On a 10,000-dollar transfer the card rail costs 10 times the wire (250 / 25) and on the order of 25,000 times the stablecoin cost. This is why Gurley says Visa and Mastercard, at roughly 60 percent operating margins as a bank-created duopoly, will be heavily threatened.
Citations: ep03:151-154 (rails table: wire $25, card 2 to 3 percent, USDC seconds for pennies), ep03:146 (60 percent operating margins).

**Q8 [EXPLAIN, like new]. What are first, second, and third-derivative effects? Use the dating-site story.**

Answer: The first derivative is the direct result of your change. A second-derivative effect is a consequence of the consequence. The dating site made profiles longer: engagement rose (first derivative, the measured metric), so the change shipped. Months later they discovered it was negative for conversion (second derivative: people who knew more at that level converted less). The discipline: be conscious of consequences beyond the first, never get deterministic about a single metric, and wait long enough to see the second derivative before declaring victory.
Citations: ep03:35 (second-derivative definition), ep03:33-40 (dating-site example).

**Q9 [APPLIED]. Design the one-month "know the bedrock" plan exactly as the chapter prescribes.**

Answer: Pick your field's equivalent of Lasseter's cartoons: list the ten classic works, deals, or products everyone references but few studied. Study one per week — the original, not the summary. Write a one-page note on each: what it did, why it worked, what it teaches now. Then use one in your next interview or meeting and watch the room change. Gurley's test: if week two feels tedious, you are probably in the wrong lane.
Citations: ep03:63 (Lasseter's 10-course cartoon history lesson), ep03:219 (the one-month plan), ep03:67-69 (the tedium test).

## ep04 — William Irvine (Stoicism as a User Manual)

**Q10 [NUMERICAL]. Irvine dates the savanna wiring behind the hedonic treadmill to roughly 200,000 to 70,000 years ago. How long is that window? What does the length imply about how deep the wiring runs?**

Answer: 200,000 - 70,000 = 130,000 years. The wiring — ancestors who were easily satisfied dropped out of the gene pool, the dissatisfied survivors reproduced — ran for 130 millennia, which is why wanting more feels automatic and why the treadmill needs deliberate technique (negative visualization, trichotomy, five-second rule) to counter it.
Citation: ep04:59 (savanna dates).

**Q11 [EXPLAIN, like new]. Walk a beginner through negative visualization, step by step, including the safety rule.**

Answer: Step 1: pick something you rely on daily and take for granted — your eyesight is the classic. Step 2: imagine it gone for a flickering moment: eyes glued shut, never to open. Step 3: open your eyes and look — the gratitude rush is the technique working. The safety rule: keep it flickering, never dwell. Dwelling would be a recipe for misery; touch the loss for a second, snap out of it, and go on with life.
Citations: ep04:79-83 (the three steps, flicker-not-dwell rule).

**Q12 [APPLIED]. Someone insulted you. Give the Stoic options in order and the logic behind them.**

Answer: Best: no response. Continue the conversation as if nothing happened. If they repeat it: "yeah, I heard you the first time," and continue. Next: make a joke of it and turn it around ("if that's the worst thing you have to say about me, you do not know me well enough"). The logic: their best shot did not register, which is the ultimate put-down, and it costs you almost nothing. Remember the wiring: you care because on the savanna, accepting an insult meant losing status, which meant less food and fewer mates. The insult is words. Words only.
Citations: ep04:168 (insult techniques, savanna wiring), ep04:205 (Q5 ordered options).

## ep05 — Robert Greene (Power, Research, Live Time)

**Q13 [NUMERICAL]. "The 48 Laws of Power" began as roughly 80 recurring themes and was whittled to 48. How many themes were discarded, and what percentage is that? The human-nature book went 30 to 24 to 18: what was cut in total, and what percentage?**

Answer: 80 - 48 = 32 themes discarded; 32 / 80 = 40 percent cut. Human nature: 30 - 18 = 12 cut in total; 12 / 30 = 40 percent cut. Both books were reduced like a cook reduces a sauce — not too strong, not too weak — and the themes emerged from the material, not from an outline.
Citation: ep05:62 (80 to 48; 30 to 24 to 18).

**Q14 [EXPLAIN, like new]. What is the difference between live time and dead time?**

Answer: You own nothing in life — not stock, not money. The only thing you own is time, say 85 years. Live time is time that is your own: nobody commands how you spend it, you are urgent, a bit edgy, aware of each moment passing. Dead time is time others own: flipping burgers at Burger King for someone else, fog, no energy, no thought of the future. The conversion: the same Burger King shift becomes live time if you bring ownership — read on break, set the plan (one year, quit, save, night school), study the customers. Dead time with a purpose you chose is live time.
Citations: ep05:143 (you own only time, 85 years), ep05:145-149 (dead vs live, the Burger King conversion).

**Q15 [APPLIED]. You are hiring. Give Greene's bullshit-detection protocol in order.**

Answer: First, the record: what has this person actually accomplished? Cut the verbiage, check the outcomes — Machiavelli's "effective truth": ignore what they say, look at their actions. Second, the room: watch how they interact with people, especially those with no power over them — that is where toxicity shows. Third, the nonverbals: constant talking signals insecurity; quiet seriousness and admitting you are not always right mark the non-bullshitter. Fourth, remember the interview is a performance; the interactions between performances are the data.
Citations: ep05:107-112 (detecting bullshit: actions over words, nonverbals), ep05:190 (Q3 four-step protocol).

## ep06 — Jim Collins (Level 5, Flywheel, Luck, Decline)

**Q16 [NUMERICAL]. Recompute the 292-to-1 quiz inputs: Company A grows 25 percent a year with standard deviation +-15 points; Company B grows 45 percent with +-115 points. What range does each actually land in, and why does A win 292 to 1 over 20 years?**

Answer: A: 25 +- 15 lands between 20 and 30 percent every year, never below 20, almost never above 30. B: 45 +- 115 ranges from -200 to +300 percent, above 30 percent two-thirds of the years. A wins 292 to 1 because compounding punishes down years savagely: a -50 percent year needs +100 percent just to recover, while the steady 20 to 30 percent never digs a hole. The lesson is arithmetic, not philosophy: volatility is a tax, and consistency compounds.
Citations: ep06:135 (both companies' parameters), ep06:137 (292 to 1 outcome), ep06:266-267 (the compounding arithmetic).

**Q17 [EXPLAIN, like new]. What is the difference between the flywheel and the Doom loop?**

Answer: The flywheel: good decisions build momentum that compounds — one slow turn, then four, then eight, then the breakthrough "comes out of nowhere." Wooden's UCLA teams won 10 championships in 12 years, but it was a decade-plus of pushing before it looked like a breakthrough. The Doom loop: disappointing results arrive, the company reacts without understanding (new direction, new leader, new fad), gets a burst of false hope like a sugar drink instead of training, then more disappointment. Same setback, opposite response — the difference is grasp of the momentum machine.
Citations: ep06:92 (Wooden, 10 championships in 12 years), ep06:104-108 (flywheel vs Doom loop mechanics).

**Q18 [APPLIED]. You have a big bet you want to make. Give the bullet-or-cannonball decision procedure.**

Answer: Ask three questions. (1) Have you fired a bullet — a small, excellent test? Fire it well, or a miss teaches nothing. (2) Did it calibrate — empirical validation, not theory? Apple's iPod bullet calibrated when iTunes went on Windows and non-Mac users flooded in; in the 2002/2003 10-K it had gotten about three sentences. (3) Is the big bet an extension of a turning flywheel? Three yeses: fire the cannonball. Any no: you hold an uncalibrated cannonball — the move most correlated with stage-2 decline.
Citations: ep06:165 (iPod bullet, three sentences in the 10-K, iTunes-on-Windows calibration), ep06:269-271 (Q5 three-question procedure).

## ep07 — Ray Dalio (Principles, Idea Meritocracy, Pain plus Reflection)

**Q19 [NUMERICAL]. In 1982, US banks had 250 percent of their capital loaned out to the countries Dalio judged unable to repay. For a bank with 10 billion dollars of capital, how many dollars were loaned out? And how broke was Dalio personally after the call?**

Answer: 250 percent x 10 billion = 25 billion dollars loaned out. After his prediction inverted — August 1982 was the exact bottom of the stock market — clients left, money ran out, he let his whole team go, and he was so broke he borrowed 4,000 dollars from his father. That humiliation rebuilt his decision-making around one question: "How do I know I am right?"
Citations: ep07:37 (250 percent of capital, Mexico default), ep07:39 (exact bottom, borrowed 4,000 dollars).

**Q20 [EXPLAIN, like new]. What is believability-weighted voting, and how does the three-doctor example work?**

Answer: An idea meritocracy has three steps: put honest thoughts on the table, engage in thoughtful disagreement, and use fair agreed-upon ways past disagreement. Autocracy fails twice (the boss may be wrong; others do not own the decision) and democracy fails differently (it ignores that some thinking has more merit). So: weight views by believability — literal scores from tests and track records. The example: on a medical question, your own believability is low. Find three highly believable doctors who will disagree with each other, listen to the disagreement, and decide from where their weighted views converge.
Citations: ep07:51 (three steps), ep07:55 (autocratic vs democratic failure, three-doctor method).

**Q21 [APPLIED]. Your team cannot do thoughtful disagreement. What is the smallest start, per the chapter?**

Answer: Dalio's three questions work at any size. In one meeting this week: (1) can everyone put their honest thought on the table? (2) can you have one real back-and-forth about it? (3) if you still disagree, what fair mechanism decides? Write the answers down. You have installed the idea meritocracy's kernel; the tools, scores, and taped meetings are later, optional upgrades. Dalio's rule inside the system: if three people more believable than him think one thing and he thinks another, he switches into learner mode.
Citations: ep07:211 (Q3 three-question kernel), ep07:55-57 (learner mode on believability).

## ep08 — Seth Godin (Practice, Smallest Viable Audience, Emotional Labor)

**Q22 [NUMERICAL]. Godin wrote 7,500 daily blog posts in a row. At 365 days per year, how many years of daily posting is that? Why does the streak matter to him?**

Answer: 7,500 / 365 = 20.5 years, approximately. The streak matters because it is a constructive sunk cost: on a Thursday with nothing to say, the streak's emotional cost gets him over the hump. It is a gift from his former self — deliberately installed, and he should stop if it ever stops giving joy long-term.
Citations: ep08:25 (7,500 daily blog posts in a row), ep08:115 (the streak as constructive sunk cost).

**Q23 [EXPLAIN, like new]. What is the smallest viable audience, and why is "for everybody" a hiding place?**

Answer: The goal is the smallest viable audience, not the biggest possible one. Harry Potter, worth over a billion dollars, is for almost no one: precocious 12-year-olds and adults who remember being one — a tiny group. "It's for everybody" is a hiding place: make it for everyone and you can never fail, because "everyone has not found it yet." Make it for someone specific and you are on the hook: if they hate it, you were wrong. The internet enforces this: it is a micro medium, the best ever for reaching specific people, terrible at reaching everyone.
Citations: ep08:91 (Harry Potter audience), ep08:101 (comparison table: hiding vs on the hook).

**Q24 [APPLIED]. You keep polishing and never ship. What is the chapter's procedure?**

Answer: Write the spec first: what must this do, for whom, to what standard? Deming's definition: quality is one thing — it meets spec. Lexus's original spec was one standard deviation better than Mercedes; perfection was never the target. When the work meets spec, ship — "good enough" is a definition, not a slur. If shipping feels wrong, the spec was wrong: change the spec, not the shipping. Then the Kind of Blue test: Miles Davis recorded one of the best-selling jazz albums ever in four days — if they spent four more days, what would result? Usually nothing worth the days.
Citations: ep08:135-137 (quality meets spec, Lexus), ep08:141 (Kind of Blue test), ep08:198 (Q3 ship procedure).

## ep09 — Daniel Kahneman (Noise, Intuition, Better Judgment)

**Q25 [NUMERICAL]. Kahneman's noise metric is difference divided by the average. Two underwriters price identical cases at 200 and 300 dollars. Compute the noise. How does it compare with what executives expected versus what the study found?**

Answer: Difference = 300 - 200 = 100. Average = (300 + 200) / 2 = 250. Noise = 100 / 250 = 40 percent. Executives expected about 10 percent between two random underwriters; the measured result across about 50 underwriters was about 50 percent — a complete surprise to the company. Rule: where there is judgment, there is noise, and more of it than you think.
Citations: ep09:61-63 (50 underwriters, 10 percent guess, 50 percent result), ep09:72-74 (noise table).

**Q26 [EXPLAIN, like new]. What is the difference between bias and noise?**

Answer: Bias is the average error: everyone shoots left of target. Noise is the scatter around it: shots land everywhere. The 50 underwriters were not systematically too high or too low — they disagreed with each other, expensively, at random. Organizations obsess over bias and ignore noise, but noise is often the bigger and more fixable problem. Fix it with algorithms (better than humans), structured decomposition (score aspects independently), and scale discipline (train people on what the scale means).
Citations: ep09:67 (definitions, underwriter scatter), ep09:76-82 (three noise-reduction procedures).

**Q27 [APPLIED]. Give the premortem procedure exactly, including timing and the honest caveat.**

Answer: Timing: the group is close to a decision but has not made it. Gather the deciders and say: imagine two years pass, we made this decision, and it was a disaster. Everyone writes the story of the disaster in bullet points. Collect the loopholes. For each, ask: what would make the plan safer? Why it works: near a decision, doubt is socially costly; the premortem legitimizes doubt because everyone competes to write the best disaster story. The caveat: it will not prevent big mistakes, but it alerts the group to loopholes and to what would make the plan safer.
Citations: ep09:95 (procedure), ep09:97 (legitimizing doubt, honest caveat), ep09:193-194 (Q3 applied version).

## ep10 — Howard Marks (Risk, Cycles, Second-Level Thinking)

**Q28 [NUMERICAL]. Oaktree invested an average of 650 million dollars a week for the last 15 weeks of 2008. Compute the total and confirm the chapter's figure.**

Answer: 650 x 15 = 9,750 million = 9.75 billion dollars, which the chapter rounds to "roughly 10 billion total." The logic was not a prediction of recovery: if the world melted down, nothing they did mattered; if it did not, not investing meant abdicating the responsibility clients hired them for. A crisis that does not end in meltdown is the best possible environment for investing.
Citations: ep10:41 (650 million x 15 = 9.75 billion), ep10:37-39 (the two-question forced move).

**Q29 [EXPLAIN, like new]. Why does Marks reject volatility as the definition of risk, and what is his definition?**

Answer: Academics defined risk as volatility of prices or returns because volatility is measurable — you can observe past wiggles and extrapolate. There is no observable historical number for the probability of bad outcomes, so the formulas used what existed. Marks's definition: risk is the probability of bad outcomes — at least two: the probability of loss, and the probability of gains you miss. Volatility misses unsurvivable tails by construction. His craps-table image: two dice, 36 outcomes, 7 the most likely over 1,000 rolls — but roll once and you have no idea. That gap between probability and outcome is where risk lives.
Citations: ep10:73 (volatility chosen because measurable), ep10:75 (probability of bad outcomes, two risks), ep10:79-81 (craps table, cliff-diving unsurvivable tails).

**Q30 [APPLIED]. Where are we in the cycle right now? Give the chapter's method, not a forecast.**

Answer: Do not ask when it ends. Measure excesses: are investors credulous? Is debt building? Is psychology at "perfect" or "no chance of survival"? Remember every development has two sides — a long, slow expansion has the negative of slowness and the positive of no accumulated excesses. Then ask what would have to correct, and whether you are positioned for the correction overshooting to the downside. Cycles happen because people, not electrons, commit excesses out of enthusiasm, which must be corrected, and corrections overshoot.
Citations: ep10:209 (Q4 method), ep10:103-105 (excess, correction, overshoot mechanics).
