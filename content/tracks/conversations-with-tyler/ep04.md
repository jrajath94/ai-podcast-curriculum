## Why this episode matters

This is the WATCHING GREAT MINDS chapter on thinking at the largest scale. Thiel's production function is integration: take Girard's anthropology, Schmitt's politics, the Bible's narrative, and Shakespeare's psychology, and aim them all at one question, how to get through an apocalyptic age without building the totalitarian machine. Tyler's role is the skeptic with the cyclical objection. You learn three options on terrible history, the katechon, and why Thiel bets against the math people.

## What is political theology?

Tyler: the phrase comes from Bakunin mocking Mazzini, then Carl Schmitt saying everyone needs one. What does it mean to Thiel?

Thiel: a fuzzy, broad concept, best motivated by contrast. Late modernity is hyper-specialization where you cannot think about the big picture: "Adam Smith's pin factory on steroids." Political theology integrates the facets of life to make progress. The Enlightenment abandoned it for two reasons: it was too hard, a fool's errand, or too dangerous and divisive. Westphalia in 1648 settled religion by forgetting it. That compromise fit the 18th century. In the 21st, "it is maybe more dangerous not to think about things," more dangerous to become "ever smaller cogs in an ever bigger machine."

The method: start at human scale. Do you begin with the microscopic, telescopic, atomic, cosmic, or with common sense, the world around you, politics, economics, society, culture? Thiel picks the Socratic option: the human world is the access point. "There is some deep link between the university and the universe. There is some deep link between the failing multiversity and the crazed multiverse." Fix the university and you start to make sense of the universe.

## Why not Calvinism?

Tyler: Thiel calls himself religious but not spiritual. That is a Calvinist point. Put aside predestination: Calvinism insists we know nothing about heaven, so claiming knowledge of heaven is an arrogation of man's power. That matches Thiel's critique of the left. Calvinism is concrete, serious, takes governance and authority literally. Why is Thiel not just a Calvinist?

### The five points

Thiel: "I am still mostly a libertarian, Tyler." There are some good things in Calvinism. It is so anti-utopian it helps against communism. But five-point Calvinism is total depravity, unconditional election, limited atonement, irresistible grace, perseverance of the saints. He agrees with maybe not one of the five.

### The Girardian argument

His Girardian anthropological argument: archaic religion links gods and scapegoats. We project violence onto scapegoats and turn them into gods. Atheist liberalism does the same with Mr. God. "Is not Calvinism just an extreme form of scapegoating, where Mr. God did everything, he determined everything?" Distrust scapegoating Mr. God for everything.

### The intellectual argument

His intellectual argument: the medievals believed in the power of the intellect and the weakness of the will. The moderns reversed it. If total depravity means you cannot even think, we could not hold this conversation. The Thomistic distinction between intellect and will matters here.

### The effective altruists are the real Calvinists

Then the sting: the effective altruists and East Bay rationalists are closer to Calvinism than he is. "They claim to be rationalists, but... the inward-facing thing, it is all just spaghetti code. You can never be right about anything. Maybe you can be a little bit less wrong." He opposes both Calvinism and so-called rationalism.

### Pope Francis and Lutheranism

Tyler: why not slide into Catholicism or Orthodoxy or Mormonism, where free will has room? Thiel: "My two-word rebuttal to Roman Catholicism is Pope Francis." He grew up Lutheran. Judged by 16th-century standards, the Reformation had to come from outside. It could not start from within. The Lutheran church was less globally centralized, and "there is probably still some part of the Protestant political project that lines up more closely with a libertarian view."

## The Bible takes the victim's side

Tyler: what from the Hebrew Bible entered his political thought?

### Continuity, not two gods

Thiel: his views are fairly orthodox Christian. There is continuity between Old and New. "The Christian God is the original progressive, where the new is better than the old." But exaggerate the difference and you get two gods. The extreme progressive higher criticism of the 19th century "was all deeply anti-Semitic." Too progressive ends in anti-Semitism.

### The victim-side reversal

The Girardian intuition: the Bible always reverses perspective and takes the side of the victim. Genesis gives Cain and Abel. Rome's founding myth gives Romulus and Remus, told from Romulus's point of view. The Bible tells its founding murder from Abel's point of view. The Exodus would normally be told from the Egyptians' view: troublemakers expelled. The Bible tells it from the Israelites' view. "You have this inversion of perspectives throughout the Old Testament."

## Three options on terrible history

Tyler: maybe the woke are right that history is something really bad, with excesses as advertising. Why not join them?

Thiel: history was very bad, and conservatives should not whitewash it. The happy-slaves argument "is a loser argument." The rough Christian frame: history is terrible and awful. Christianity is worse than Islam or Judaism on this, because only in Christianity can you murder God in the form of a person. The original sin and the violence run deeper. "Then there is some way that we are all part of that matrix. You need to have forgiveness."

Then the three options:

### The Christian in-between

One, the Christian in-between: history is terrible, but we must find a way to forgive people.

### The woke version

Two, the woke version: history is terrible, and we forget the forgiveness part.

### The Nietzschean right

Three, the right-wing Nietzschean, Bronze Age Pervert alternative: forget the history, it is oppressive, "I am sick of this guilt trip."

"The in-between Christian one, I think, is the most tenable, even though there are all sorts of tensions in that."

### The victimhood cap

On victimhood, from his Harvard talk: the left must learn to relativize victimhood. It is okay to say you are a victim, up to a point. "You cannot say that you are a greater victim than Christ. Once you do that, you have probably lost perspective."

### The Western canon

Other holy books: the Western canon. Shakespeare, Cervantes, Goethe treated as almost semi-divine writers. That is the attitude needed to read any of them seriously. Science fiction: he read a lot as a kid, little now. "It is all too depressing."

## Schmitt: friends, enemies, and the scapegoat machine

Tyler: his graduate students keep asking why Carl Schmitt is back. What is valuable in him?

### Weimar and the 2020s rhyme

Thiel: Schmitt rose in 1920s Weimar. He got entangled with the Nazis, distanced himself later: "very bad judgment in certain ways." The interest comes from the parallel: post-WWI Germany could not go back to throne and altar and did not want liberal democracy. Weimar thinkers reopened the political theology questions the Enlightenment whitewashed. The dwarves in Moria dug too deep and woke the Balrog. The US in the 2020s rhymes: liberalism exhausted, questions far outside the Overton window, the range of ideas acceptable in public discourse.

### Friends and enemies

Schmitt's powerful insight: The Concept of the Political defines politics as the division into friends and enemies.

### The Reagan dinner

Apply it to the 1980s Reagan coalition: free-market libertarians, defense hawks, social conservatives. Seat the millionaire, the general, and the priest at dinner. What do they talk about? Almost nothing in common, yet the coalition worked. The answer: anti-communism, a common enemy. That was Thiel's formative political idea as a teenager in the late 70s and early 80s. When the Berlin Wall fell in 1989, the constellation disintegrated. "There is a natural Schmittian analysis of this."

### The scapegoat-machine critique

Where Schmitt goes haywire: is politics a market that works better when understood, or a scapegoating machine that works only while hidden? Tell a village to accuse a random elderly woman of witchcraft for psychosocial unity, and the trick fails once everyone sees the mechanism. Schmitt had an optimistic Enlightenment rationality: describe the friend-enemy division and the political strengthens. In practice, describing it "probably, actually, in some ways accelerated its disintegration instead."

### The cyclicality objection

Tyler: does Schmitt miss cyclicality? Liberalism collapsed before WWI, collapsed in Weimar, came back by 1948 in West Germany. Why is the cyclical view not correct?

Thiel: he stresses the one-time and world-historical over the timeless. Every moment happens once. History has meaning and a linearity: the Judeo-Christian view against the classical Greco-Roman cyclical one. "I do not know if you can have a concept of history that is cyclical."

### Thucydides versus Daniel

His exhibit: Thucydides versus Daniel. Thucydides gives the Periclean peace collapsing into the Athens-Sparta war, studied again after WWI for the parallel with the hundred years after Napoleon. But none of the details matter. Thucydides makes up the speeches. Daniel gives four kingdoms in succession, a one-time world history where everything is unique, not repeated. "The real first historian was Daniel, and Thucydides is not even close."

The EU is not the Roman Empire, because we have nuclear weapons and they did not, even in 1900. Science and technology have a progressive character. "I do not know how you unlearn all the knowledge we gained, even since 1900."

## The Antichrist comes first

Tyler: are we entering a millenarian age like the English 17th century?

### The risk list

Thiel: no moment repeats. But the apocalyptic elements are real, and they come from a nonreligious source: science and technology. Nuclear weapons after 1945, bioweapons, the Wuhan lab question, AI paperclips, climate change. Girard noted the Catholic Church stopped its Advent end-times sermons after 1945, to reassure people that nuclear weapons had nothing to do with Armageddon, even as the first test was named Trinity and the rockets got gods' names.

### Scylla and Charybdis

Add one more existential risk to the list: a one-world totalitarian government, "at least as scary as the others." In biblical eschatology you worry about Armageddon, but "you are also supposed to worry about the Antichrist. Maybe you are supposed to worry more about the Antichrist because the Antichrist comes first." Navigate between the Scylla of the risks and the Charybdis of the totalitarian catastrophe.

### Two Antichrist novels

The literary problem: the two good Antichrist novels are pre-WWI. Robert Hugh Benson's Lord of the World (1908), a Catholic book, and Solovyov's War, Progress, and the End of History (1900). Both show a future world dictator taking over, by a daemonium ex machina: hypnotic speeches nobody remembers, souls sold for no reason.

### The post-1945 novel

Post-1945 the novel writes itself: the Antichrist comes to power by constantly talking about Armageddon. The short film One World or None made the case: give nuclear weapons to a one-world government or the world blows up. "Dr. Strangelove or Greta?" Strangelove is more dangerous, but making everyone ride a bicycle "requires some real, real enforcement."

### The Bostrom 2019 recipe

The Bostrom 2019 essay on stopping AI risk proposes changing the culture so nobody has heterodox ideas, plus effective global government and global compute governance. "That sounds to me at least as scary as the AI."

### The feudalism objection

Tyler: is the greater risk not disorderly feudalism, Balkanization, chaos? Thiel: he leaned that way after 9/11, expecting chaotic terrorism. Instead came the Patriot Act, tracking of money flows, monitoring of people. The Antichrist's slogan, 1 Thessalonians 5:3, is "peace and safety." We went the peace-and-safety direction, not the chaos direction. Illegal Swiss bank accounts are nearly impossible. "It is much harder to exit the United States than it was 20, 30 years ago."

## Muddling through is not thinking

Tyler: what variables track whether the West muddles through?

### The muddle-through narcotic

Thiel: it is not an empirical question. Agree on the variables and you have won half the argument. The sunrise argument is a bad argument: the sun rose and set every day, so it will probably keep doing that. The post-Cold War globalization story said goods, people, ideas, and money would move freely and the world would integrate peacefully: just muddle through. Then the US-China story went haywire versus Fukuyama's 1989 vision. Without the muddle-through narcotic, "the alarm bells would have gone off much sooner." Maybe globalization leads to neoliberal paradise. Maybe to the Antichrist's totalitarian state.

### The analytic and moral case

Tyler: so it is metaphysical? Thiel: not metaphysical, "somewhat analytic." And moral: there is a duty in refusing muddle-through talk. Libertarians were not critical enough of the one-world state over the last 20 or 30 years. "There was some failure of the imagination."

### You are not a lottery ticket

His positive restatement: "Whenever people think you can just muddle through, you are probably set up for some kind of disaster." From Zero to One, the chapter "You are not a lottery ticket": the investor who writes checks to everyone at a desk on South Beach, muddling through, has "a formula for losing all your money." Muddling through, Calvinism, rationalism: the shared flaw is "a distrust in human agency, a distrust in human thought, a distrust in our ability to make choices." It is anti-intellectual.

## The katechon: the finger in the dyke

### A substitute for muddling through

Tyler's flash of insight: the katechon is Thiel's substitute for muddling through. Thiel will not believe things just muddle along, yet nothing collapsed. Something holds the finger in the dyke. That something is the katechon. Correct?

### The restraining force

Thiel: it is a mysterious idea from the Bible, the restraining force that holds back the Antichrist and the one-world state. He does not put too much stock in it. It is unstable, provisional, archaic, and you cannot identify it with an institution: "you are not supposed to immanentize the katechon," just as you do not immanentize the eschaton. The eschaton is the final state of history, the end toward which everything points. To immanentize it is to force that end-state into present politics.

### The Girardian cut

The Girardian cut: anything set up as the opposite gets mimetically entangled with its enemy. There is always a risk the katechon becomes the Antichrist. The proto-Antichrist was Nero. Claudius, the good emperor, was the katechon restraining Nero. But both are Roman emperors.

### Anti-communism, 1949 to 1989

His historical identification: from 1949 to 1989, the katechon was anti-communism, and communism was the Antichrist ideology of the 20th century. What stopped communism was not one country or a libertarian debating society. It was "something pretty violent, pretty hard to morally justify, not really that Christian, but it had this unifying effect." Then 1989: anti-communism morphs into neoliberalism and Bush 41's new world order. Anti-communism aspires to stop world control, not to exercise it. But the thing perpetuates itself and starts governing the world in anti-communism's name.

### The recursive danger

The recursive danger: "Does not the Antichrist just come to power by acting as a katechon?" Greta says she is the katechon stopping climate change. Useful concept, limited weight.

## Agency, Shakespeare, and Caesar

Tyler: is all this weight on human agency compatible with Lutheranism?

### Agency and Lutheranism

Thiel: "I am not a perfect Lutheran." The biblical anchor is the anti-sacrificial theme. The individual emerged where the state was not absolute, sacred, or providential. Girard called Christ the first political atheist: if Christ is the Son of God, then Caesar Augustus, son of the divinized Caesar, is not, and the Roman Empire is not divinely ordained. That opens the space for a less unitary system.

### Rand the Christian

Thiel even calls Ayn Rand "a pretty good Christian in this way," scandalous but consistent: "you just cannot sacrifice the individual. You should not sacrifice your mind. You should not sacrifice your reason."

### Mimetic Shakespeare

On Shakespeare versus Christianity: Macbeth's "Life is a tale told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, signifying nothing" is Macbeth speaking, not Shakespeare. The real contrast is with Marx. Marx thought conflicts came from objectively different class interests. Shakespeare is proto-Girardian, mimetic: "conflicts are the most intense when they do not differ at all." Romeo and Juliet opens with two houses "alike in dignity." Identical, which is why they hate each other so much. Hamlet: the truly great person fights over things as ephemeral as honor or an eggshell, while average people fight over things that matter. Shakespeare would distrust today's extreme ideological differences: another form of political atheism.

### Julius Caesar and reenactment

Tyler: Julius Caesar has no katechon and no muddling through. They sacrifice Caesar, get civil war and endless dying. The pessimistic Thiel scenario.

Thiel: the play is about reenactment. Brutus joins the conspiracy because his ancestor, another Brutus, overthrew Tarquin in 509 BC. He thinks he repeats the old murder. Shakespeare even has the actor anticipate future audiences repeating the scene on stage, "the future applause in the Shakespearean theater."

### Booth as Brutus

The literal reenactment: John Wilkes Booth, a Shakespearean actor, shooting Lincoln in 1865 crying "Sic semper tyrannis," thinking he was Brutus. Lincoln's own 1838 Young Men's Lyceum address casts him as a proto-Caesar, warning of ambitious men for whom the presidency is not enough. Cycles are visible, Thiel concedes, but "that is what we want to transcend." Brutus was wrong to reenact, and the Booth story is "pretty sad."

## AI: bad news for the math people

Tyler: will the wordcels lose to LLMs?

### Worse for the math people

Thiel's intuition: the opposite holds. It looks much worse for the math people than the word people. Within three to five years, models may solve all US Math Olympiad problems.

### The egalitarian math test

The history of the math bias: the French Revolution started testing math because verbal ability was believed to run in families, while math ability was distributed "in this idiot savant way throughout the population." Prioritizing math was meritocratic and egalitarian.

### The Soviet control machine

By the Soviet 20th century, the number theorist with a medal looks more like a control mechanism: "the math people are singularly clueless. They do not understand anything, but if we put them on a pedestal... then it is actually a way to control." The chess grandmaster who understands nothing about the world serves the same function.

### The Silicon Valley bias

Silicon Valley in the early 21st century is "way too biased toward the math people," and the bias looks deeply unstable. Medical schools weed out through physics and calculus, "and I am not sure that is really correlated with your dexterity as a neurosurgeon." He does not want his brain surgeon doing prime factorizations mid-operation.

### The 1997 lesson

Thiel's own chess bias in the late 80s and early 90s: test everyone on chess, gate everything on it. Computers undermined that in 1997. "Is not that the fate of math? And is not that a long-overdue rebalancing of our society?"

### AI geopolitics

On AI questions worth asking: GDP versus inequality, centralization, "if crypto is libertarian, why cannot we say that AI is communist?" Returns to scale are so large that "maybe even San Francisco will survive." Geopolitics: the pro-China case is no hesitation about training on their people. The pro-US case is the lead, plus the hope that a model that cannot answer about Winnie the Pooh must be nerfed into uselessness. The effective altruists, he suggests, do what the CCP wants by trying to stop the LLMs. If AI helps the US more, the volatility could accelerate China's timetable on Taiwan: "you are ultimately going to lose, and maybe you have to invade Taiwan in the next year or two."

### The hedgehog and the fox

The final question, what he will learn next: the hedgehog and the fox from Isaiah Berlin. Tyler is the fox. Thiel is the hedgehog with a few core ideas: "this wonderful and terrible history of the world that we live through as Christianity's unraveling our culture, and we have to figure out a way to get to the other side."

### The Bitcoin coda

Audience coda on Bitcoin: still hopeful it is net anti-one-world-order, "just based on all the people who are against it," but that may be too simplistic and Schmittian. Genuine anonymity is the test. Physical cash may still beat Bitcoin for questionable purposes. As a one-time exit from a particular government's control, "it is probably still extremely good for that."

## Think it yourself

Fermi and journaling with less scaffolding. No answers shown. Reason from scratch.

1. Fermi: models may solve all US Math Olympiad problems within three to five years. Estimate how many working mathematicians do work that a Olympiad-solver cannot touch. Show the decomposition. What breaks in your estimate?
2. Journal: write your own three options on one terrible history you know well. What does your in-between look like? What forgiveness does it require, specifically?
3. Identify a katechon in your industry: the restrainer everyone relies on. Now argue it turns into the Antichrist. Steelman both sides in one page.
4. Take the Reagan dinner test to a coalition you belong to. Seat its three types at a table. What is their common enemy? What happens to the coalition the day the enemy falls?
5. Rewrite one current feud as mimetic: two houses alike in dignity. What do the identical rivals actually fight over? What would Marx say, and why is he wrong here?
6. Audit your own muddling-through: list three decisions you dodge with "it will work out." For each, write the lottery-ticket version and the agency version.
