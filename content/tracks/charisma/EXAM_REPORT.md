## PART 1: The 30 questions

### Episode 1 — Vanessa Van Edwards, Cues

**Q1 (numerical).** The chapter says the most popular TED talks averaged 465 hand gestures per 18 minutes and the least popular averaged 271. Compute the gesture gap and the percentage difference. Does the chapter's "about 72 percent more" check out?

**A1.** 465 − 271 = 194 gestures. 194 ÷ 271 = 0.716, about 72 percent. The chapter's figure checks out exactly. Cite: ep01:104 ("The difference is 194 gestures, or about 72 percent more movement"), ep01:181-183 (figure 4 table: 465, 271, gap 194).

**Q2 (explain like new).** Explain signal amplification bias to someone who has never heard the term, and say what the chapter prescribes about it.

**A2.** You believe other people read your cues louder than they really do. You think your nerves scream; they whisper. You think your confidence shines; it glows dimly. Because you underestimate how quietly you signal, the prescription is to turn the volume up on purpose: bigger gestures, clearer inflection, deliberate warmth. Cite: ep01:38 (full definition and prescription).

**Q3 (applied).** You walk into a pitch radiating competence (steeple hands, numbers voice, downward inflection) and the room reads as cold and untrusting. Name the thermostat region you are in and the repair.

**A3.** High competence plus low warmth is the danger zone: respected, not trusted (ep01:60, ep01:66 figure row). The repair is to add warmth cues, triple nod, head tilt, lean in, moving both dials to the sweet spot just past the middle (ep01:60, ep01:65).

### Episode 2 — Chris Voss, Hard Conversations

**Q4 (numerical).** Voss permits a mirror of one to three words, and one move per text. A counterpart says "We cannot meet the deadline Friday." Give the longest legal mirror and the complete mirror move.

**A4.** The longest legal mirror uses the last three words: "the deadline Friday." The full move is "The deadline Friday?" with an upward, curious inflection, then silence (most people fill the silence with information). Cite: ep02:51 (mirror definition, one to three words, the worked "Cannot do that?" example), ep02:53 (one to three words, one move per text).

**Q5 (explain like new).** Explain labeling and the emotion sequence it walks.

**A5.** Labeling means naming the other person's emotion or situation out loud, tentatively: "It seems like this feels frustrating." Emotion runs as rock, paper, scissors: sadness moves into anger, anger into calm, calm opens into positive feelings. You cannot jump from furious to happy; you walk the sequence, and labeling is the tool that walks it. A wrong label still works, because the person corrects you and the correction is information. Cite: ep02:43 (rock-paper-scissors sequence), ep02:45 (tentative label definition; the chapter's "react before you answer" rule at ep02:47 sets the order).

**Q6 (applied).** A counterpart makes a vague threat ("we could pull the account") and adds urgency ("this offer expires today"). Walk through the chapter's playbook in order.

**A6.** First, run the specificity test: a vague threat is noise, a specific threat is signal. Demand specifics and bluffs collapse (ep02:69). Second, treat urgency as the scam flag: insist on verification, as in the Vegas breakfast story. Legitimate deals survive verification. If it cannot wait for a check, it is not a deal (ep02:77, ep02:137 figure row). If suspicion remains about your side, open with an accusation audit: list the worst things they could think about you before they say them (ep02:59).

### Episode 3 — Vanessa Van Edwards, Conversations

**Q7 (numerical).** The Candor Corpus finding sets the good-turn bar at 15 to 30 seconds, 45 at most. Your average turn runs 60 seconds. By what factor are you over the hard cap, and what does the chapter call what you are doing?

**A7.** 60 ÷ 45 = 1.33, so about one third over the hard cap. Past 45 seconds you are broadcasting, not conversing (ep03:93; figure 3 at ep03:157-163 names the same bar).

**Q8 (explain like new).** Explain an information gap and how Howard Stern uses one.

**A8.** An information gap is the space between what the listener knows and what they want to know. Stern opens the gap early and lets the guest close it slowly. The listener stays because the brain hates an open gap. A good question opens a gap; a bad question closes the topic (ep03:57).

**Q9 (applied).** You are stuck in a fading conversation at a networking event. Execute the graceful exit.

**A9.** Exit at the peak, never after it rots. The four-step sequence: (1) future question, "What are you looking forward to," which ends on anticipation; (2) a specific compliment or promised follow-up; (3) exit voice, slightly brighter and conclusive; (4) physically step back so the body ends what the mouth started (ep03:121, ep03:164 figure rows). The peak-end rule: people remember the peak moment and the ending, so the ending writes the memory (ep03:119, ep03:193).

### Episode 4 — Charlie Houpert, Charisma on Command

**Q10 (numerical).** Count the hooks in this Velcro answer and name each: "I grew up in Philadelphia, but I spent last year in Brazil learning to surf." What rule does the count imply?

**A10.** Three hooks: Philadelphia, Brazil, surfing (ep04:88, ep04:156). The rule: a smooth answer gives the listener nothing to grab; three hooks give the other person three doors into the conversation (ep04:156: "Philadelphia is smooth. Philadelphia plus Brazil plus surfing is Velcro").

**Q11 (explain like new).** Explain what makes an answer Velcro instead of smooth.

**A11.** Small talk fails because answers are smooth: no edges, nothing to grab. Velcro answers carry hooks, specific vivid details the listener can grab. "Where are you from?" "Philadelphia" is smooth. "I grew up in Philadelphia, but I spent last year in Brazil learning to surf" is Velcro: three hooks, and the listener grabs one (ep04:88, ep04:95 figure row).

**Q12 (applied).** You are a shy engineer. Design your first week of the 30-day arc without breaking the "vibe one level higher" constraint.

**A12.** Week 1 is courage reps: one extra sentence daily, one extra sentence to the taxi driver, one comment in the meeting. The dose must stay small enough to survive and big enough to count (ep04:72, ep04:133). Calibrate to one level above your own baseline, never ten: one level is sustainable, ten levels is a costume (ep04:68). And pick only the one or two charisma styles that fit your wiring, never someone else's: you do not need all five (ep04:41).

### Episode 5 — Huberman on Bonding

**Q13 (numerical).** Write Cacioppo's loneliness equation and apply it: you rate wanted connection at 8 out of 10 and actual connection at 4 out of 10. What is the score? Also state the finding of the 10-hour isolation study.

**A13.** Loneliness = wanted connection minus actual connection = 8 − 4 = 4 points (ep05:51). The Saxe and Tye study: after 10 hours of isolation, images of social scenes activated the brain's craving regions the way food images do after fasting; fasting reversed the effect. Social contact and food run on overlapping craving machinery (ep05:63).

**Q14 (explain like new).** Explain why dopamine is the movement-toward molecule and not the feel-good molecule.

**A14.** Dopamine drives craving and pursuit, not pleasure. Acute isolation raises dopamine-driven social craving, which is why you want people more after being alone. The same system can later drive you away. Craving without liking explains addiction, stalking exes, and doomscrolling (ep05:57, ep05:148: "dopamine drives movement-toward. Pleasure is a different system").

**Q15 (applied).** A friend has been alone for two weeks: irritable, withdrawn, snapping at you. Name the phase, the driver molecule, and the two-step response.

**A15.** This is the chronic isolation phase: weeks and beyond, behavior antisocial, irritable, withdrawn (ep05:102-104 timeline table). The driver named is tachykinin: prolonged isolation raises tachykinin signaling, producing the cranky, prickly state of the long-lonely (ep05:53). The response is the tachykinin check: label it, "this is chronic-isolation chemistry," then schedule contact before deciding anything about anyone. Start with low-demand contact, a walk not a party (ep05:163, ep05:170-174 skills list).

### Episode 6 — Dr. Kay Tye, Valence and Rank

**Q16 (numerical).** Parole grant rates ran near 90 percent after a meal and near 10 percent before the next meal. Compute the swing as a ratio and restate the mechanism in fork terms.

**A16.** 90 ÷ 10 = 9, a ninefold swing between fed and hungry judges (ep06:52). In fork terms: the amygdala carries ghrelin receptors; when hunger rises the fork's priorities flip, fear and caution outrank reward and exploration, and an avoid decider votes no (ep06:50, ep06:52, ep06:122).

**Q17 (explain like new).** Explain why the amygdala is a valence fork and not a fear button.

**A17.** The textbook says amygdala equals fear. The lesion evidence says otherwise: monkeys with amygdala damage did not become fearless. They became flat: nothing tagged as good or bad, so nothing was worth approaching or avoiding (ep06:31). The basolateral amygdala sends reward and fear through separate projections. Patient SM showed no fear of frightening faces yet went into full panic on a CO2 challenge: evaluation and arousal are separable (ep06:35). The structure evaluates good versus bad; fear is one branch. FAB: Fear is A Branch (ep06:31-35, ep06:119).

**Q18 (applied).** You are about to judge founder pitches before lunch, and you keep filing one founder as a "competitor." Name the two confounds the chapter warns about and the prescribed reframe.

**A18.** Confound one is hunger: a hungry brain is a defensive brain, and the fork flips toward avoid, so eat first and run the hunger audit (ep06:50-52, ep06:165). Confound two is competitor framing: once the brain files someone as a competitor, empathy circuits throttle down and you cannot empathize with someone you try to beat (ep06:76). The reframe: rewrite the other person from competitor to counterpart in one sentence, and empathy comes back online (ep06:76, ep06:165: "check the big three: hunger, rank threat, competitor framing").

### Episode 7 — Robert Cialdini, Influence

**Q19 (numerical).** The chapter says donations rose 400 percent and that this quintupled giving. Show the arithmetic, and explain what was wrong with the old wording "quadrupled."

**A19.** A 400 percent increase means the original plus four times the original: 1 + 4 = 5 times the original, which is quintupled. "Quadrupled" means 4x, which would be a 300 percent increase, so it contradicted the stated 400 percent (ep07:112: "Donations rose 400 percent. Four words of shared identity quintupled giving").

**Q20 (explain like new).** Explain the rule of reciprocation using the McDonald's balloon study.

**A20.** The rule says we must give back to those who first gave. Cialdini's colleague gave children a balloon for one week. Families who received it on entry bought 25 percent more food than families who received it on leaving. First is the key. The standard business model asks the customer to go first: sign, then we serve. Reciprocation reverses it: we go first (ep07:35, ep07:37).

**Q21 (applied).** Draft a unity opener for a hard ask, then name the chapter's defense against a fake unity claim.

**A21.** Unity first, request second. Cialdini needed data from a colleague famous for refusing. Leading with the deadline had already failed. He led instead with "twelve years in the same psychology department" and had the data that afternoon (ep07:114). The campus version: "I am a student here too" added to a donation ask raised donations 400 percent (ep07:112). The defense against fake unity: check the membership is real and relevant (ep07:125 figure row: "Fake tribal claims" defended by "Check the membership is real and relevant"). "We are both human" is true and useless.

### Episode 8 — Robert Greene, The Daily Laws

**Q22 (numerical).** Verify the triad mnemonic's arithmetic against the thirteen laws. Show the full count.

**A22.** Triad 1, self: demonstrate, purpose, release (3). Triad 2, mind: negative capability, character craft, drop cynicism (3). Triad 3, others: judge behavior, absence, desire (3). Triad 4, world: time, groups, fools (3). Plus one: insignificance. 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 13 (ep08:178).

**Q23 (explain like new).** Explain negative capability.

**A23.** Negative capability is the habit of suspending judgment and entertaining views opposite to your own. Most people start a project seeing only options A, B, and C: A is what they always do, B is what someone else did, C is the odd one they will never try. Negative capability adds D, E, and F. It asks what your enemy would do. It drops the ego enough to admit someone else's approach is better, as Mozart did when he studied Bach's counterpoint and absorbed it (ep08:55-57).

**Q24 (applied).** Your team keeps explaining a strategy instead of showing it, and your calendar is full of dead time. Give the Law 1 move and the Law 10 conversion.

**A24.** Law 1: demonstrate, do not explicate. Explaining puts you in the weak position because nobody trusts words anymore. Wren's logic: a critic demanded an extra column for his church's roof, so Wren added one with a six-inch gap at the top that held nothing. The critic felt he had won. Wren had won, without an argument (ep08:37, ep08:39). Law 10: convert dead time to live time. Reclaim ownership mentally: the job is temporary, the hours are yours. Extract learning from the worst parts. Aim energy outward at real humans. The brown branch greens when you stop donating it to drama (ep08:103, ep08:171).

### Episode 9 — Charles Duhigg, Supercommunicators

**Q25 (numerical).** Supercommunicators ask 10 to 20 times as many questions as average. If an average person asks 3 questions in a conversation, what is the supercommunicator range? Also state the two phone-compensation numbers from the chapter.

**A25.** 3 × 10 = 30 to 3 × 20 = 60 questions per conversation (ep09:58). On phones, where the face channel is missing, people compensate by over-enunciating about 30 percent and adding about 15 percent more vocal emotion (ep09:124).

**Q26 (explain like new).** Explain neural entrainment.

**A26.** When two people hold the same conversation type, their brains and bodies sync: breathing rates converge, heart rates converge, pupil dilation mirrors, and the same brain regions activate with the same energy. This releases a reward cascade: the brain pays you for connecting, because connection kept the species alive. It fails across mismatched types, which is why Duhigg's emotional arrival and his wife's practical answer produced zero connection (ep09:45, ep09:43).

**Q27 (applied).** Your partner comes home upset about work. You offer solutions. Both of you get angrier. Diagnose the PES mismatch and write the corrected response, including the third looping step.

**A27.** Your partner is in an emotional conversation ("hear how I feel") and you answered in a practical one ("what should we do"). Different circuits, different brain regions, zero connection. Neither is wrong; you are unmatched (ep09:43). The correction: match first with one small move into the emotional region, "What did that feel like," then run the loop. Step 1: ask the deep question. Step 2: repeat back in your own words to prove processing. Step 3, the one everyone skips: "Did I hear you correctly." Step 3 asks permission to be acknowledged as a listener, and once acknowledged, the speaker becomes far more likely to listen to you in return (ep09:62-70, ep09:140).

### Episode 10 — Matthew Dicks, Storytelling

**Q28 (numerical).** Dicks uses a six-praises-to-one-correction ratio with kids, and he names six attention devices. If you give 2 corrections in a workshop, how many praises does the rule demand? Name the six devices.

**A28.** 2 × 6 = 12 praises (ep10:140: "Keep the ratio at six praises to one correction"). The six devices: Elephant, Backpack, Breadcrumbs, Hourglass, Crystal ball, Humor (ep10 figure 1, ep10:84-91).

**Q29 (explain like new).** Explain the but-and-therefore test.

**A29.** Replace "and then" with "but" and "therefore." An and-story is a list: this happened and then this happened. Remove any scene and nothing breaks, which proves the scene was decorative. A but-therefore story is a chain: this happened, but that blocked it, therefore this. Remove any scene and the chain snaps, which proves every scene was load-bearing. "But" is the most powerful word in storytelling: "I got in the Uber today, but..." makes you need the next clause (ep10:105, ep10:108).

**Q30 (applied).** Convert this and-story into a but-therefore story: "I had an idea and then I built a prototype and then I showed my boss."

**A30.** Expected rewrite (any version that passes the test is correct): "I had an idea, but my first prototype failed the one test that mattered, therefore I rebuilt it overnight, and when I showed my boss she killed it anyway, but her objection revealed the real product." Every "and then" must become a "but" or "therefore," and every scene must be load-bearing: remove one and the story collapses (ep10:105). A rewrite that keeps three disconnected facts with no causal chain fails the test.
