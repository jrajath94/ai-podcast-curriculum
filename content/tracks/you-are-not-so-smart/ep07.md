## Why this episode matters

Six chapters of diagnosis, one of repair. This chapter is the field manual. After the fraud, the retraction, and the restart, a randomized trial showed that one specific kind of conversation durably reduces prejudice: about 10 new supporters per 100 conversations, effects lasting months, surviving attack ads. It works for the exact reason the earlier chapters predict: nobody changes a mind from the outside. The voter changes it from the inside. No other episode gives you a tested protocol instead of advice.

## The loss that started it

### Proposition 8

In 2008, California's Proposition 8 threatened to make same-sex marriage illegal. Polls showed voters evenly split. The LGBT community expected to prevail. Daily life in California felt positive. Then the vote: 52 percent voted against same-sex marriage. Shock, fury, humiliation, and one question above all: why did they vote against us?

Dave Fleischer, who directs the Leadership LAB, the political arm of the Los Angeles LGBT Center, proposed the radical move: "Let's just go ask." Recruit a team, knock on the doors of people who voted against them, and find out why.

The scale of the LAB: the Center runs on more than $83 million a year, mostly healthcare. The Leadership LAB takes about 1 percent of that budget. One percent of $83 million is about $830,000 a year. The long game, on a shoestring inside a giant.

### Twelve thousand conversations

Over the following years the team held more than 12,000 conversations. They recorded them, first audio then video, watched them together, and kept what worked. Throw away the rest. Iterate. The technique that emerged was not designed from psychology journals. It was discovered in the field, though it rhymes with much of the literature: shades of cognitive behavioral therapy and Rogerian therapy in the listening, Arthur Aron's intimacy research in the vulnerability, Robert Cialdini's persuasion principles in the rapport, the Socratic method in the questions. None of it was planned. All of it was found.

## The fraud and the restart

The first batch of scientific research on the technique produced exciting results. Then David Broockman and Joshua Kalla found fraud in that first batch and helped get it retracted. The episode treats this as science working, not failing: the grade-school version where someone checks the exciting study, finds irregularities, and the field starts over.

They started over with a blank slate in Miami. The Los Angeles LGBT Center and SAVE went door to door deep canvassing about transgender rights, with Broockman and Kalla running a proper trial. Kalla went canvassing himself first, to feel what the conversations were like from the inside.

## The trial

### Treatment versus placebo

Definition: the deep-canvassing trial was a randomized field experiment modeled on a medical trial. Half the households got the intervention: the deep-canvassing conversation. Half got a placebo: a conversation about recycling. Attitude change was recorded at the door, then the same people were tracked with disguised follow-up surveys to see whether the change stuck or the peer group reasserted itself.

The measurement schedule: 3 days, 3 weeks, 6 weeks, and 3 months after the conversation. The outcome scale measured transgender tolerance. The comparison, at every point, was treatment-group average versus placebo-group average.

### The numbers

The treatment effect: about 0.25 to 0.3 standard deviations on the tolerance scale, sustained across the whole measurement window. Kalla's translation into plain numbers: about 10 new supporters for every 100 conversations. About 10 percent.

The hand-worked meaning of 0.25 standard deviations: on a normal distribution, moving the average by a quarter of a standard deviation moves the median person from the 50th percentile to about the 60th. One in ten doors flips. In politics, where elections turn on points, that is, in Kalla's field, huge.

Two properties make it remarkable. Duration: the effect lasted at least 3 months, which Kalla calls almost unheard of in persuasion research, where most effects die within days. [Note: trainer Laura Gardner says in the episode the change "lasts up to 9 months." The trial's published measurement window was 3 months, so 3 months is the verified figure and 9 months is her characterization.] Resistance: the team showed voters real opposition campaign ads against transgender rights, and even tried to persuade them the other way. The treated group stayed more supportive than the placebo group anyway. The change survived attack.

## Why it works: the theory

### Nothing you say works

The first thing trainers tell new canvassers, and it always surprises them: "There is nothing you can say, there is nothing you can tell this person that's going to change their mind." Talking points are impersonal and unpersuasive. Facts and figures bounce off the gut, which trusts feelings over data and rejects the rest "like an unhealthy virus" to protect internal consistency.

What works is reality over hypotheticals. Do not ask "what if you had a transgender child." That is make-believe, easy to answer any way you like. Ask what they actually do in a restroom: they go into a stall. Ask about their actual experience noticing people around them. Ground every step in their lived life, and pair any fact with something personal.

### System 2 does the lasting work

Broockman and Kalla's theoretical account runs through Daniel Kahneman's two systems. System 1 is fast, effortless, associative. System 2 is slow, effortful, deliberative. Standard campaigns speak System 1: thirty-second ads, mailers, billboards. A mailer can move someone for a day. Then the old belief returns.

Deep canvassing forces System 2. The canvasser asks questions the voter must answer, which forces the voter to do mental work: retrieve memories, examine feelings, construct sentences. The old voter-mobilization finding underneath: the same script read rushed does nothing. Read with questions that demand answers, it moves turnout, because the mental work is the active ingredient. Petty and Cacioppo's persuasion research says the same: effortful processing of a message predicts lasting attitude change. Passive exposure predicts nothing durable.

### Analogic perspective taking

The hardest mental work in the protocol has a name: analogic perspective taking. Take an analogy from your own life and apply it to someone else's. It is a high-level cognitive task. Young children do it poorly. After rapport is built and the issue is made personal, the canvasser asks the voter about times they felt judged, ostracized, or made to feel lesser. The voter's own memory of being on the receiving end becomes the bridge to the out-group's experience.

Broockman's version of the moment it lands: saying "discrimination is wrong" now feels different in his body. The abstract fact "prejudice is bad" was always known and always inert. The analogic link makes it felt, and the felt version rewrites the attitude.

McRaney's closing formula for the whole technique: a person changes their mind only by changing it themselves. The canvasser is a guide, not a persuader. The conversation is cooperative, not competitive: two people teaming up to solve a mystery, with no pressure to agree and nothing to prove.

## The technique in practice

### Radical hospitality

The doorstep numbers: conditional on someone opening the door, about 70 percent finish the conversation. Seventy percent, for a stranger at the door wanting to talk about prejudice. The trainers credit radical hospitality: from the minute volunteers arrive Saturday morning, they are treated as honored guests, and they are trained to extend the same to the voter. Active listening, shared stories, rapport first, always.

The training itself: about 1.5 hours, volunteers in small pods with a veteran pod leader, breaking into "puddles" for mock conversations at each step, alternating roles as canvasser and voter. McRaney, who attended three sessions, reports finding himself exploring vulnerable parts of his own life in the role-plays. The method trains the trainer too.

### What the canvasser actually does

Steve Deline, a field organizer, gives the field rules. Resist the urge to trust your superior knowledge. Do not deliver conclusions. Instead, uncover what in the voter's own life could lead to a shared value. Ask, listen, mirror. Proactively draw out the vulnerable, emotional material they would never volunteer. Then let them talk themselves through it.

Laura Gardner, who runs training, adds the psychological read the team converged on: these voters do not think of themselves as prejudiced. They want to be supportive of everybody. Something in their life history holds them back. The canvasser's curiosity is genuine because the answer is genuinely unknown at the start: "we had just absolutely no idea what we would find at the doors."

### The tree-bark stage

Broockman's honesty about the science: the team does not know exactly why it works. His analogy: 2,500 years ago, chewing a certain tree bark cured headaches. Later the active ingredient was distilled as aspirin. Deep canvassing is at the tree-bark stage. The effect is real. The active ingredient is not isolated. The technique is a kitchen sink: active processing, perspective taking, stories, video, rapport. Which levers matter most, and whether the principles generalize to climate change or vaccines, is the next research program. The episode is an invitation, not a conclusion.

### The Ross echo

McRaney closes the loop to episode 2 deliberately, replaying Lee Ross's line: in 40 years of conflict work, Ross never met anyone who wanted to meet the other side to be corrected. Deep canvassing is built for that world. It never asks anyone to admit they were wrong. It asks them to remember being judged, and lets the analogy do the rest.

## Questions and answers

> [!QA]
> Q: Explain deep canvassing like I am new. What happens at the door?
> A: A trained volunteer knocks, builds rapport through radical hospitality and active listening, and asks questions instead of delivering points. The voter is asked about their own experiences of being judged or ostracized, which creates an analogic bridge to the out-group's experience. Nothing the canvasser says changes the mind. The voter changes it by doing the mental work out loud.
> Follow-up: Why do 70 percent finish? Because the conversation is cooperative, not competitive: two people solving a mystery together, no pressure to agree, nothing to prove. People stay where they are not being sold.

> [!QA]
> Q: What did the trial actually find, in numbers?
> A: Randomized: deep canvassing versus a recycling placebo, measured at 3 days, 3 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months. Effect: 0.25 to 0.3 standard deviations on transgender tolerance, about 10 new supporters per 100 conversations, lasting at least 3 months and surviving opposition ads. In persuasion research, where effects usually die in days, that duration is almost unheard of.
> Follow-up: What is the honest uncertainty? The 9-month figure is the trainer's characterization. The trial measured 3 months. And the active ingredient is unknown: the technique is a kitchen sink at the tree-bark stage, aspirin not yet distilled.

> [!QA]
> Q: Why do facts and talking points fail at the door?
> A: The gut trusts feelings over data and rejects mismatched facts "like an unhealthy virus" to protect internal consistency. Talking points are impersonal. They motivate the base that already agrees and persuade nobody new. Hypotheticals ("what if you had a transgender child") are make-believe and easy to dodge. Only the voter's own lived reality has weight.
> Follow-up: What replaces the talking point? A reality check plus a personal pairing. "What do you actually do in a restroom? You go into a stall." Facts are allowed, but only paired with something from the voter's own life.

> [!QA]
> Q: What is the System 1 versus System 2 account of why it lasts?
> A: Campaigns speak System 1: fast, passive, ads and mailers. Effects last about a day. Deep canvassing forces System 2: slow, effortful, the voter retrieving memories and constructing answers. Effortful processing predicts durable attitude change (Petty and Cacioppo). The mental work is the active ingredient. The script without the questions does nothing.
> Follow-up: What is the mobilization parallel? The same get-out-the-vote script read rushed does nothing. Read with questions that demand answers, it moves turnout. The words are identical. The demanded thinking is the difference.

> [!QA]
> Q: What is analogic perspective taking, and why is it the hard part?
> A: Taking an analogy from your own life and applying it to someone else's: your memory of being judged becomes the bridge to the out-group's experience. It is a high-level cognitive task that young children do poorly. It is the moment the abstract fact "prejudice is bad" becomes felt, and the felt version rewrites the attitude.
> Follow-up: Why can the canvasser not do this step for the voter? Because a handed analogy is another talking point. The voter must retrieve their own memory and build the bridge themselves. Borrowed bridges do not hold weight.

> [!QA]
> Q: What happened with the fraud, and why does the episode lead with it?
> A: The first research batch showed exciting results, then Broockman and Kalla found fraud and got it retracted. They restarted from a blank slate in Miami with a proper randomized trial. Leading with the fraud is the point: science is the process that catches its own frauds and reruns the experiment. The vindication means more because the failure is on the record.
> Follow-up: What does "tree-bark stage" mean? Chewing bark cured headaches millennia before aspirin was distilled. Deep canvassing's effect is real. Its active ingredient is not isolated. Knowing the stage prevents both dismissal and overclaiming.

> [!QA]
> Q: How does this connect to episode 2's Ross?
> A: Ross: in 40 years, nobody ever wanted to meet the other side to be corrected. Deep canvassing never asks for that. It never delivers facts to fix anyone. It asks voters to remember their own experience of being judged, which sidesteps the naive-realist defense entirely. The technique is engineered for the world Ross describes.
> Follow-up: How does it connect to episode 4's Mercier? The voter produces the arguments. The canvasser evaluates by listening and questioning. Production plus evaluation, split across two people, with truth winning. The doorstep is the argumentative theory in the field.

> [!QA]
> Q: Apply this to a colleague who resists a technical decision.
> A: Do not open with benchmarks or docs (talking points). Open with hospitality: genuine curiosity about their position. Ask about their lived experience: the outage that scarred them, the migration that burned them. Let them talk themselves through what that experience implies for the current choice. Pair any fact you bring with something from their history. Cooperative mystery-solving, not a debate to win.
> Follow-up: What is the honest limit? Ten per hundred is the measured rate, not a hundred per hundred. Most doors do not flip. The technique is the best-tested method available, not a mind-control ray. Budget for the base rate.

## Memory aids

- The rule in one line: nothing you say changes the mind. The voter changes it by doing the work.
- The numbers: 12,000 conversations, 10 per 100 flip, 0.25 to 0.3 SD, at least 3 months, survives attack ads.
- The trial: treatment versus recycling placebo, hidden surveys at 3 days, 3 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months.
- System 2 is the ingredient: effortful processing lasts. Passive exposure dies in a day.
- Analogic perspective taking: your memory of being judged becomes the bridge. Borrowed bridges do not hold.
- Reality over hypotheticals: the stall, not the imagined child.
- Never-confuse pair: persuasion versus conversion. Persuasion is something you do to someone. Conversion is something they do to themselves. The protocol only creates the conditions.
- Never-confuse pair: the script versus the questions. The same words rushed do nothing. With questions that demand answers, they move turnout. The active ingredient is the demanded thinking, not the text.
- Trap card: "I just need the right argument." The trainers' first lesson: there is nothing you can say that will change their mind. If you polish your points, you do the wrong activity.
- Trap card: "The fraud discredits the finding." The fraud was caught by the field's own replication attempt, and the rerun confirmed the effect. That sequence is science working, and it is why the finding is trusted.
- Trap card: "This works on everyone." Ten per hundred. Budget for the base rate or you will misread every campaign.

## How to imbibe this

- This week: pick one person whose mind you want to change (colleague, family). Do not prepare arguments. Prepare five questions about their relevant lived experience. Have the conversation. Ask, listen, mirror. Log what they said that surprised you.
- Catch yourself: the next time you feel the urge to send a link or a doc to win a point, stop. Replace it with one question about the other person's experience with the thing. Send the question.
- Behavior change per major idea:
  - Nothing you say works: ban talking points in one live disagreement for two weeks. Questions only. Count how often you slip.
  - Reality over hypotheticals: in your next technical debate, replace every "what if" with "when did you actually experience this." Log the difference in traction.
  - System 2: before your next decision meeting, write three questions that force attendees to retrieve and construct, not just react. Open with them.
  - Analogic bridge: when someone resists your position, ask about a time they felt the way your position's beneficiaries feel. Do not name the analogy. Let them build it.
  - Cooperative frame: state the frame out loud at the start of one hard conversation: "Let us solve this mystery together. No one has to agree today." Notice the temperature change.
- Monthly: review your question log. Which questions produced real thinking? Keep the five best as your personal canvassing script.

## Think it yourself

Do these with your own brain before touching AI. Write answers by hand.

1. Guided: 10 supporters per 100 conversations, 70 percent of opened doors finish. If a team knocks on 1,000 doors and half open, how many conversations complete and how many minds change? (Answer: 500 open, 350 complete, about 35 change. Now ask: at that rate, how many doors for 1,000 converts? About 28,600 knocks. What does that say about the ground game required?)
2. Fermi: the effect is 0.25 SD, moving the median from the 50th to about the 60th percentile. Suppose an election needs a 2-point swing and the electorate's tolerance scale has SD of 20 points. How many treated voters per 100 deliver the swing? (Answer: 0.25 times 20 is 5 points per treated voter on average. 2 points over 100 voters needs 200 point-voters of movement, about 40 treated voters per 100 at full effect. Now ask: what breaks first, the linearity or the turnout?)
3. Journal: "My unasked question." Think of someone you argue with regularly. Write the question about their lived experience you have never asked. Then write why you never asked it. The second answer is the diagnostic.
4. Argue both sides: "The active ingredient is analogic perspective taking" versus "the active ingredient is just being heard by a kind stranger." Design the experiment that separates them: what does each arm get? (Hint: one arm gets perspective questions without the hospitality script. Another gets hospitality without the perspective questions.)
5. Observe: for one week, in every disagreement, mark whether anyone asked a genuine question about the other's experience. Tally. What is the base rate of curiosity in your arguments? What would deep canvassing predict about their outcomes?
6. First principles: derive the "nothing you say works" rule from episodes 2 and 3 alone. If naive realism and keystone beliefs are true, what must a successful persuasion protocol look like? Check your derivation against the actual protocol. Where did you miss?

## Skills you can now use

- Run a deep-canvassing-style conversation: rapport, questions, lived experience, analogic bridge.
- Replace link-bombing with one experience-seeking question.
- Distinguish System 1 channels (ads, mailers) from System 2 channels (demanded thinking) and choose by desired duration.
- Tell reality-checks from hypotheticals, and use the former.
- Frame hard conversations as cooperative mystery-solving.
- Budget persuasion campaigns at the measured base rate: 10 per 100.
- Read a fraud-and-retraction sequence as science working, not failing.
