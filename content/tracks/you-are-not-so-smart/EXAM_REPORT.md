## ep01 — The Dunning-Kruger Effect

### Q1 [explain like I am new]
**Explain the double curse like I am new. What are its two problems, and why can they not be fixed separately?**

A: The double curse (double burden) is the finding that the same gaps in expertise cause two problems at once. Problem one: you make mistakes, because lacking expertise you choose badly. Problem two: you cannot detect those mistakes, because in most areas of life you use the exact same expertise to judge your performance that you used to produce it. The judge and the defendant are the same broken instrument. The two problems cannot be fixed separately because the judging instrument IS the performing instrument; only something outside the instrument (training up to competence, other people's eyes) breaks the loop. The chapter's own cure clause: incompetent people can recognize their incompetence once they become competent.
Citations: ep01:60 (definition), ep01:62 (two problems, judge/defendant), ep01:54 (recognition follows competence).

### Q2 [numerical]
**You estimate a migration at 6 weeks. It touches a system you have never worked in. A routine feature is also estimated at 6 weeks. Compute both buffered estimates under Dunning's rules, showing the arithmetic.**

A: Rule from the chapter: experienced managers inflate software estimates by 30 percent, and by up to 100 percent for a new operating system (ep01:183). The chapter's worked example: 10 weeks becomes 13 weeks at 30 percent, and up to 20 weeks for a new OS (ep01:185). Applying the same rules: the novel-system migration gets up to 6 x 2 = up to 12 weeks. The routine feature gets 6 x 1.3 = 7.8 weeks. Say the buffered number out loud before the raw one.
Citations: ep01:183 (buffer rules), ep01:185 (worked arithmetic).

### Q3 [mechanism]
**The chapter insists the effect "is not denial." Why does that exclusion matter, and what fix would treating it as denial wrongly prescribe?**

A: Denial means you know the truth and hide it from yourself; self-deception means the truth is in there somewhere. The chapter excludes both: the incompetent "are not in a position to make the call correctly at all" (ep01:56). Treating it as denial would prescribe honesty, confession, or "facing the truth" as the fix. The real fix targets the broken instrument instead: borrow other people's eyes, install devil's advocates, and budget buffers, because introspection cannot repair the judge that is the defendant (ep01:183).
Citations: ep01:56 (exclusion), ep01:183 (institutional fixes).

## ep02 — Naive Realism

### Q4 [explain like I am new]
**Explain naive realism like I am new. What is the claim, and what happens when someone disagrees with you?**

A: Naive realism is the conviction that you see the world in an objective, essentially unmediated way, with a one-to-one relationship between your experience and what the world really is (ep02:61). From this follows an expectation: other reasonable people will share your view. When they do not, the thing to explain is them: something about them (ignorance, self-interest, culture, youth) blocks the true picture, which is to say, your picture. You are uniquely the objective one.
Citation: ep02:61 (definition and the disagreement corollary).

### Q5 [mechanism]
**Why does Ross reject the "walk in their shoes" and "see through their glasses" metaphors? What does he offer instead?**

A: He dislikes them because the whole point of naive realism is that you cannot occupy their construction; the metaphor promises an impossible transfer (ep02:146). Instead he prescribes two smaller, honest moves: practice recognizing the authenticity of the other person (they act from values that are real to them), then hunt for shared fundamentals beneath the policy disagreement. The Ireland exercise is the field version: stop asking policy questions and ask what they want their communities and family life to look like; the differences shrink or vanish (ep02:146).
Citation: ep02:146 (rejection + replacement + Ireland exercise).

### Q6 [numerical]
**Ross reports 40 years of conflict-resolution work with zero cases of someone meeting the other side to be corrected. If he averaged 20 such meetings a year, what is the observed rate, and what does it imply for the bucket-of-facts model?**

A: 40 years x 20 meetings = about 800 meetings, with 0 cases of "I think I have things wrong" (ep02:103). Observed rate: 0 in 800. The bucket-of-facts fallacy is the belief that disagreement is a fact shortage curable by delivering facts; 0 conversions in 800 attempts means facts are not the bottleneck. The scientific method's actual contribution is not more facts but one prohibition: you may interpret data through your beliefs, but you may not then test your beliefs on the data you just interpreted (ep02:118).
Citations: ep02:103 (40 years, zero cases), ep02:118 (polarization study + the one prohibition).

## ep03 — Belief

### Q7 [explain like I am new]
**Explain the keystone-versus-brick distinction like I am new, using Monckton.**

A: A brick is ignorance: a missing piece, and facts fill it. A keystone holds the arch: beliefs that nourish your hero narrative (who you are, who the villains are, what fight you are in) are load-bearing, and facts that threaten them threaten the self, so the brain defends the arch, not the brick (ep03:90, ep03:222). Monckton's climate skepticism is the keystone of his identity as the aristocrat robbed of empire fighting the forces that stole it; "no paper, no graph, no MSNBC segment can move this belief" because "if it was to change, the entire sense of self would fall to pieces" (ep03:140).
Citations: ep03:90 (hero narrative definition), ep03:222 (keystone vs brick), ep03:140 (Monckton).

### Q8 [mechanism]
**Mackay answers "So they can eat watermelons" when asked why the T-Rex has huge teeth in a perfect Eden. Why does this answer prove keystone rather than ignorance?**

A: The answer's job is to protect the arch, not to track truth: any answer that preserves Eden counts as success. Mackay had faced "a long list of basic scientific facts and simple logic questions" with no movement; he debates scientists and wins crowds, so stupidity is ruled out (ep03:38). Ignorance is a missing brick that facts fill; a keystone holds the arch and facts threaten the arch, so you diagnose before you prescribe (ep03:222). The watermelon-teeth answer is story maintenance wearing the shape of an answer.
Citations: ep03:38 (impervious to facts, not stupid), ep03:222 (diagnose before prescribing).

### Q9 [applied]
**You feel heat level 8 out of 10 on a topic and feel the urge to post a correction online. State Storr's protocol and explain why the urge itself is the signal.**

A: Check the heat before you check the facts. Storr's self-application: thinking about Gaza makes him want to shout and cry, so his policy is to stay silent about it in public, "because a spiked needle means the brain cannot report the truth" (ep03:63). McRaney's version of the same tell: write the angry reply, read it, delete it (ep03:182). The urge to broadcast your view to the world is itself the warning signal that the sacred-emotion detector has tripped: irrationality lives where emotion lives, and a heat spike means the brain is in no state to report the truth (ep03:63).
Citations: ep03:63 (Storr's policy), ep03:182 (McRaney's delete rule).

## ep04 — Arguing

### Q10 [numerical]
**A second coin flip pays $200 half the time and loses $100 half the time. Compute its expected value and the forfeiture from refusing it when the first outcome is unknown. Why does the machinery refuse?**

A: Expected value = (0.5 x 200) - (0.5 x 100) = 100 - 50 = plus $50 (ep04:105). Refusing the flip when the outcome is unknown forfeits $50 of expected value. The machinery refuses because it needs one justifiable story: the winner's reason (streak, can spare it) and the loser's reason (odds favor recovery) point in different directions and "cannot merge into one good reason." No single justifiable reason, no flip (ep04:105). Reason optimizes for justifiability, not quality.
Citation: ep04:105 (full worked arithmetic and the mechanism).

### Q11 [mechanism]
**Why does telling someone "the answer is 5 cents" fail to convert them on the bat-and-ball problem, while 5 to 10 minutes of argument succeeds?**

A: Telling supplies the conclusion with none of the listener's production or evaluation work, and "saying 'the answer is 5 cents'... changes nothing" (ep04:136). Argument works because the joint process runs: the arguer produces the steps and the listener evaluates them; "the arguer's production and the group's evaluation do jointly what neither does alone" (ep04:136). Mercier's result: with one exception, everyone converts, sometimes after 5 to 10 minutes of argument (ep04:134). This is the argumentative theory in miniature: reason is a lawyer plus a judge, and telling only hires the lawyer for the other side.
Citations: ep04:134 (conversion result), ep04:136 (telling fails, joint process).

### Q12 [explain like I am new]
**Explain the argumentative theory like I am new. What did reason evolve for, and what goes wrong solo?**

A: In the 2011 Mercier and Sperber account, humans evolved reason to do two things: produce arguments and evaluate arguments, in order to overcome trust bottlenecks — convincing others without force, coordinating without shouting (ep04:34). Solo is the off-label use: alone you have no sounding board, so you collect reasons for your side and stop, growing "more polarized and more confident" while beliefs stay the same or get worse (ep04:44). Put the same machinery in a group and truth wins, because one side produces and the other evaluates.
Citations: ep04:34 (the claim), ep04:44 (solo failure mechanism).

## ep05 — Bayes' Theorem

### Q13 [numerical]
**A screening test is 90 percent accurate: it catches 90 percent of true cancers and clears 90 percent of healthy patients. The disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. A patient tests positive. Recompute the probability she has cancer, showing the two-group count.**

A: Work it on 1,000 patients. One patient has cancer; the test catches her: 1 true positive. Of the 999 healthy patients, the test wrongly flags 10 percent: about 100 false positives. Total positives: about 101. True cases among them: 1 out of 101, under 1 percent (ep05:108). Exact form: (0.9 x 0.001) / (0.9 x 0.001 + 0.1 x 0.999) = 0.0009 / 0.1008, about 0.0089, roughly 0.9 percent (ep05:110). The lazy answer (90 percent) ignores the prior; even doctors who remember the base rate exists guess 70 or 80 percent, because the vivid new evidence crowds it out (ep05:110).
Citations: ep05:108 (count), ep05:110 (exact arithmetic + doctor error).

### Q14 [mechanism]
**Why does the fact that "the brain is not a Bayes net" doom the Jenga strategy in argument?**

A: In a Bayes net, changing one node reprices every related node instantly. The human brain skips propagation because full repricing would be "computationally ruinous" — every glance would force thousands of belief updates (ep05:158). So beliefs that look logically connected are stored independently, and catching one contradiction does not cascade: "there is no such block, because the tower is not one tower" (ep05:160). Galef's replacement for the Jenga strategy is the source-check: when a belief matters, trace it to its source manually; if the source is defunct, downgrade the belief (ep05:173).
Citations: ep05:158 (non-propagation), ep05:160 (no Jenga block), ep05:173 (source-check).

### Q15 [applied]
**Your deploy fails right after your change ships. Apply the two-worlds test instead of building the exonerating story.**

A: The Bayesian definition of evidence compares how likely an observation is in two worlds: the world where the hypothesis is true and the world where it is false (ep05:77). World one: your change caused it. World two: your change did not. How likely is a post-deploy failure in each? Like the car crash: even with a true exonerating story, bad drivers crash more often on average, so the crash is evidence against your driving and confidence should downgrade "at least a little" — the story explains this crash but does not erase the base pattern (ep05:85). The discipline is Galef's Bob-and-Amy correction: do not ask only "does this fit my hypothesis"; ask "does it also fit the alternative" (ep05:77).
Citations: ep05:77 (two-worlds definition), ep05:85 (car crash application).

## ep06 — The Search Effect

### Q16 [explain like I am new]
**Explain the search effect like I am new, and why "the internet makes us stupid" is the wrong headline.**

A: The internet became your transactive memory partner: fast, accurate, always available, asking nothing back. The boundary between its knowledge and yours blurred, and "you erroneously include knowledge stored outside of your own head as your own" (ep06:71). The wrong headline claims lost knowledge; the real claim is misattribution plus overconfidence (ep06:75). The knowledge is real and the access is real; the error is in the accounting: the feeling of ownership moved without the knowledge moving (ep06:156).
Citations: ep06:71 (the sentence), ep06:75 (misattribution, not stupidity), ep06:156 (the accounting error).

### Q17 [mechanism]
**The experiments use two decisive controls: identical information for both groups, and a filtered search with useless results. What does each prove, and what would collapse without the first?**

A: Identical information: the search group finds answers online while the no-internet group is handed the same answers from the same URLs, so "both groups see the exact same information" and "the only difference is the act of searching" (ep06:85). Without it, the inflation could be explained by searchers simply learning more; the control leaves only the act (ep06:222). Filtered search: participants sent to a last-week-only Google search found nothing helpful, "and still rated themselves higher afterward" (ep06:108), proving the mechanism is the act of searching — sitting inside the search environment — not the learning.
Citations: ep06:85 (identical-information design), ep06:108 (useless-results result), ep06:222 (why the control is necessary).

### Q18 [applied]
**You claim to understand your team's payments service. Run the closed-laptop protocol from the chapter.**

A: Write what you can explain with the laptop closed (the owned list), then write what you needed docs for (the borrowed list); the second list is the partner's (ep06:190). Corrections: "I can look it up" is not "I know it" — interviewers test the closed-laptop list. In design docs, mark every claim owned or borrowed and weight borrowed claims as hypotheses until someone owns them (ep06:190). Keep the partner; fix the books.
Citation: ep06:190 (borrowed list, interview correction, design-doc rule).

## ep07 — Deep Canvassing

### Q19 [numerical]
**A team knocks on 1,000 doors; half open; 70 percent of opened doors finish; the measured conversion is about 10 per 100 conversations. How many conversations complete, how many minds change, and how many knocks are needed for 1,000 converts? Also: what does an effect of 0.25 standard deviations mean for the median voter?**

A: 1,000 knocks x 0.5 open = 500 doors opened. 500 x 0.70 finish = 350 conversations (ep07:120). 350 x 0.10 = about 35 minds change (ep07:62). For 1,000 converts: 1,000 / 35 x 1,000 = about 28,600 knocks. A 0.25-standard-deviation shift moves the median person from the 50th percentile to about the 60th percentile on the tolerance scale (ep07:64). Duration: at least 3 months, surviving opposition ads (ep07:147).
Citations: ep07:120 (70 percent finish), ep07:62 (10 per 100), ep07:64 (percentile meaning), ep07:147 (duration).

### Q20 [mechanism]
**Why do talking points fail at the door while questions succeed? Give the System 1 versus System 2 account.**

A: Talking points are impersonal and unpersuasive; facts and figures "bounce off the gut, which trusts feelings over data and rejects the rest 'like an unhealthy virus' to protect internal consistency" (ep07:90). Standard campaigns speak System 1 — fast, passive ads and mailers — and their effects last about a day. Deep canvassing forces System 2: slow, effortful, the voter retrieving memories and constructing answers. Effortful processing predicts durable attitude change; "the mental work is the active ingredient" (ep07:157). The trainers' first lesson states the rule: "There is nothing you can say, there is nothing you can tell this person that's going to change their mind" (ep07:90).
Citations: ep07:90 (virus rejection + nothing-you-say rule), ep07:157 (System 2 account).

### Q21 [applied]
**A senior engineer resists your migration proposal. Design the deep-canvassing-style conversation, including the honest limit.**

A: Do not open with benchmarks or docs; those are talking points. Open with radical hospitality and genuine curiosity: "resist the urge to trust your superior knowledge. Do not deliver conclusions. Instead, uncover what in the voter's own life could lead to a shared value. Ask, listen, mirror" (ep07:126). Ask about lived experience: the outage that scarred them, the migration that burned them. Pair any fact you bring with something from their history. Frame it as cooperative mystery-solving with no pressure to agree. Honest limit: the measured base rate is 10 per 100, not 100 per 100 — most doors do not flip, so budget for the base rate (ep07:62).
Citations: ep07:126 (field rules), ep07:62 (base rate).

## ep08 — QAnon and Conspiratorial Narratives

### Q22 [numerical]
**State the measured reality of QAnon against the three media myths, with the chapter's numbers. Then compute the JFK-inclination share.**

A: Not growing: feeling-thermometer polling in Florida scored QAnon at 24 out of 100 in 2018, two points above Fidel Castro at 22, with no national increase since (ep08:122). Not far-right: partisanship and ideology drop out of the models; the surviving predictors are conspiratorial thinking, dark-triad traits, and institutional antipathy (ep08:196). Not big: 5 to 7 percent believers at most; rallies of 40 to 50; the hashtag matched #MeToo in volume "while changing nothing in society" (ep08:122, ep08:126). Harm, comparatively: 22 violent incidents tied to QAnon believers in three years versus 139 far-right non-QAnon acts in the same window (ep08:141). JFK arithmetic: about 45 percent endorse a Kennedy-assassination conspiracy, but half cannot name the conspirator, so about 22.5 percent hold a free-floating preference for conspiracy-shaped explanations with no narrative attached (ep08:151).
Citations: ep08:122 (thermometer, 5-7 percent), ep08:126 (rallies, hashtag), ep08:141 (harm numbers), ep08:151 (45 percent split), ep08:196 (myth corrections).

### Q23 [mechanism]
**Why does debunking a conspiracy narrative often feed it instead of weakening it?**

A: Two mechanisms. The logic loop: counterevidence gets reinterpreted as part of the cover-up and missing evidence as proof of how deep the cover-up goes, so "every input becomes support" and no possible input can weaken it (ep08:99). The channel problem: in a distrust community "the debunker is read as part of the conspiracy," so the correction arrives through a distrusted channel and the loop absorbs it as more cover-up (ep08:192). Debunking treats the claims; the believer's motive was never the claims.
Citations: ep08:99 (logic loop), ep08:192 (distrusted channel).

### Q24 [explain like I am new]
**Explain the hourglass model like I am new.**

A: Sternisko's model pictures the process as an hourglass (ep08:66). The fat top holds everything a person brings: attitudes, beliefs, values, anxieties, the whole psychological profile. The bottleneck is one conspiracy theory. The fat bottom is what comes out the other side: new beliefs, attitudes, and values absorbed from inside the conspiracy, which become markers of the new group identity. The fandom analogy: enter through spaceship movies or through horror, land on Alien either way, and pick up the shared costumes and lore. Many doors in, one identity out.
Citation: ep08:66 (hourglass stages).

## ep09 — Tribal Psychology

### Q25 [numerical]
**In Tajfel's allocation task, subjects chose $4 for their group and $3 for the other over $5 each. Compute each side's sacrifice and the total welfare cost, and state what the choice proves.**

A: Your group: $5 to $4, sacrifice $1. The other group: $5 to $3, sacrifice $2. Total welfare: $10 to $7, a net cost of $3. The gap moves from $0 to $1 in your favor. Subjects "gave up a dollar for their own group to take two dollars from the other group"; "maximum ingroup advantage beat maximum total welfare" (ep09:40). It proves relative standing beats absolute welfare, from people sorted by a coin toss minutes earlier allocating money they would never receive. The minimal group paradigm: humans form groups over anything, then immediately favor us and punish them (ep09:40).
Citation: ep09:40 (choice, sacrifices, paradigm).

### Q26 [mechanism]
**Why does better math skill produce WORSE interpretation of gun-control numbers when the numbers oppose the subject's party?**

A: On skin-cream numbers, better math meant better interpretation for everyone; on gun-control numbers, better math meant worse interpretation, but only when the numbers opposed the subject's party (ep09:100). The mechanism: on identity-fused issues, "the individual interest in conforming to the group's view dominates by far the person's interest in getting the right answer" (ep09:102). The cost-benefit: your climate belief changes nothing about the climate, but getting it wrong in front of your tribe can cost you your standing, your job, your relationships — "the incentives point at belonging. The math follows" (ep09:102). Skill does not protect against bending; it improves the bending.
Citations: ep09:100 (study result), ep09:102 (conformity mechanism).

### Q27 [applied]
**Your company is rolling out a controversial new on-call policy. Apply the HPV-versus-HBV lesson.**

A: Two similar vaccines, two fates: HBV was introduced by doctors, reached 95 percent acceptance, and never entered politics; HPV was introduced via congressional debate and cable-news moral combat and became a tribal badge. "Same medicine type, opposite fates. The difference was the track it traveled into public awareness" (ep09:141). Kahan's rule: depoliticize evidence-based issues before they go public, because any issue can be polluted (ep09:155). Application: route the policy through a trusted member of the skeptical camp first so their fingerprints are on it; share only sources the other camp trusts; frame the goal as fixing the on-call environment, not converting the skeptics.
Citations: ep09:141 (two fates), ep09:155 (depoliticization rule).

## ep10 — The Self

### Q28 [explain like I am new]
**What does Hood mean by calling the self an "illusion," and what does he NOT mean?**

A: He means "an experience that is not what it seems": the experience of having a self is real, but what it seems to be is not (ep10:36). The seeming has components — you inhabit your body, you are coherent across time, you are an individual, you have free will — and science shows each is illusory to some degree. He does NOT mean the self does not exist. The visual analogy: the brain registers a nonexistent square so strongly that neurons fire for it; illusions are perceptions with a loose relationship to what is there (ep10:36).
Citation: ep10:36 (definition and the square analogy).

### Q29 [mechanism]
**What does the Libet half-second prove, what does Hood explicitly say it does NOT prove, and what breaks?**

A: It proves the conscious experience of willing an action arrives about half a second after the brain has already prepared the movement: "the conscious self does not initiate. It arrives late and claims authorship" (ep10:42). It does NOT prove that no action is ever consciously initiated: "Hood is careful: not every action works this way" (ep10:44). What breaks is the universal naive model — decide, then act, always, in that order. Practical takeaway: treat "I decided" as a press release and audit decisions by outcomes, not by the vividness of the choosing (ep10:163).
Citations: ep10:42 (the finding), ep10:44 (the limit), ep10:163 (what breaks).

### Q30 [mechanism]
**Episode 10 says every instrument from episodes 1 through 9 "is held by the narrator." If the debugger is constructed, why is Hood's advice "operate the instrument" rather than "discard it"?**

A: Because the illusion "is real in the brain" and does real work: the I tracks outcomes of hidden processes, the me runs the social game (ep10:9 summary). "Hood does not offer an exit from the construction. He offers a different relationship to it... It demotes the narrator from author to instrument. You still use the self. You stop obeying it blindly" (ep10:148). Discarding is not an option; the track's final skill is to hold the debugger lightly, via three operating rules: distrust authorship feelings (audit by outcomes), expect the reflection (treat audience-shifts as instrument data), and maintain the story deliberately (ep10:193).
Citations: ep10:146 (debugger held by narrator), ep10:148 (no exit, author to instrument), ep10:193 (three rules).
